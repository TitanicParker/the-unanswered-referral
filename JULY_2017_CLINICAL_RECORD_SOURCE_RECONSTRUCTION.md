# The July 2017 Clinical Record

## A source-by-source reconstruction of the patient's account

*Documentary appendix for **The Unanswered Referral** · Working contribution · 19 November 2026*

> **Scope.** This appendix compares four documentary stages: (1) the GP's referral of 29 June 2017; (2) the hospital's compressed Rx/reason for referral; (3) the neurologist's handwritten interview of 3 July 2017; and (4) the typed discharge history and clinical Impression. Its purpose is to identify what each source actually records and how wording, attribution and clinical meaning change between sources. It does not treat the typed history as a verbatim transcript of the handwriting.
>
> **Transcription discipline.** Quotations are differentiated from paraphrases. Punctuation, parentheses and distinctions between *foot pain*, *leg pain*, and *leg tension* matter. The handwritten readings reproduced here are working transcriptions from supplied page images; where the handwriting is uncertain, the original image controls. The repository's compiled HTML source includes editorial material and must not be mistaken for a native hospital record.

## 1. The GP referral: one developing presentation

The original GP referral documents an abnormal gait, bilateral foot problems, muscle tension, toe clawing, increasing tone and stiffness, deterioration in dexterity, and interference with work. The patient had attributed his gait to corns and calluses; the GP subsequently became concerned about the developing neurological signs.

Among the reported difficulties was an inability to relax the muscles, especially in bed, with toe clawing and discomfort. The referral raised a possible parkinsonian or other underlying diagnosis and sought urgent specialist assessment.

**Documentary finding:** The referral does not establish that the foot symptoms and movement disorder had the same cause. It does establish that the GP transmitted them together as features of the presentation requiring assessment.

## 2. The hospital Rx: a materially compressed question

The hospital's abbreviated reason for referral retains difficulty initiating movement, stiffness and increased tone in all four limbs, and wrist cogwheeling. It does not reproduce the GP's account of bilateral foot problems, corns, calluses, toe clawing or difficulty relaxing in bed. It also omits much of the developing chronology and functional consequences.

A particularly clear change in qualification is:

| GP referral | Hospital Rx | Transformation |
|---|---|---|
| “?Some cogwheeling at the wrist” | “COGWHEELING AT THE WRIST” | Removes the question mark and “Some” |

**Documentary finding:** This is not merely shorter wording. The compressed statement carries forward the parkinsonian findings while excluding the explicit foot-related part of the referred presentation and the history connecting the two.

## 3. The handwritten interview: recovery of the missing history

The handwritten notes are ordered here by the **actual interview sequence** supplied by the patient, not by ascending hospital page number:

**107 → 108 → 109 → 100 → 101 → 102 → 103 → 104.**

### 3.1. Bodily tension and posturing

Page 108 records the patient's retrospective account of bodily tension, beginning around 2004, including difficulty feeling relaxed at bedtime without tension and adopting postures in which tension could be felt. This restores a history akin to the difficulty relaxing documented by the GP but not reproduced in the hospital Rx.

### 3.2. The next issue: corns, calluses and fallen arches

Page 109 records corns, calluses and fallen arches, followed by a treatment history **since 2009–2010**. The treatment dates are not stated onset dates for the foot problems.

The patient then describes leaving bar work in 2012, after which foot pain became less prominent. The working handwritten reading records:

> Began to be aware that foot pain and leg tension weren't the same

The page also contains a **parenthetical addition at the bottom**:

> (as the leg pain settled)

The parenthesis refers to **leg pain**, whereas the preceding contrast is between **foot pain** and **leg tension**. That difference is retained; it is not silently corrected or reconciled.

**Provenance clarification (25 November 2026).** The patient alleges that the separate parenthesis was inserted after the 10 July protest and denies reporting leg pain in this history. Its wording combines the anatomical location from “leg tension” with the sensation from “foot pain”; it subsequently becomes the explanatory bridge in the typed account. The manuscript is therefore itself an object of provenance examination. Neither the interview date nor the final authorisation date establishes when this individual inscription was made. This is relevant to the central allegation of [the substitution of documentary defensibility for genuine medicine](index.html#gravity-statement): the patient says the construction responded to his demand for clinical work. The original quotations remain unchanged.

### 3.3. Progression and alcohol cessation

Pages 100–103 describe continuing difficulty with bodily tension, leg control, dexterity, occupational tasks and attention. Page 101 records the start of a materials-handling role in January 2017 and subsequent difficulties.

On page 102, the account of reducing alcohol and stopping two weeks previously is followed by the standalone entry:

> No improvement.

This statement must be read with the immediately preceding alcohol history. It is not an unqualified assertion about the response of every symptom to every earlier change in circumstances.

**Documentary finding:** The neurologist's handwritten interview recovered a substantial portion of the earlier foot and tension history that the hospital's compressed referral had omitted.

## 4. Typed history: expansion of a patient-reported distinction

The typed discharge history substantially reproduces the handwritten account, but with consequential changes of grammatical structure and attribution.

| Handwritten page 109 | Typed discharge summary, page 1 |
|---|---|
| “Began to be aware that foot pain and leg tension weren't the same” | “Therefore in 2012 he began to realise that the foot pain and the leg tension were not the same problem because: as the leg pain settled --- he was more aware of the tension” |
| “(as the leg pain settled)” — a separate parenthetical addition | Incorporated into the typed explanatory sentence, without its original parenthetical presentation |

The key additions are **“Therefore”**, **“because”** and **“he was more aware of the tension.”** The typed sentence makes the recognition explicitly causal and gives it a fuller explanation than the corresponding handwritten entry.

**Documentary finding:** A brief record of emerging awareness is transformed into a more developed explanation attributed to the patient. The fact that a distinction already appears in the handwriting must be preserved: the typed account did not invent the existence of a distinction. It did change its presentation and explanatory force.

## 5. The improvement statements must be kept separate

| Source | Wording | Immediate context |
|---|---|---|
| **H102** | “No improvement.” | Reduction and cessation of alcohol |
| **T2** | “There was no improvement off alcohol.” | Typed history of cessation |
| **I4** | “Not clear as this has improved.” | Discussion of possible separate leg symptoms or secondary peripheral neuropathy, potentially associated with earlier alcohol intake |

The typed history **preserves the absence of improvement after stopping alcohol**. The Impression later introduces improvement alongside a possible alcohol-related cause. The referent of **“this”** is not clearly specified in the Impression passage, nor is its timing established there.

**Documentary finding:** The record supplies no clear symptom-specific or time-specific bridge between the reported *no improvement off alcohol* and the Impression's *this has improved*. The proximity of the improvement statement to the alcohol-related neuropathy suggestion is therefore a substantive point for documentary examination, not an explanation that can be supplied by silently merging the two statements.

## 6. The Impression: hypothesis, attribution and relative significance

### 6.1. Possible peripheral neuropathy

The Impression states:

> The leg symptoms including corns and callouses and burning may have been completely separate or perhaps a secondary peripheral neuropathy perhaps in somewhat respect related to the significant original alcohol intake? Not clear as this has improved.

The description here includes **burning**, whereas handwritten page 109's account of the next foot issue specifies **corns, calluses and fallen arches**. The Impression proposes possible separateness or secondary peripheral neuropathy; in the quoted passage, it does not establish that diagnosis.

### 6.2. The patient-attributed separation

The next paragraph states:

> However he has in his own mind - markedly divorced the feelings of tension in the legs which was/is the predominant problem - from the "corns and callouses" issues and pains in the feet---while corns and callouses remain a major problem, in reality they are much less of an issue since he is on his feet much less in the last 3-4 years.

The progression across the documentary stages is clear:

| Handwritten interview | Typed history | Clinical Impression |
|---|---|---|
| “Began to be aware” | “Therefore in 2012 he began to realise” | “he has in his own mind - markedly divorced” |

“Began to be aware” describes an emerging distinction between experiences. “Markedly divorced” asserts a much stronger separation, explicitly located in the patient's own mind.

The enhanced patient attribution appears **beside the clinician's own possible separate/peripheral explanation**. These are not two independent sources of evidence: the stronger attribution is clinician-authored text derived from the same encounter and its documentation.

### 6.3. “In reality” and the changing importance of the feet

The Impression acknowledges that corns and calluses remain a major problem, then says they are **“in reality”** much less of an issue because the patient is on his feet much less over the preceding three to four years.

The handwritten history associates less prominent foot pain with leaving bar work in 2012; it also documents subsequent worsening of tension and dexterity and a new materials-handling role in January 2017. The Impression does not expressly reconcile its longer-term account of reduced standing with the recent reported deterioration and occupational demands.

**Documentary finding:** The Impression moves from reporting the patient's symptom history to asserting the clinician's assessment of the relative significance of the foot problems, while attributing the emphatic separation itself to the patient.

## 8. The documentary mechanism

Two processes occur in succession.

**Information transmission:** the GP refers the abnormal feet alongside the movement symptoms; the hospital précis omits the explicit foot history; the neurologist's handwritten interview recovers it.

**Construction of meaning:** the handwritten record contains a limited experiential distinction between foot pain and leg tension; the typed history expands that distinction into an explanatory narrative; the Impression makes it emphatic and attributes it to the patient's own mind beside a possible separate/peripheral cause.

The distinction between the authority **to interpret symptoms** and the authority **to attribute that interpretation to the patient** is decisive. A clinician's interpretation does not gain independent corroboration merely by being written as the patient's own understanding.

## 9. Conclusion

The July 2017 documents show a material transformation in the recorded account of bodily tension and painful abnormal feet. The original GP referral combined those complaints within a developing presentation. The hospital's compressed referral omitted much of the foot history. The handwritten neurological interview recovered it. The typed history then expanded a tentative distinction into a fuller causal explanation, and the Impression strengthened it into a patient-attributed separation alongside a possible peripheral-neuropathy explanation.

The Impression also introduced an ambiguous assertion of improvement despite the same typed document recording no improvement after alcohol cessation.

**The central documentary finding is that a clinician-authored interpretation acquired the appearance of support from the patient's own understanding, although the corresponding handwritten words are materially narrower.** The original foot–movement relationship remains the clinical question against which that representation should be assessed.
