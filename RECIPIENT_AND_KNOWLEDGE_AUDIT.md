# Recipient-and-Knowledge Audit — The Unanswered Referral

*Evidence-led counsel working audit | 18 November 2026 | Version 1.0*

**Purpose.** This audit follows the movement of material information between the GP, hospital neurologist, podiatry, Parkinson's/movement-disorder nurse, patient, complaint reviewers, hospital data/records officers and University authorities. It distinguishes **acquisition, recording, transmission, receipt, use and responsibility**. It supplements, without replacing, [the orientation essay](THE_UNANSWERED_REFERRAL.md), [six decisive propositions](SIX_DECISIVE_PROPOSITIONS_HOSPITAL_ANSWER.md), [25 understandings](THE_25_ESSENTIAL_UNDERSTANDINGS.md), [56 matters](FIFTY_SIX_MATTERS_FOR_EXAMINATION.md), and the [40-document source index](DOCUMENTARY_SOURCE_INDEX.md).

## Executive finding

The original June 2017 GP referral transmitted an **integrated presentation**: painful, physically abnormal feet; toe clawing; a markedly abnormal gait; increased limb tone; and progressive loss of movement and function. The hospital's opening referral précis substantially shortened that presentation, omitting its explicit foot-pain and clawing detail, though the receiving neurologist separately elicited the foot history and observed a dystonic-looking gait. The admission produced a substantial Parkinsonism investigation and treatment plan. It did not, in the identified completed documentation, show a reasoned answer to how abnormal neurological posture and tone contributed to painful foot loading, nor identify an accountable owner and return route for resolving that uncertainty. That is a question of **clinical allocation**, not proof that a definitive diagnosis was medically available.

The neurologist both **acquired** the history and **authored** its authoritative synthesis. The handwritten interview recorded that foot pain and leg tension became easier to distinguish when pain reduced after less standing. The final impression proposed complete separation or peripheral neuropathy as possibilities and stated that the patient had **“in his own mind - markedly divorced”** the problems. The latter is a strengthened *clinician-authored attribution*; no independent patient endorsement of different medical causes has been identified. The patient also reports a direct pre-discharge protest on 10 July 2017, with four trainees present. If corroborated, the protest was knowledge available to the consultant before discharge; it was not preserved in the completed account. The account's later authorisation is established, but its first-draft history is not.

The downstream consequences cannot be reduced to an unquestioning GP. The GP expressly associated Parkinson's disease, abnormal tone, gait and clawed painful feet in a 2019 podiatry re-referral. In March 2023 he prescribed pregabalin, urgently requested specialist assistance over severe pain and reported a previous response of pain to better Parkinsonism control. The movement nurse demonstrably relayed the GP's referral and severe symptoms to the neurologist. The specialist pathway made treatment adjustments and examined other possibilities, but a completed foot–movement/pressure explanation and assigned responsibility are not identified. By 2025 the GP explicitly said he would not adjust levodopa independently without specialist guidance.

The 2025 complaint challenged diagnostic anchoring and failure to reassess. The neurologist's reply substantially defended the timing and risks of dopaminergic treatment. The hospital then reported a PAR/SIMT conclusion of **no deficit**, principally explaining it in those treatment terms. **Institutional receipt** of the particularised July 2025 teaching/protest access request and an August records response is documented before the SIMT meeting. It is **not established** whether the individual PAR authors or SIMT members examined that allegation, the original referral, or the handwritten-to-typed transformation: the full PAR, its evidence list and meeting record are not in the assembled collection.

**Central conclusion:** The strongest demonstrated risk is not simple transmission failure or proof of deliberate falsification. It is the possibility that a partially unresolved specialist **disposition** acquired durable authority while the original question's **clinical owner** was never clearly specified, and that later reviewers tested treatment within that disposition more visibly than they tested its source and adequacy. The earliest and most important way to resolve the case is to obtain the original allocation/handover records and the full PAR source inventory.

## Scope, method and evidential controls

**Time covered:** April 2017 through 2026, with emphasis on 2017, 2018, 2019, 2023 and 2025–26. The 40 local numbered documents are a **compiled, redacted presentation**, with transcriptions and editorial apparatus, not 40 individually authenticated native clinical files. Refer to the originals when the precise reading, date or authorship is disputed. The three local [manuscript photos](document_05_manuscript.jpg), [page 2](document_06_manuscript.jpg) and [page 3](document_07_manuscript.jpg) should be compared with the transcription; the [signed supplementary statement](Article_16_Supplementary_Statement_July_2017.pdf) is later firsthand testimony.

**Evidence tags:** **D** documentary statement/clinical entry; **R** receipt independently recorded or confirmed; **T** patient's firsthand retrospective testimony; **C** contemporaneous corroboration in a different source; **I** reasoned inference; **U** recipient, search, content or timing unproved; **N** source not contained in the local 40-document compilation. The same clinical interview appearing in two versions is **one informational origin**, not two independent confirmations. A GP's own examination is an independent source. An email proves what was sent to named recipients, not necessarily that every recipient read and adopted it. Absence from the **reviewed** record does not prove no conversation or assessment existed. A patient's request that an entity search records does not prove the entity held those records or acted as their data controller.

**Local citations:** Links in the form [Document 2](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-2) use the **original 1–40 numbering**. Records outside that compilation are identified by dated email, source filename and where available the separate canonical transcription's CLIN identifiers, solely as interim provenance; they should be copied as a *small redacted exhibit set*, not by importing an unrelated repository.

## The 2017 information chain: original signal, receiving précis and clinical synthesis

### What the GP sent and what the opening précis preserved

[Document 1](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-1) records the GP's April foot examination (bilateral pain, mallet toes, calluses/pressure areas, abnormal gait), the June observation of initiation difficulty and interest in Neurology's opinion about “gait, feet and ‘muscle tension’,” and emerging increased tone. The 29 June [full urgent referral](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-2) states that the patient attended with bilateral foot issues and blamed his gait on calluses/corns, describes toe clawing in bed and difficulty relaxing, and notes deterioration at work. The doctor asks for urgent medical assessment of possible Parkinsonian **or other** disease. This was an evolving joined-up inquiry, not a referral for the DaTscan diagnosis alone.

The local collection includes an explicit side-by-side comparison: the opening hospital précis in [Document 4](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-4) records a 39-year-old with difficulty initiating movement, increased tone and stiffness in all four limbs and wrist cogwheeling, **without reproducing** the GP's bilateral foot pain, corns/calluses or painful toe clawing. This establishes **textual compression**, not that the receiving team lacked access to the original. The full letter may have remained in the admission file. The neurology complex-case meeting is placed at **approximately 4 July 2017** in the earlier case chronology ([Master Case for Counsel](https://github.com/TitanicParker/prima-facie/blob/main/MASTER_CASE_FOR_COUNSEL.md)), about a day after the 3 July handwritten interview. Its exact date requires the native meeting record. The completed account does not identify which original referral pages were supplied to participants. Its source pack and the person who composed the précis therefore remain outstanding.

This is the first information fork. A clinician reading only the précis would encounter a substantially narrower problem than one reading the full referral. But the consultant's subsequent detailed history shows that he personally learned about the feet through another route. Compression cannot explain away his later knowledge.

### What the neurologist independently learned, did and recorded

The three handwritten pages of 3 July in [Documents 5–7](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-5) preserve a patient history of years of abnormal tension, a 2014 hospital presentation for limb tension, podiatric problems since about 2009–10 and foot pain becoming less prominent after leaving work requiring prolonged standing. On that last point the manuscript already records that foot pain and leg tension “weren't the same” as pain settled. This **must not** be described as a phrase newly invented in the typed version. The epistemic difference is between **distinguishable experiences** and **independent underlying causes**.

The full hospital account in [Document 4](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-4) contains extensive Parkinsonian findings, pes planus/hammertoes and a striking gait that looked somewhat like foot drop but suggested a dystonic posture. The neurologist treated leg tension with nighttime clonazepam and planned procyclidine and rasagiline; an electronic discharge summary reports podiatry referral. These are substantive acts of clinical recognition. The record does **not** establish exact first administration of clonazepam, uninterrupted nightly use for years, or an anatomical mechanism for the recurrent lesions.

The final clinical impression proposes that the corns/calluses/burning **“may have been completely separate or perhaps a secondary peripheral neuropathy”**, adding **“Not clear”**. Immediately afterward it says that the patient **“in his own mind - markedly divorced”** the tension from painful feet. The two statements occupy different evidential categories: the former is a clinician's expressly qualified hypothesis; the latter is a clinician-authored claim about the patient's understanding. In the surviving handwritten excerpt there is no explicit endorsement by the patient of medically separate causes. The final wording could make an unsettled distinction seem patient-supported without actually demonstrating its causal basis.

The plan identifies clinical actions and movement-disorder follow-up (including nurse contact). It also requests GP help with community physiotherapy/occupational therapy and chiropody/podiatry; the SHO had tried to arrange podiatry. What it does not expressly identify, in the reviewed text, is a named person or test charged with resolving **whether dystonic or Parkinsonian movement contributed to toe clawing, loading, recurring corns and pain**. Podiatric treatment of skin/pressure pathology and neurological responsibility for its potential motor cause are not interchangeable. The possibility that standard reasonable care did not require further testing remains open for independent clinical opinion.

### The reported 10 July pre-discharge protest: a conditional knowledge event

The patient states that shortly before discharge he asked four medical students to summon the consultant to challenge the fact that his painful feet had not been properly investigated; his account recalls the “full NCT / bald tyres” analogy and a response directing attention toward podiatry. His latest signed written account is available [locally](Article_16_Supplementary_Statement_July_2017.pdf), associated with [Document 38](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-38). It is **direct retrospective testimony**, not independent witness corroboration.

The *documentary genealogy* precedes September 2026. The earliest surviving later statement located in the connected Gmail material was attached to a **6 June 2025 Patient Advocacy Service email**, then a **7 June 2025 self-addressed reconstruction**. That private reconstruction placed the exchange on Tuesday **11 July**, whereas the later detailed account places it on Monday **10 July**; the date discrepancy should be resolved, not erased. On **7 July 2025**, the patient wrote to the GUH DPO (copying hospital educational/governance addresses) with a detailed description of four trainees, a summons, the NCT analogy and a paraphrased consultant response; he asked for student, teaching, ward and supervisory records. GUH replied in a **5 August 2025** access decision that it did not hold records in several requested categories; its letter also referred to supplying clinical notes for **10–11 July 2015**, despite the request concerning **2017**. The wrong-year text may be clerical; the actual search terms and enclosures require verification. Neither non-holding nor absence from the discharge summary disproves the encounter.

**If the event occurred as recalled**, the neurologist personally received a direct challenge to investigation sufficiency while discharge remained a live decision. A final account that attributes strong *mental separation* to the patient but omits his actual objection would then produce an especially consequential selective account. The record was discharged 11 July, had DaTscan/addendum transcription finalisation dated **13 July at 21:41**, and confirmation of the discharge summary dated **17 July at 19:30**. Those dates establish post-encounter **authorisation**, **not** first composition or post-protest rewriting of the contested words. Draft/version metadata, ward notes and witnesses are decisive.

A further knowledge boundary matters: the **19 March** and **18 April 2025** complaints did not yet specifically raise this encounter. The consultant's March rebuttal must not be criticised for failing to respond to an allegation he had not then been sent as a complaint. The hospital institution, however, received and answered the detailed July request **before** the August SIMT meeting. Whether PAR/SIMT individuals knew of it is a separate, unproved question.

## The downstream clinical chain: who could act upon what?

### Hospital to GP: known diagnosis, unproved reading of every line

[Document 4](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-4) contains both a shorter electronic discharge summary and a longer consultant history/plan. Do not confuse their dates, contents or delivery routes. In the [GP record](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-1), the GP noted on **14 July 2017** that he was waiting for formal Neurology correspondence; on **17 July**, after discharge, he again recorded that no formal discharge had yet arrived. On **18 July** he entered **“Extensive correspondence Dr Thomas Monaghan - Diagnosis early Parkinsonism. Further testing awaited but positive DaTscan and clinical features - see scanning”** and added Parkinson's disease as an active diagnosis. This is unusually good evidence of the precise **transition into the GP information system**. It proves incorporation of specialist diagnostic information; it does not prove which full pages were scanned, when each was delivered, or that the GP read or endorsed the “markedly divorced” wording.

Neither the reviewed discharge summary nor the longer account records the reported 10 July challenge. No independent evidence presently identifies a separate contemporaneous notice of that objection to the GP. A GP may have been able to infer foot issues persisted, but the completed account did not tell him **that the patient had expressly contested the sufficiency of the hospital investigation**, if the alleged discussion occurred.

The 2019 referral rebuts an overstatement of specialist dominance. [Document 11](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-11) reproduces a GP podiatry re-referral noting previous foot trouble **“in relating to increased tone and abnormal gait relating to Parkinson's disease”** and **“clawing feet” and pain**. The GP independently preserved an integrated relationship; there is **no proof that he adopted complete separation or confirmed neuropathy**. The record also shows that on 6 July 2017, during hospital admission, the GP independently pursued podiatric prioritisation ([Document 3](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-3)). Primary care did not surrender diagnostic agency, but specialist neurological treatment remained a distinct function.

### Podiatry: physical lesions, repeated treatment, uncertain transmission back

[Document 11](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-11) documents recurring callus/corn at the lateral left fifth metatarsophalangeal region, toe props, debridement, exercises, insoles and clawing. These are physical observations and treatment actions, not merely a neuropathic pain label. The 2019 GP referral links the pressure problem to movement. It is **not established** that the consultant received the 2019 referral or reviewed the complete podiatry chronology: a document held in community podiatry is not automatically knowledge in Neurology. The hospital's 5 August 2025 response identified podiatry as an HSE community-service record pathway; this separate administrative route reinforces the need to establish actual transfer and shared access rather than assume either.

### Neurologist's own later knowledge: 2018 and 2023–24

At his **April 2018** appointment, the consultant recorded the patient's report that symptoms at the **outside-left-foot corn site** returned rapidly when procyclidine was stopped, along with worsening tension; he also entertained young-onset PRKN/PARK2. [Document 12](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-12) supplies firsthand consultant **documentation of the report**, though the medication response remains the patient's reported observation, not proof that a physical corn regrew overnight. The neurologist therefore had specific foot-related treatment-state information independent of the GP's 2019 letter. [Documents 9–10](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-9) separately show the 2017 request for genetic opinion and Professor Healy's limited-legibility September 2017 response that he thought PARK2 probable. A genetic opinion need not directly determine lesion mechanism.

In 2023 Neurology considered other pain mechanisms, including peripheral neuropathic symptoms, medication-state effects and Fabry testing ([Document 18](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-18)). The record shows **real reconsideration and treatment**, not nine years of fixed prescriptions. [Document 19](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-19) records that pregabalin/Lyrica substantially improved pain while toe curling during activity continued: a distinction between analgesic response and persistent abnormal movement. It does not settle whether movement contributed to the physical lesions.

### March 2023: the clearest closed-loop stress test

The GP's **26 March 2023 urgent Healthlink referral** ([Document 17](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-17)) went to Neurology, named the consultant, described extreme bilateral burning foot/lower-leg pain and reported a **death wish**. He said he had started pregabalin at **25 mg twice daily**, with subsequent dose escalation through several clinicians, and noted an earlier episode of pain settling with better Parkinson's control. The wording demands both urgent **clinical access/safety triage** and reconsideration of a possible neurological treatment–pain relationship. Only **page 1 of the two-page referral** is in the local compilation, so attached material and the complete transmission should be obtained.

On **28 March** the Parkinson's/movement-disorder nurse emailed the neurologist directly after speaking to the patient. The original nurse email says that the **GP initiated pregabalin**, that the patient was taking about **600 mg/day**, had debilitating pain and reduced quality of life, and asked for neurological review. The nurse expressly wrote she was **unsure whether the consultant had seen the GP referral**. This is an unusually precise receipt boundary: **the nurse knew of the referral and sent an urgent clinical summary to the consultant; the consultant's direct receipt and reading of the GP's full referral are not proved by her wording**. The nurse also relayed that the patient then believed the pain was not Parkinson-related and had stopped Parkinson's medicines for ten days; this was a reported patient position, not the nurse's diagnostic conclusion. The full nurse email is outside the local 40-document compilation (canonical CLIN-002675–002688).

An associated note records the consultant discussion and a cancellation-list route because of clinic constraints, with the patient advised to return to the GP if difficulties arose meanwhile (canonical source note; not locally separately numbered). This shows an immediate access and responsibility bottleneck, **not** a complete absence of subsequent neurological work. The precise safety assessment, triage category, urgency rationale, offer of earlier contact and any structured plan following the reported death wish require the native correspondence and clinical record.

In **August 2025**, the GP wrote that he would not independently adjust levodopa without specialist guidance (canonical CLIN-005019–005025). This is direct evidence of an appropriate scope boundary, not evidence of a formal prohibition imposed by the 2017 letter. The GP's recognition of a motor–foot relationship and inability to independently deliver specialist revisions are **different forms of clinical agency**.

## The complaint, institutional review and records-rights chain

The patient's [19 March 2025 complaint](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-21) alleged anchoring, failure to reassess pain and the importance of Sinemet CR response; it requested independent review. It had a substantial **medication** component but was not confined to medication. The consultant's [25 March reply](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-22) acknowledged distress and defended the Parkinsonism diagnosis, levodopa/dyskinesia caution, longstanding Sinemet use and consideration of alternatives. It also referred to **“peripheral neuropathic pain symptomatologies”**, retaining an explanatory vocabulary without furnishing an integrated foot–posture–loading resolution. Some treatment rationale may be sound even if the original clinical allocation remained open.

The rebuttal described pregabalin as introduced partly to spare increasing Sinemet. Its **later specialist Sinemet-sparing role** is compatible with the 2023 record, but should not be passed backwards as proof of **the GP's reason at initial prescription**. The GP's contemporaneous referral and nurse's contemporaneous email establish treatment for severe pain, begun by the GP. This is a demonstrable distinction between the original decision's provenance and its later management interpretation, not proof of deceit.

A signed [SIMT outcome](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-31) and [3 September text](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-32) state that the PAR was presented **27 August 2025**, and that no deficit in care was found, citing careful levodopa timing and side-effect risks, alternatives considered, and first Sinemet prescription in 2021. The conclusion is broad; the **reasons communicated are substantially treatment-centric**. The full PAR, who authored it, its attachments, source inventory, evidence-review matrix, reviewer conflicts and meeting minutes are **not** reproduced. One cannot say the reviewers definitely ignored the referral, 2017 handwritten notes or protest; one can say the signed outcome does **not demonstrate their examination**.

By 27 August 2025, hospital-held institutional correspondence included the **12/15 June Article 16 rectification requests** ([Document 26](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-26) covers 12 June), the **7 July protest-related Article 15 request**, a **4 August Article 18/review-source email** asking about data versions and the proposed SIMT process, and the **5 August hospital records response**. Some requests were copied to QPS functions; that makes organisational notice stronger than mere delivery to an unconnected office. It **does not establish personal review** by individual SIMT members. A records-routing audit must test whether the PAR included the dispute and whether the reviewers were warned that an authoritative source was contested.

The hospital's [6 September decisions](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-33) and [Document 34](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-34) defended historical professional opinions and declined requested record changes. They should be judged against the actual remedies requested: fidelity of attributed patient words and supplementary annotation are analytically distinct from erasing a historically held medical opinion. The [26 January 2026 QPS response](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-35) said a later dystonia diagnosis did not require reopening previously appropriate reviews. That statement does not answer whether the **2017 evidence already available then** warranted an integrated foot assessment or named owner.

In February 2026 a specialist letter to the GP stated that two pathogenic Parkin variants explained Parkinsonism and dystonia ([Document 36](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-36)). A later 2026 laboratory report separately qualifies its causative interpretation by the variants' **in-trans/compound-heterozygous phase**, recommending segregation evidence (canonical genetic report CLIN-006181 onward). The consultant letter's unequivocal formulation and laboratory's conditional phase language must be read separately, not silently reconciled; neither alone proves the mechanical cause of lesions in 2017.

The [31 August 2026 Article 15 provenance request](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-37) targets both source/version history of the 2017 synthesis and the PAR/SIMT evidence/recipient chain. The [2 September Article 16 request](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-38) instead seeks durable attachment of a signed patient supplement **without altering** the original document; subsequent complaints appear as [Documents 39–40](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-39). These are not merely repeat clinical complaints: they test **how the historical information objects were made, transmitted and reused**. Their allegations of statutory non-compliance require controller/DPC assessment.

## Responsibility analysis: three separate duties to test

**Clinical responsibility.** The hospital recognised Parkinsonism and the possible dystonic gait, and obtained podiatry treatment. That may have been reasonable. The issue is not that certainty was obligatory; it is whether an unresolved interaction of dystonic movement, clawed toes, pressure lesions and pain was **adequately assessed or consciously allocated**—with a named service, follow-up trigger and feedback path. The recorded plan is clear about Parkinsonism medication and follow-up but not comparably specific about who would resolve that relationship. An independent clinician must examine what further assessment was reasonable in 2017 and later.

**Documentary responsibility.** A specialist may record provisional impressions, but the boundaries of the patient's own account should remain visible. The handwritten distinction was real; the final “markedly divorced” wording is a stronger authored attribution, not an independently confirmed patient aetiology. The protest, if proved, was contrary decision-live information absent from the completed account. Final confirmation after 10 July does not prove intentional revision. The native drafting history and witnesses control any allegation of conscious preservation.

**Transmission responsibility.** The hospital's output was downstream of its author and upstream of the GP's problem list and later clinician pathway. The GP recorded actual receipt of extensive correspondence on 18 July; later GP and podiatry material preserved the original integrated interpretation. The nurse relayed severe pain and treatment history to the consultant in 2023, without proving the consultant had read the GP's full referral. A clinically incomplete account may influence later actions **without** rendering downstream practitioners credulous or deprived of judgment. Specific recipient/read evidence, not general professional hierarchy or geographic proximity, must determine whether the missing objection or unassigned question materially changed care.

**Institutional review responsibility.** The 2025 PAR/SIMT conclusion was broader than its communicated rationale. The hospital had access to the July/August challenge in its organisational correspondence before SIMT, but that is not evidence every reviewer knew it. Its duty to establish a fair, independently evidenced determination must be tested using the PAR and distribution logs; the absence of discussion in the final letter is a **scope-of-reasons problem**, not proof of a hidden review's contents.

## Strongest supported conclusions and counter-explanations

**Supported with substantial confidence:**
- The full GP referral contained foot and clawing detail omitted from the hospital's opening précis; the neurologist nonetheless independently recorded important foot and dystonic-looking findings.
- The 2017 clinical account paired an expressly uncertain peripheral possibility with a strengthened claim about the patient's own mental separation; no locally identified clinical owner is specified for resolving the foot–motor relationship.
- The GP on 18 July 2017 recorded receiving extensive consultant correspondence and adopted the early Parkinsonism diagnosis; by 2019 he explicitly linked painful feet and clawing with Parkinsonian abnormal tone and gait.
- In March 2023 the GP urgently asked for help, and the nurse directly sent the consultant a further report. Treatment activity occurred; the original foot biomechanical–neurological relationship is not shown as finally answered.
- The July protest allegation was formally supplied to the hospital in a specific July 2025 access request, and a hospital access response preceded SIMT. The public no-deficit reasoning does not show assessment of that allegation or the initial handwritten-to-typed construction.

**Serious, still-contingent inferences:** The protest, if corroborated, would mean the consultant knew before discharge that the patient contested the adequacy of the feet investigation. Preservation of a stronger attributed separation while omitting that challenge could function to defend an established plan. The downstream information chain could then have reproduced a misleading appearance of patient agreement or completed investigation. **Neither intention, causation of later prescribing, nor individual reviewer knowledge is yet proved.**

**Strongest alternative explanations:** Clinicians may reasonably distinguish pain from rigidity and treat foot lesions through podiatry while evaluating Parkinsonism; specialist notes are condensed syntheses, not transcripts of every exchange; peripheral neuropathy was clearly qualified, not falsely labelled confirmed; a movement-disorder nurse and GP had independent access to fresh patient reports; a GP's refusal to adjust complex levodopa was prudent scope-of-practice rather than constraint created solely by one letter; a reviewer might have examined the disputed sources within the full PAR despite a short outcome letter. These alternatives materially weaken overbroad claims, but **do not themselves identify the original clinical owner or demonstrate the review's source validation**.
