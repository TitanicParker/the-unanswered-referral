# When Institutions Become the Authority on Their Own Errors

## What the possibility of the unanswered referral reveals about medical authority

*Independent philosophical essay · 18 November 2026 · A critical examination of the institutional mechanisms alleged in this case, not an adjudicated finding of dishonest intention.*

A case like this reveals a danger deeper than a mistaken diagnosis, an inaccurate note or a complaint rejected after review. **A healthcare institution can have the information necessary to recognize an important clinical question, fail to connect or adequately answer that question, and still produce an authoritative account in which the work appears complete.** Later clinicians and reviewers can inherit the account; the continued existence of an organized treatment history can then be mistaken for proof that the original representation was faithful.

The disturbing possibility is that the institution's documentary strength can increase at the same time as the patient's original question becomes harder to recover. That is not merely a failure of expertise. It is a problem concerning the production of institutional knowledge, the distribution of professional authority, and the ability of the person being treated to challenge an official history written in his name.

The distinctive moral question is this: **What happens when the clinician's power to interpret the patient, the record's power to define the patient, and the institution's power to judge its own conduct all reinforce the same potentially mistaken account?**

## 1. A hospital can lose the patient while preserving the paperwork

The [urgent GP referral of 29 June 2017](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-2) brought together painful foot pathology, toe clawing, abnormal gait, increased tone and deteriorating movement. The hospital's opening précis, visible in the [July admission and discharge record](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-4), retained much of the movement presentation but omitted the explicit painful-foot and clawing details. That was not merely shorter wording. It narrowed the formulation of the problem presented at the institutional threshold.

The neurologist then recorded abnormal foot posture and a striking gait suggestive of dystonia rather than true foot drop. Relevant information therefore existed within the hospital's own records. Yet the causal relationship between neurological motor activity, altered toe position, pressure lesions and pain was not demonstrably resolved by identifying Parkinsonism and providing a podiatry route.

An institution can thus possess pieces of information without performing the intellectual work that makes them clinically useful. The missing act is *integration*: recognizing the common question, bringing the relevant observations into relation, assigning clinical responsibility for what remains unknown, and checking that the disposition answers what prompted the referral. An institution may conduct many tests and treatments and nonetheless fail to complete that act.

**The presence of extensive medical work is not proof that the question actually referred was answered.** A process can be administratively finished while a patient's problem remains clinically unresolved.

## 2. The official patient can displace the person whose experiences supplied the record

The [3 July handwritten interview](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-7) records the patient distinguishing foot pain from leg tension after reduced standing altered the prominence of the pain. The reported sensations were different. The patient could perceive that difference with authority because they were his sensations.

But distinct sensations do not entail distinct pathological causes. An abnormal contraction could affect toe posture; pressure could then create corns or calluses; those lesions could cause a kind of pain different from muscular tension. This motor–mechanical sequence is a hypothesis requiring investigation, not a diagnosis established by the materials here. Its logical importance is that **experiential separation cannot itself establish medical independence**.

The [completed impression](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-4) entertained the possibility that the problems were separate or represented a secondary peripheral neuropathy, while noting uncertainty. It also attributed to the patient the stronger position that he had “in his own mind - markedly divorced” the leg tension from the foot complaints. The patient alleges that this was a deliberate strengthening of his meaning and that he did not endorse a medical separation of causes.

If that allegation is proved, the wrongdoing is not simply the recording of a questionable clinical inference. It is the *appropriation of another person's testimonial authority*: a clinician's uncertain explanation is made to appear corroborated by the patient's supposed understanding. The person from whom the evidence came is transformed into a witness for the interpretation applied to him.

The asymmetry is severe. The patient can know what he experienced and what he said, while the professional writes the enduring institutional account of both. Once that account is treated as authoritative, the patient may have to challenge a document that purports to speak for him. His attempt to recover his own meaning can be heard as disagreement with expertise when it is, in the first instance, a challenge to attribution.

**Professional authority to interpret symptoms is not authority to determine retrospectively what the patient believed.** When that distinction collapses, the record begins to govern the source of the very testimony from which it was constructed.

## 3. A warning that should prompt correction can instead become a threat to an existing disposition

The patient's [signed supplementary statement](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-38) describes a confrontation on 10 July 2017. Expecting discharge the next day, he says he asked four medical students to obtain the neurologist and protested that his feet had not been adequately investigated. He recalls the neurologist responding about the ease of obtaining a podiatry appointment. This is retrospective firsthand testimony, not a contemporaneous hospital entry; its status should be described accurately and its implications examined seriously.

The philosophical significance of this encounter is that it was not simply another symptom report. **The patient was returning the missing substance of the original referral to the clinician responsible for addressing it.** He was alerting the clinician that the actual work of the admission might not correspond to the question the hospital had received.

That made the protest a corrective opportunity. The clinician could explain what had been investigated, recognize the remaining causal uncertainty, identify further assessment or an appropriate owner for the question, and record the disagreement faithfully. A podiatry referral might remain useful; it would not by itself show that the neurological contribution had been assessed.

On the patient's allegation, the completed narrative instead gave his words an appearance of endorsing the clinical separation and omitted the very objection that challenged it. If done knowingly, the response to a potential system failure became a means of protecting the position the system had already taken.

That possibility reveals a general institutional danger: **an organization becomes unsafe when evidence that it may have failed creates pressure to defend its prior decision rather than an effective mechanism for reconsidering it**.

## 4. Defensibility can be constructed before clinical responsibility is fulfilled

A clinical decision is justified when good reasons, faithful investigation and appropriate professional judgment support it. It is defensible when reasons can be marshalled and presented for it. These are not identical achievements.

The record is meant to preserve what medicine examined, what medicine found, what medicine could not establish and what medicine intended to do next. Its authority should derive from that correspondence. A professional account cannot create the missing investigation merely by rendering the outcome coherent.

The [completed July discharge account](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-4) was confirmed on 17 July after the alleged 10 July confrontation. The precise date at which the disputed phrase was first drafted is not established from confirmation alone. But the professional significance of authorisation remains: the completed account was adopted after the patient says he had directly challenged the sufficiency of the foot investigation.

This is the patient's *line in the sand*: the point where an open clinical question entered the continuing record as an apparently adequately disposed-of problem. Parkinsonism was investigated; foot pathology had a peripheral possibility and a podiatry route; the patient appeared to have divided the two in his own thinking. The unresolved question could travel forward beneath the appearance of an answer.

**Premature defensibility occurs when an account is made capable of justifying an existing disposition before the clinical work needed to justify that disposition has been performed or adequately allocated.** On the allegation of knowing misrepresentation, professional record-making was diverted from faithful representation toward the protection of a settled clinical position.

## 5. An unproven proposition can gain authority through inheritance

One of the most dangerous properties of medical documentation is that a tentative possibility can remain tentative in wording yet become effectively settled in practice.

A clinician writes that neuropathy may be involved. A later clinician reads the note and treats neuropathic pain. Several years of follow-up accumulate. A reviewer then sees a long, coherent history of diagnosis and treatment. No one needs to have formally asserted that the original hypothesis was proved for it to have become part of the patient's institutional identity.

This is *epistemic inheritance*: a claim gains operational authority by being repeatedly relied upon, not necessarily by being independently verified.

The later records matter because they contain evidence that could complicate an overly simple division. In [2018](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-12), the neurologist recorded renewed trouble at the outside-left-foot corn site following withdrawal of procyclidine. In [2023](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-18), he considered Parkinsonian OFF dystonia as a possible explanation for lower-limb cramping. In [2024](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-19), pregabalin relieved pain while toe curling persisted. In [2025](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-20), the neurologist recorded Sinemet-related relief of foot discomfort, acknowledged that dystonia can be painful, and maintained a distinction between the patient's particular pain and direct Parkinsonian pain.

None of these observations proves a single unified cause. They make a different point: there were repeated opportunities to examine how abnormal movement, toe posture, mechanical loading and painful lesions might relate. A prior peripheral possibility could properly remain part of a differential diagnosis. It should not become a reason for refusing to investigate connections that new observations make pertinent.

**The record ought to carry uncertainty forward as uncertainty—not convert continued reliance upon an explanation into evidence that the explanation was already established.**

## 6. An institution can review conduct without reviewing the formation of its knowledge

The [neurologist's March 2025 response](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-22) and his [July 2025 submission to the Medical Council](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-29) defended the rationale for cautious levodopa treatment, particularly its risks in a younger person. The submission expressly refers to the Neurology Department's 2017 complex-case discussion. These are substantive clinical arguments, not something to dismiss simply because the patient disputes the outcome.

But the arguments address specific treatment decisions. They do not, merely by existing, establish that the original referral was answered or that the patient's understanding was faithfully attributed in the completed 2017 account.

The [September 2025 signed hospital outcome](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-31) communicated a finding of no deficit in care, referring to the clinical notes, caution about introducing Sinemet at an early age and consideration of alternative diagnoses. The underlying full Preliminary Assessment Report is not reproduced in the collection. Therefore the outcome alone does not establish exactly what the reviewers did or did not inspect.

Nevertheless, the institutional question is exact: **did the review independently test the fidelity of the initial representation against the GP referral, handwritten interview and patient account of the protest—or did it chiefly test the reasonableness of treatment as documented within the completed representation?**

A review is not independent simply because a different professional reads an earlier clinician's notes. Independence requires the ability to challenge the premises of the notes themselves. When the allegation is that the record was knowingly distorted, the record cannot authenticate its own accuracy. Otherwise the initial author's interpretation becomes both the subject of the dispute and the evidence that supposedly settles it.

**An institution can review a hundred decisions and still fail to examine the one mistaken premise that shaped them.**

## 7. Patient safety and institutional success can part company

The patient's continuing condition must not disappear behind debates about historical defensibility.

The [urgent 2023 GP referral](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-17) described extreme bilateral burning pain and psychological distress, including a reported death wish. That is a serious clinical and safeguarding concern whatever explanation eventually proves correct. Later treatment responses and persisting abnormal toe movement likewise demanded responsiveness to the patient as he actually was, not solely to his established diagnostic history.

It is possible for individual prescriptions to be reasonable and for an important causal question still to be unanswered. It is possible for a hospital to make a defensible decision about levodopa while failing to provide a demonstrably integrated assessment of painful clawed feet. It is possible for care to occur over many years without anyone clearly taking ownership of the relationship between abnormal movement and mechanical injury.

A hospital's success cannot consist exclusively in showing that procedures were followed, treatment risks were considered or notes were extensive. Those are necessary considerations, but they do not exhaust the patient-protective purpose of medicine. **The institution's responsibility is to make the patient safer, not merely to make its decisions harder to challenge.**

That principle is not hostile to clinicians. It is the justification for their authority. Expertise and documentation are entrusted to professionals so that human beings can receive better care. If those tools cease to be responsive to the patient's condition and the evidence challenging the established view, their institutional legitimacy has begun to separate from their purpose.

## 8. The patient's request to preserve disagreement reveals what accountability actually demands

The [September 2026 supplementary-statement request](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-38) makes an ethically important point. The patient did not demand deletion or silent rewriting of the historical clinician-authored account. He asked for his signed contrary statement to be durably attached or cross-referenced, so that later readers would see that the account was contested and understand the basis of the contest.

That is not a demand that the patient replace clinical judgment with his own. It is a demand that the institution preserve the evidential conditions under which conflicting accounts can be evaluated.

A responsible institution can hold both the historical note and the patient's challenge without pretending that the coexistence of the two resolves the disagreement. It can preserve versions, authorship and provenance. It can ask what was recorded at the time, what was later remembered, what the words logically entail and what happened after notice was given.

The ethical contrast is striking. The patient asked the system to keep the clinician's original account intact while making his own testimony visible. His central allegation is that the clinician did not provide the same fair representation of the patient's contrary view when completing the original record.

**Accountability is not the power to choose which voice survives. It is the discipline of preserving the evidence necessary to decide whether an authoritative account was faithful.**

## 9. Clinical, documentary and reviewing authority can reinforce one another

The deepest systemic danger lies in the alignment of three powers.

First, the clinician has authority to interpret the patient's condition. Second, the medical record carries that interpretation forward as the official account. Third, the hospital has authority to review whether the resulting course of care was appropriate.

Each power can be a safeguard. Yet each can also amplify the others. A clinician interprets; the record preserves the interpretation; later care follows the record; the long history seems to confirm the interpretation; reviewers assess the care through the accumulated record. An apparently substantial institutional consensus can emerge without the original question ever being reopened against its source materials.

This is why institutional agreement must not be confused with independent corroboration. **Several authorities relying on the same original representation do not become several independent witnesses to its truth.** Their agreement may show consistency in practice; it may also reveal the reach of an inherited premise.

Where there is an allegation of knowing alteration, the moral stakes increase. If the clinician deliberately transformed what the patient said into supposed support for the clinician's explanation, the original account was not simply an uncertain judgment. It was potentially a corrupted foundation for subsequent authority. Later institutional confidence cannot repair that foundation without examining it.

## 10. What it says that this case can arise at all

The most dangerous institutional failure is not always one in which nobody has enough knowledge to help. It may be one in which **enough is known to ask the right question, but the organization can more readily produce and defend an answer to a narrower question**.

That is the possibility this case exposes. The GP transmitted a connected problem. The hospital's opening representation narrowed it. The clinician acquired further relevant information. The patient reports returning the unanswered question directly to him. The final authorised account attributed a stronger separation to the patient's understanding and did not preserve the reported protest. Later clinical observations invited renewed attention to the original relationship. The clinician and institution then defended the treatment history within the accumulated record.

The documentary differences, later clinical entries and published institutional outcome can be inspected. The allegations about knowing intention, exact drafting chronology, and the complete scope of the hospital review require their own evidential examination. A rigorous moral argument loses nothing by keeping those tasks distinct.

The conclusion is not that doctors must always find the correct cause or that every adverse outcome proves misconduct. It is that **clinical and institutional authority remain legitimate only if they can discover and acknowledge that their official history may be wrong**. A review must be able to examine the record as a disputed human construction, not merely use it as a settled account of the patient.

The record must answer to the patient and the evidence. The patient must never be made to answer to the record.

**A hospital must be capable of discovering that its own authoritative account is mistaken, even when that discovery threatens the apparent adequacy of its earlier decisions. Without that capacity, accountability can become the procedure through which the institution proves it was right, instead of the means through which it can discover that it was wrong.**
