# THE CASE: THE PROTEST, THE ALTERED MEANING, AND THE PERIPHERAL-NEUROPATHY FRAMEWORK

## The mechanism

**Prior knowledge → Direct protest → Deliberate alteration of meaning → Peripheral explanation → Omission of objection → Authorised record → Prolonged clinical consequences → Retrospective defence**

The sequence is anchored to the **29 June 2017 GP referral**, **3 July admission and handwritten interview**, **approximately 4 July complex-case discussion**, **10 July patient protest**, **11 July discharge**, and **17 July confirmation of the discharge account**. The patient gives firsthand testimony of the 10 July confrontation. The documentary comparison concerns the GP referral, the neurologist's handwritten interview, and the completed clinical account. The account of deliberate purpose and of what informed the complex-case meeting is the patient's case for independent examination.

## Statement I — The direct confrontation and the rewritten patient

The GP referred a patient whose painful, clawing feet, abnormal gait, increased tone and developing neurological dysfunction required investigation together. The hospital's opening summary compressed that presentation, and a senior neurological discussion had already established a management direction by approximately 4 July. On 10 July, the eighth day of admission, the patient confronted the treating neurologist about the failure to study his feet. The neurologist's answer was that they were going to podiatry. The patient was not introducing a new complaint: he was returning the substance of the original referral to the clinician responsible for answering it.

The completed clinical account did not preserve that confrontation. Instead, it turned the patient's distinction between *foot pain* and *muscular tension* into a stronger attribution that he had, **“in his own mind - markedly divorced”** the problems. That attribution sat beside the clinician's tentative explanation that the painful feet might be separate or represent **secondary peripheral neuropathy**. The patient's own discernment was made to serve a clinical division he had not endorsed.

**The case is that, faced with a direct challenge to the adequacy of the admission, the neurologist deliberately altered the patient's meaning, reinforced a peripheral explanation, omitted the protest and authorised a clinical account that protected the established disposition rather than answering the referral.**

## Statement II — The manufacture of apparent clinical support

The clinical question was whether the painful foot deformity, toe clawing and recurrent pressure pathology were connected with the movement disorder, including its dystonic component. A patient can distinguish the sensation of pressure-related foot pain from muscular tension without deciding that the underlying causes are medically unrelated. Such a distinction may be fully consistent with dystonic muscle activity producing abnormal toe posture, altered loading, corns, calluses and pain. The GP's referral required precisely that relationship to be examined.

The written sequence moved in the opposite direction. The original referral presented the problem together; the opening summary narrowed it; the handwritten interview recorded different experiences; and the completed impression attributed an emphatic mental separation to the patient while entertaining peripheral neuropathy. The neurologist also used burning and leg-pain descriptions within the peripheral explanatory framework, whose precise provenance must be examined against the original notes. The completed account retained the clinician's speculation but not the patient's 10 July objection that his feet had not been adequately investigated.

**The case is that the neurologist converted firsthand symptom description into apparent patient endorsement of the neurologist's own causal hypothesis. The record thereby acquired support for a peripheral-neuropathy framework that the original interview did not supply, while the protest that challenged the separation disappeared from the authorised clinical history.**

## Statement III — The patient who carried the consequences

The significance of the 2017 record extended beyond discharge. Once the painful feet were framed as potentially peripheral to the movement disorder, the patient could continue receiving treatment for Parkinsonism and local treatment for painful lesions without the integrated neurological–mechanical explanation being resolved. The peripheral-neuropathy hypothesis was recorded as uncertain, not established as a proven diagnosis. Yet its presence supplied a durable explanatory route by which the feet could be considered apart from dystonic toe movements, abnormal posture and pressure-related pain.

The actual patient carried the ongoing symptoms: recurrent painful foot pathology, abnormal toe curling, impaired mobility and the continuing need for assessment and treatment. Later evidence of dystonic phenomena and persistent abnormal foot posture makes the original neurological contribution an essential question, not a question disposed of by a speculative neuropathy label. When the clinical history was subsequently defended, the recorded diagnosis, treatment and follow-up could appear coherent without demonstrating that the GP's original foot question had ever been answered.

**The case is that the neurologist knowingly preserved a separation of the patient's problems through a strengthened attribution of the patient's own words, omission of the direct protest and post-protest authorisation of the record. That representation helped sustain a peripheral explanation while the potential dystonic mechanism remained unresolved, and it later supplied the terms on which the adequacy of care was defended.**

## The precise issue for independent ethical and documentary examination

**Did the completed record faithfully answer the problem referred by the GP and directly raised again by the patient on 10 July, or did the clinician's own construction of the patient's meaning make the unanswered neurological–foot relationship appear settled enough to leave within a peripheral pathway?**

The core documentary distinction is between **different experienced symptoms** and **different medical causes**. The central ethical distinction is between **a clinician openly proposing a hypothesis** and **a clinician attributing that hypothesis's supporting premise to the patient**. The central temporal distinction is between **drafting an account** and **authorising it on 17 July after the patient's protest**.

The patient's firsthand account of the confrontation, the originals of the GP referral and handwriting, the complete discharge record, the sources available to the complex-case meeting, and the subsequent movement-disorder and foot findings must all be examined on their own terms. Neither an omitted protest nor a professionally authored account can, by itself, settle the reliability of that account.
