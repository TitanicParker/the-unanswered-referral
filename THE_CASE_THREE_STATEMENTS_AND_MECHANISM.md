# THE CASE: THE PROTEST, THE ALTERED MEANING, AND THE PERIPHERAL-NEUROPATHY FRAMEWORK

## The mechanism

**Prior knowledge → Direct protest → Deliberate alteration of meaning → Peripheral explanation → Omission of objection → Authorised record → Prolonged clinical consequences → Retrospective defence**

The sequence is anchored to the **29 June 2017 GP referral**, **3 July admission and handwritten interview**, **approximately 4 July complex-case discussion**, **10 July patient protest**, **11 July discharge**, and **17 July confirmation of the discharge account**. The patient gives firsthand testimony of the 10 July confrontation. The documentary comparison concerns the GP referral, the neurologist's handwritten interview, and the completed clinical account. The account of deliberate purpose and of what informed the complex-case meeting is the patient's case for independent examination.

## Statement I — The direct confrontation and the rewritten patient

The GP referred a patient whose painful, clawing feet, abnormal gait, increased tone and developing neurological dysfunction required investigation together. The hospital's opening summary compressed that presentation, and a senior neurological discussion had already established a management direction by approximately 4 July. On 10 July, the eighth day of admission, the patient confronted the treating neurologist about the failure to study his feet. The neurologist's answer was that they were going to podiatry. The patient was not introducing a new complaint: he was returning the substance of the original referral to the clinician responsible for answering it.

The completed clinical account did not preserve that confrontation. Instead, it turned the patient's distinction between *foot pain* and *muscular tension* into a stronger attribution that he had, **“in his own mind - markedly divorced”** the problems. That attribution sat beside the clinician's tentative explanation that the painful feet might be separate or represent **secondary peripheral neuropathy**. The patient's own discernment was made to serve a clinical division he had not endorsed.

**The case is that, faced with a direct challenge to the adequacy of the admission, the neurologist deliberately altered the patient's meaning, reinforced a peripheral explanation, omitted the protest and authorised a clinical account that protected the established disposition rather than answering the referral.**

## Statement II — The manufacture of apparent clinical support

The clinical question was whether the painful foot deformity, toe clawing and recurrent pressure pathology were connected with the movement disorder, including its dystonic component. A patient can distinguish the sensation of pressure-related foot pain from muscular tension without deciding that the underlying causes are medically unrelated. Such a distinction may be fully consistent with dystonic muscle activity producing abnormal toe posture, altered loading, corns, calluses and pain. The GP's referral required precisely that relationship to be examined.

The written sequence moved in the opposite direction. The original referral presented the problem together; the opening summary narrowed it; the handwritten interview recorded different experiences; and the completed impression attributed an emphatic mental separation to the patient while entertaining peripheral neuropathy. The neurologist also used burning and leg-pain descriptions within the peripheral explanatory framework, whose precise provenance must be examined against the original notes. The completed account retained the clinician's speculation but not the patient's 10 July objection that his feet had not been adequately investigated.

**The case is that the neurologist converted firsthand symptom description into apparent patient endorsement of the neurologist's own causal hypothesis. The record thereby acquired support for a peripheral-neuropathy framework that the original interview did not supply, while the protest that challenged the separation disappeared from the authorised clinical history.**

## Statement III — The patient who carried the consequences

The significance of the 2017 record extended beyond discharge. Once the painful feet were framed as potentially peripheral to the movement disorder, the patient could continue receiving treatment for Parkinsonism and local treatment for painful lesions without the integrated neurological–mechanical explanation being resolved. The peripheral-neuropathy hypothesis was recorded as uncertain, not established as a proven diagnosis. Yet its presence supplied a durable explanatory route by which the feet could be considered apart from dystonic toe movements, abnormal posture and pressure-related pain.

The actual patient carried the ongoing symptoms: recurrent painful foot pathology, abnormal toe curling, impaired mobility and the continuing need for assessment and treatment. Later evidence of dystonic phenomena and persistent abnormal foot posture makes the original neurological contribution an essential question, not a question disposed of by a speculative neuropathy label. When the clinical history was subsequently defended, the recorded diagnosis, treatment and follow-up could appear coherent without demonstrating that the GP's original foot question had ever been answered.

**The case is that the neurologist knowingly preserved a separation of the patient's problems through a strengthened attribution of the patient's own words, omission of the direct protest and post-protest authorisation of the record. That representation helped sustain a peripheral explanation while the potential dystonic mechanism remained unresolved, and it later supplied the terms on which the adequacy of care was defended.**

## The precise issue for independent ethical and documentary examination

**Did the completed record faithfully answer the problem referred by the GP and directly raised again by the patient on 10 July, or did the clinician's own construction of the patient's meaning make the unanswered neurological–foot relationship appear settled enough to leave within a peripheral pathway?**

The core documentary distinction is between **different experienced symptoms** and **different medical causes**. The central ethical distinction is between **a clinician openly proposing a hypothesis** and **a clinician attributing that hypothesis's supporting premise to the patient**. The central temporal distinction is between **drafting an account** and **authorising it on 17 July after the patient's protest**.

The patient's firsthand account of the confrontation, the originals of the GP referral and handwriting, the complete discharge record, the sources available to the complex-case meeting, and the subsequent movement-disorder and foot findings must all be examined on their own terms. Neither an omitted protest nor a professionally authored account can, by itself, settle the reliability of that account.

## Contribution 1 — The authority to interpret versus the obligation to represent

**Question:** At what point does legitimate clinical interpretation become wrongful attribution of an interpretation to the patient? Distinguish what the patient felt, what the physician inferred, and what the patient was said to believe. Examine professional control over wording and the unequal institutional authority of the completed record.

**Independent contribution:**

### The distinction that makes authority legitimate

Clinical expertise confers authority over medical *interpretation*, not ownership of what another person experienced, asserted or understood. In a consultation the patient reports a lived event, the physician examines a body, and the physician develops an explanation. These are three different evidential acts. The ethical legitimacy of the record depends on leaving their origins distinguishable. The patient may be wrong about disease mechanism, but the clinician cannot correct an alleged diagnostic error by attributing a different belief to the patient. An accurate statement that the doctor thought the conditions separate cannot substitute for an inaccurate statement that the *patient* thought so.

The 3 July manuscript says that the patient began to appreciate foot pain and leg tension “weren't the same” as the pain diminished after less prolonged standing ([Document 7](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-7)). This is a report about experienced difference and divergent symptom courses. It supplies no necessary conclusion about independent causes. The completed account adds a second-order claim about the patient's thought: he had “in his own mind - markedly divorced” the two problems, next to “may have been completely separate or perhaps a secondary peripheral neuropathy” and “Not clear” ([Document 4](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-4)). The inference is transformed into an attribution. The point is not that the patient never distinguished the sensations; the manuscript shows that he did. The point is the *increment in attributed meaning* and its proximity to a causal hypothesis.

The most charitable reading is that “markedly divorced” means only that the patient's experiences seemed different to him. Ordinary clinical shorthand often compresses those distinctions. But if that is its intended sense, it cannot independently support the separate-neuropathy explanation: it adds no causal evidence beyond the original experiential report. If instead it implies that the patient regarded the underlying conditions as causally distinct, it requires an evidential basis the quoted manuscript does not supply. The clinician cannot obtain independent corroboration in either direction merely by interpreting his own summary generously.

The physician controls what becomes the durable record. The patient does not ordinarily control its technical language, transmission or later institutional standing. This asymmetry makes fidelity a substantive duty, not a matter of stylistic preference. S. ## Contribution 2 — The original referral and the ethics of answering the actual question

**Question:** What obligation does a specialist admission owe to the substantive clinical question in the referring GP's letter? Explore the difference between successfully diagnosing Parkinsonism and adequately investigating its possible relationship to abnormal toe posture, tissue loading and foot pain. Ask whether a referral can be *functionally unanswered* despite extensive clinical activity.

**Independent contribution:**

### The referral defines a problem, not merely a diagnostic destination

A specialist admission owes the referral a reasoned *disposition* of its material questions. It does not owe a guaranteed diagnosis or every investigation requested, and the neurologist may legitimately prioritise a serious movement disorder. It does owe intelligible consideration of the clinical relationship that motivated referral, including a decision about what remains uncertain, who will examine it and how the answer will reach the patient and GP. A correct diagnosis of Parkinsonism is not logically equivalent to answering whether abnormal muscular activity produced toe clawing, altered pressure and painful lesions.

The GP's 29 June letter describes bilateral foot problems, corns and calluses, stiff/festinant gait, marked tone and toes clawing when the patient sought comfort in bed ([Document 2](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-2)). Earlier GP notes likewise join gait, feet and muscle tension ([Document 1](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-1)). The hospital's opening précis retains movement initiation, stiffness, tone and cogwheeling but not the explicit painful-foot and clawing components ([Document 4](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-4)). This is not proof that no doctor knew of the feet—the neurologist later described them—but it is a change in the *question made salient* at the entrance to specialist reasoning.

The philosophical danger is **answer substitution**: the team may answer “What movement disorder is present?” and allow the answer to stand in for “What accounts for the joint presentation of disordered movement and painful deformed feet?” An elaborate admission can be diagnostically successful in one dimension and explanatory incomplete in another. A referral can therefore be *functionally unanswered* despite examinations, imaging, medication and referral onward. The decisive criterion is not the amount of work done but whether the initiating clinical problem received a reasoned answer or an explicit, owned plan for resolving it.

The case becomes more exact if framed in terms of responsibility rather than assuming every foot abnormality had to be fully explained as an inpatient. Podiatry may treat a callus; neurology may identify dystonic posture; the crucial integrative question is whether someone was responsible for their possible causal interaction. A referral to another service is adequate only to the extent that it addresses the question referred or expressly transfers the unresolved question with sufficient information and responsibility. **A pathway that treats each manifestation while leaving the mechanism between them unowned can look complete precisely because everyone has performed a task.**

## Contribution 3 — The 10 July confrontation as knowledge, notice and corrective opportunity

**Question:** What changed ethically and epistemically when the patient personally confronted the neurologist about his feet? The patient states this occurred on 10 July and that the neurologist answered that the feet were going to podiatry. Examine this as a direct challenge to the adequacy of an admission already shaped by a complex-case discussion, not merely another symptom report. Explain why the absence of the protest from the hospital record is not a logical disproof of the encounter.

**Independent contribution:**

### Notice of a failure is not the same epistemic event as reporting a symptom

The 10 July bedside encounter, taken as the patient's firsthand account states, was a direct challenge to the sufficiency of the admission. The patient had already supplied the foot history. He now confronted the responsible neurologist because discharge approached without, in his understanding, an adequate investigation of the feet. His “full NCT” and “bald tyres” analogy was an argument about the mismatch between an extensive general examination and a still-unanswered presenting problem, not an invitation simply to add a further symptom to the chart ([Document 38](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-38)).

The ethical transformation is from *possible oversight* to *received notice*. The clinician now knew that the patient disputed whether the actual reason for referral had been answered. That knowledge does not prove the patient's preferred investigation was required. It does create distinct obligations: to understand the challenge, reconsider whether the current explanation and plan adequately cover the feet, explain the disposition, and preserve materially significant disagreement and responsibility for follow-up. The clinician might have answered that neurologic causes had been considered and podiatry would assess mechanical pathology; a faithful account could have recorded that reasoning and the patient's objection. Directing the patient to podiatry, by itself, did not answer whether neurologically abnormal posture was generating the pathology requiring podiatry.

An objection about investigation sufficiency is *meta-clinical evidence*: it concerns the adequacy of the clinical process as applied to this patient. Its importance lies partly in the fact that it may reopen the definition of the problem. A doctor can disagree with it, but must not knowingly convert the record into a picture of tacit patient endorsement.

The protest's absence from the identified completed narrative does not logically disprove the encounter. A record cannot be treated as a perfect census of events when the complaint specifically concerns its selectivity. The patient's signed statement is affirmative firsthand testimony; the four students, ward records and earlier 2025 accounts are potential additional evidential routes, not preconditions for analysing what the encounter means. On the premise that it occurred, the subsequent 17 July authorisation raises a different question: why was a material challenge known before completion not preserved or substantively addressed in the authoritative synthesis? **The protest was an opportunity to correct the clinical problem-definition before the hospital's version became final.**

## Contribution 4 — The complex-case meeting and the information available to decision-makers

**Question:** What follows if the approximately 4 July senior discussion received only the hospital-compressed presentation rather than the full GP referral? Identify what is known, what remains to be obtained, and why the 10 July protest might have challenged the informational basis of decisions already made. Do not treat the meeting's source material as established without its records.

**Independent contribution:**

### The epistemic consequences of an early narrowed problem representation

A complex-case meeting can reason expertly from the information it receives and still reach a disposition insufficiently responsive to the full referral. Collective expertise does not cancel the epistemological effects of case framing. If the approximately 4 July discussion worked principally from the compressed hospital précis, its participants would have confronted a problem accentuating initiation difficulty, rigidity and Parkinsonism rather than the full question about painful clawed feet, abnormal loading and neurological tone. That could rationally focus discussion on Parkinsonian diagnosis and medication while rendering the foot mechanism peripheral to the agenda.

This is an inquiry into *information architecture*. A summary determines what is cognitively available, salient and assigned responsibility. The ethical burden is not that meetings must reproduce every referral sentence. It is that the summary must preserve features whose omission could alter the decision or obscure an unresolved task. A multidisciplinary conclusion is only as responsive as the problem it has actually deliberated. A clinician with fuller firsthand knowledge bears an additional responsibility not to let a narrowed team summary substitute for that knowledge.

The record shows the opening précis was narrower and the neurologist separately documented a dystonic-looking gait, pes planus and hammertoes ([Documents 2 and 4](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-4)). It does not reproduce the meeting pack, minutes or individual contributions. Accordingly, the strongest philosophical proposition is conditional and causal rather than a fabricated quotation from the meeting: **to the extent the clinical direction was determined through a narrowed account, the consultation's authority could not settle the omitted foot–movement relationship**. The reported 10 July protest then became a direct challenge to a disposition already formed. A professional response would reconsider whether the team's conclusion depended on an incomplete framing and, if necessary, return the question to an accountable clinical process.

The decisive documentary tests are the meeting agenda and pack, original referral in the inpatient chart, presentation notes, attendance, recorded differential diagnoses, podiatry instructions and discharge responsibilities. A strong opposing possibility is that the full referral and foot findings were discussed and a reasoned separation was adopted. Even then, that reason would have to be identified; the existence of a meeting alone does not supply it. The deepest principle is that **seniority and consensus cannot make a question answered if the question was not meaningfully put before the decision-makers.**

## Contribution 5 — The manufacture of apparent corroboration

**Question:** How could the written phrase **“in his own mind - markedly divorced”**, alongside a speculative peripheral-neuropathy explanation, make one clinician-authored inference look like two convergent sources of support? Examine the philosophical concepts of testimonial appropriation, evidential laundering and manufactured corroboration, distinguishing analytical metaphors from established professional or legal terms.

**Independent contribution:**

### A single source cannot corroborate itself by changing grammatical ownership

The three documentary stages should be treated as a chain of transformation, not three independent witnesses. Stage one: the patient distinguishes what foot pain feels like from what leg tension feels like, particularly after prolonged standing diminishes. Stage two: the typed history expresses those sensations as different “problems.” Stage three: the completed impression says the patient has “in his own mind - markedly divorced” them, immediately after the author's conjecture that the pain and corns/calluses “may have been completely separate or perhaps a secondary peripheral neuropathy” and the qualification “Not clear” ([Documents 7 and 4](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-7)). Each stage has the same informational origin unless an independent source of patient endorsement can be shown.

The fallacy is a shift in *source attribution*. A physician may infer a causal separation from symptom patterns. But if the inference is then phrased as the patient's emphatic belief, a reader may count professional judgment and patient understanding as converging evidence. That is manufactured *apparent* corroboration: not the creation of an additional observation, but the presentation of one interpretive move as though a second witness supports it.

“Evidential laundering” and “testimonial appropriation” are analytical descriptions of the mechanism, not formal clinical diagnoses or findings of dishonesty. They capture two losses. Provenance is lost when a clinician's inference is made to appear the patient's independent conclusion. Contestability is lost when the patient must dispute an official account that claims to speak for his own mind. Miranda Fricker's *Epistemic Injustice* (2007) explains how people may be wronged as knowers; Carel and Kidd, “Epistemic injustice in healthcare: a philosophical analysis” (*Medicine, Health Care and Philosophy*, 2014), show the heightened clinical relevance of professional credibility asymmetries. Strict testimonial injustice in Fricker's sense additionally involves identity prejudice; this case's mechanism can be wrongful misattribution without proving that extra element.

A charitable contrary reading holds that the phrase describes only subjective divergence, not etiological endorsement. That reading has a decisive consequence: the phrase then supplies *no independent evidence* for peripheral neuropathy. The stronger accusation holds where the clinician understood the statement would communicate causal patient endorsement and knowingly let it do so. **The more evidential work the patient-attributed phrase is asked to perform, the more important its fidelity becomes.**

## Contribution 6 — Peripheral neuropathy, dystonia and the separation of clinical responsibilities

**Question:** How might a provisional peripheral-neuropathy explanation and referral to podiatry allow neurological investigation, lesion treatment and pain control to proceed in parallel while the causal link between dystonic activity, toe posture, altered pressure and recurrent lesions remains unresolved? Distinguish the **hypothesised dystonic–mechanical pathway** from a subsequently established diagnosis and examine the evidence needed to test each link.

**Independent contribution:**

### Competing mechanisms and the hidden division between treating lesions and explaining their production

Peripheral neuropathy and a neurological–mechanical mechanism are not necessarily exclusive. Neuropathy can produce burning or altered sensation; structural factors can produce corns; abnormal motor activity can alter posture and loading; mixed mechanisms are possible. The 2017 completed impression tentatively suggested separate pathology or peripheral neuropathy and expressly said “Not clear” ([Document 4](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-4)). The error examined here is not the mere inclusion of a peripheral differential. It is allowing an unproved peripheral account to dispose of a connected problem without adequate inquiry into the motor–mechanical pathway.

The possible pathway has several causally distinct links: (1) Parkinsonian or dystonic muscular activity influences toe position and gait; (2) sustained or intermittent toe clawing changes plantar and digital contact pressures; (3) repeated loading contributes to localized callus, corn formation or irritation; (4) tissue pathology produces pain, which may feel different from the muscular activity that indirectly helped create it. Reduced prolonged standing may relieve pain at link three or four without altering link one. Thus the patient's differentiated sensations are compatible with an integrated cause, although compatibility does not itself prove that cause.

The neurologist recorded profound rigidity, abnormal gait with a dystonic appearance, pes planus and hammertoes. The later record includes persistent toe curling and variable responses to procyclidine, levodopa and pregabalin ([Documents 4, 12, 19 and 20](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-4)). To establish the actual lesion mechanism would require targeted dynamic motor and foot examination, lesion-location mapping, gait and pressure assessment, medication ON/OFF comparison where appropriate, evaluation of peripheral sensory signs, footwear and structural deformity, and a reasoned multidisciplinary attribution of responsibility. The point is not to prescribe a retrospective test battery as though 2017 expertise guaranteed a single answer, but to identify the *kind of question* an integrated pathway would have owned.

Podiatry can reduce painful pressure without resolving its origin. Neurology can treat movement disorder without deciding why the fifth metatarsal head repeatedly becomes painful. Pregabalin can reduce pain without correcting toe posture. The causally important boundary is therefore not between the disciplines but between treating outcomes and explaining the process producing them. **A divided care pathway is legitimate only if its divisions do not render the causal link nobody's responsibility.**

## Contribution 7 — The 17 July authorisation and responsibility for the completed account

**Question:** What does professional confirmation on 17 July require of a clinical author who, on the patient's account, received a material objection on 10 July? Distinguish the unknown first typing date from the known later confirmation date, and examine how documentation workflow affects duties of review, accuracy, amendment and disclosure.

**Independent contribution:**

### Final signature as epistemic adoption, not simply a timestamp

There are two distinct acts: composing a phrase and adopting a completed account as one's authoritative clinical representation. The first-draft timestamp for the disputed words is not established. The account's confirmation on 17 July follows the 10 July protest on the patient's firsthand chronology; the record also reflects a 13 July addendum concerning completed investigations ([Documents 4, 8 and 38](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-4)). No claim about post-protest insertion of a particular phrase is necessary to examine the duty attaching to later authorisation.

Confirmation gives institutional currency to the account. It communicates to the GP, other clinicians and future reviewers what the responsible specialist stands behind as the completed synthesis. Where the clinician has received a material objection before that act, he has knowledge that may qualify earlier drafting. A previously accurate draft can become materially misleading if a later encounter changes its completeness; an initially inaccurate draft can become a knowing misrepresentation when the author recognises the error yet authorises it unchanged.

The strongest workflow defence is that clinical summaries are iterative, dictated or electronically assembled; not every later discussion appears in a document intended principally to record diagnosis and treatment. The completed wording may have been written before the bedside exchange, and electronic “confirmation” may not represent deliberate rereading of every line. These facts are relevant to mental state. They do not erase professional accountability for a material statement expressly attributed to the patient's own mind when the author had personally received information bearing on the patient's acceptance of the disposition.

The ethical standard is proportional, not perfectionist. A discharge account need not become a transcript. But where the unresolved presenting problem is central, and where a direct objection to its investigation is known, a faithful handover should make clear what was challenged, what response was offered, what remained uncertain and who would act. The actual authorisation and amendment workflow, dictation dates, version history, recipients, final text and whether separate notes preserved the exchange are the relevant tests. **The ethical significance of 17 July is the conscious or professionally accountable adoption of the whole representation after the corrective opportunity, not a claim that every word was first typed then.**

## Contribution 8 — Diagnostic inheritance and the later patient

**Question:** How can an expressly uncertain hypothesis (“Not clear”) become an operational premise through repetition, pathway decisions, prescribing and clinical handover? Trace whether, where and how a neuropathy framework actually influenced later clinical decisions. Examine the significance of procyclidine response, GP re-referral, persistent toe curling, pain relief and later dystonic findings without assuming those observations individually prove one cause.

**Independent contribution:**

### An uncertain proposition can become operationally certain without ever being declared proven

The phrase “Not clear” is an explicit marker of diagnostic uncertainty. Yet uncertainty on paper does not ensure uncertainty survives in practice. A possibility can become a working classification, determine referrals and medication strategy, and be repeated in later clinical language until the original question becomes less visible. The relevant phenomenon is *diagnostic inheritance*: what travels forward is not always the epistemic caution accompanying the first formulation.

The 2017 account places peripheral neuropathy as a possible explanation for burning, corns and calluses while the neurologist treats Parkinsonism and arranges podiatry ([Document 4](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-4)). This supplies a possible path of influence, not proof that later clinicians read and adopted each word. The more exact longitudinal evidence is mixed. In 2018, withdrawal of procyclidine was followed by a return of trouble at the left outer-foot corn site and increased tension ([Document 12](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-12)). In 2019 the GP explicitly related Parkinsonian tone and gait to clawed painful feet ([Document 11](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-11)); the GP therefore did *not* simply inherit a separate-peripheral account. In March 2023 he urgently referred extreme bilateral burning foot/lower-leg pain and recalled pain improving with better Parkinsonism control; the nurse relayed the matter into the neurological pathway ([Document 17](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-17); [knowledge audit](RECIPIENT_AND_KNOWLEDGE_AUDIT.md)). In 2023 the neurologist entertained OFF dystonia ([Document 18](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-18)). In 2024 pregabalin reduced pain while toe curling persisted ([Document 19](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-19)). In 2025 the neurologist acknowledged pain relief with Sinemet while maintaining a substantially non-direct account of Parkinsonian pain and discussing peripheral mechanisms ([Document 20](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-20)).

These are not mutually exclusive clinical facts. Medication response does not specify causal anatomy. But they are *stress tests* of any rigid separation of motor pathology and painful-foot pathology. They call for an explanation of how movement, posture, mechanical loading and pain interrelate, rather than a binary classification of neuropathy versus Parkinson's disease.

The strongest demonstrable long-term proposition is that the uncertain peripheral framework remained clinically available while a fully articulated, owned investigation of the foot–movement–pressure relationship is not identified in the disclosed record. A stronger assertion—that a named later decision occurred *because* the 2017 phrase was read—requires the relevant note, handover, instruction or clinician account. The patient's ongoing pain is real; the precise additional injury caused by documentary inheritance must be tracked decision by decision. **The question is not whether care continued, but whether new evidence was permitted to reopen the original explanatory structure.**

## Contribution 9 — Professional self-validation and institutional review

**Question:** Can care be adequately defended by demonstrating its consistency with a record when the completeness and integrity of that very record are contested? Analyse circular justification, coherence versus correspondence, dependence on the original author's account and the distinction between organisational independence and independent examination of premises.

**Independent contribution:**

### A defence of decisions must not assume the truth of the representation that generated them

The clinical account can perform two jobs: guide future decisions and later serve as evidence that those decisions were justified. The danger is that its dual role obscures a dependence. If a clinician writes that two conditions are separate, treats them on parallel tracks, and later argues that the record of parallel treatment demonstrates appropriate care, the reasoning may prove consistency without proving the separation. The issue is *correspondence* between the account and the patient, not merely *coherence* within the account.

The 25 March 2025 response acknowledged the patient's distress and defended management substantially by reference to the complexity of young-onset Parkinsonism, levodopa risks, dyskinesia and the management of pain ([Document 22](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-22)). Those can be serious and clinically valid considerations. They do not independently answer whether the 2017 referral's foot–neurological relationship was investigated, whether the patient was faithfully represented, or whether a putative peripheral mechanism displaced the need for integrated assessment. One can defend a medication choice and still fail to defend the representational premises by which the presenting problem was divided.

The September 2025 SIMT outcome reported “no deficit” and emphasised clinical notes demonstrating care about early Sinemet and the consideration of alternatives ([Document 31](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-31)). The full PAR, terms of reference and document inventory are not reproduced in the repository. Thus the outcome visibly addresses one question—the defensibility of a recorded treatment strategy—more directly than it demonstrates independent examination of the original attribution and omitted protest. This is an epistemic distinction, not an assertion that unseen reviewers failed to perform an unseen task.

An independent review requires independence of *premises*, not just personnel. It should separately test (a) the factual integrity of the clinician's 2017 account, (b) the adequacy of the clinical disposition of the original referral, and (c) whether later choices were medically sound even on a corrected account. If the same authored document is used both to establish the original patient's position and to defend the author against the challenge to that position, institutional procedure risks magnifying rather than breaking the circle.

**The author may explain his own clinical reasoning, but he cannot make its truth independent by citing his own untested record.** Equally, the record may accurately memorialise that an opinion existed while inaccurately attributing that opinion to the patient. The 2025 Article 16 response emphasises preservation of historical opinions ([Document 33](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-33)); preserving a historical opinion does not settle the accuracy of a claim about another person's expressed understanding.

### Intent is inferred from knowledge, direction, opportunity and action—not from indignation

The deliberate-misrepresentation hypothesis should be given its strongest precise form. The physician knew the original feet and motor findings; his manuscript preserved an experiential distinction; he subsequently authorised a stronger patient-attributed separation beside a peripheral differential; and, on the patient's direct testimony, he had personally received a material protest before confirmation. The completed account did not preserve that protest. If the author understood that the final portrayal conveyed patient agreement with a causal division he knew was not endorsed, the ethical wrong is knowing misattribution, whatever his private motive.

The rival explanations are distinguishable. **Innocent shorthand:** “divorced” meant only the two feelings were unlike; test by asking what the author intended, whether other records preserve the same usage and whether any clinical decision was said to rest on patient belief. **Good-faith peripheral hypothesis:** burning or pressure symptoms raised genuine differential possibilities; test with contemporaneous sensory findings, investigations, reasoning and explicitly retained uncertainty. **Selective but ordinary discharge summary:** many conversations are omitted; test against the materiality of the known objection, related entries on understanding and counselling, separate contemporaneous notes and actual handover. **Template or workflow artifact:** wording was entered before 10 July and electronically confirmed later; test native creation/edit logs, dictation, addenda, who authorised and what authorisation required. **Independent clinical allocation:** podiatry and neurology both had legitimate roles; test whether either service was tasked to evaluate causal interaction, not merely their own compartment.

A sound inference of intent asks how likely the combined record would be under each explanation. Directionality matters when a series of independently describable acts has the same consequence—narrowing the referred problem, overstating patient endorsement, omitting contrary protest and authorising the final account. ## Contribution 11 — Foundational ethical and philosophical framework

**Question:** Develop the strongest independently grounded theory using medical professional integrity, fiduciary responsibility, epistemic injustice, patient testimony, clinical epistemology, autonomy and the ethics of record-making. Explain why knowing misrepresentation could be an ethical wrong independently of proven physical injury, and how consequential harm becomes a further, separately testable question.

**Independent contribution:**

### Professional authority as a conditional epistemic trust

Medicine cannot operate without delegated interpretive authority. Its legitimacy rests on a fiduciary-like ethical relationship: the professional possesses expertise and documentary power because those powers are to be exercised for the person seeking care, not to render the professional's own decisions unchallengeable. This is a moral account of entrusted power, not an assertion of a particular legal fiduciary cause of action. The duties arising include candour about uncertainty, fidelity to testimony, responsiveness to material dissent and preservation of sufficient information for others to reassess decisions.

The philosophy of S. Kay Toombs (*The Meaning of Illness*, 1992) distinguishes the patient's lived body from the physician's object of examination. The two views are not competing descriptions of identical scope. One supplies knowledge of experienced alteration and circumstances; the other supplies causal hypotheses and clinical judgement. Ricoeur's work on narrative identity (*Oneself as Another*, 1992 English translation) further illuminates why a narrative about a person is not a neutral container: it shapes agency, continuity and responsibility. Clinical documentation can become a public-facing institutional identity for the patient, while the patient remains the only one who lives the consequences of its practical decisions.

Miranda Fricker (*Epistemic Injustice: Power and the Ethics of Knowing*, 2007) shows how people may be wronged in their status as knowers. Havi Carel and Ian James Kidd (“Epistemic injustice in healthcare: a philosophical analysis,” 2014) trace forms of such injury in medicine. Here the most exact concept is **epistemic misattribution**: the clinician's inference is represented as the patient's belief. This may interact with testimonial injustice, but one need not prove identity-based prejudice to identify the wrong of knowingly attributing to a patient a conviction the patient did not express. Charon's *Narrative Medicine* (2006) reinforces that clinical competence includes disciplined attention to the patient's account. Annemarie Mol's *The Logic of Care* (2008) helps explain why good care requires ongoing practical responsiveness rather than a single classificatory decision. Donald Schön's *The Reflective Practitioner* (1983) sharpens the duty to revisit the *framing* of a problem, not merely adapt interventions within an unchanged frame.

The Irish Medical Council, *Guide to Professional Conduct and Ethics for Registered Medical Practitioners*, 8th edition (2016), establishes professional norms concerning accurate records, communication and continuity relevant to the 2017 encounter. These standards reflect, but do not exhaust, the moral principle: a clinician cannot claim evidential authority simply by placing a statement in a signed record. Professional authorship creates a duty to the underlying evidence; it does not reverse the burden of truth.

Three layers of justification must remain separate. **Representational:** did the record fairly report observations, patient meaning and uncertainty? **Clinical:** were decisions justified for the *actual* patient on evidence available at the time? **Consequential:** did an inaccurate representation cause an identifiable change in care or injury? A knowingly false patient attribution is an ethical wrong at the first layer even if later treatment happened to be appropriate and no additional injury can be quantified. If the distortion also narrowed the diagnostic problem or redirected care, it becomes an additional practical wrong requiring a decision-specific causal account.

The strongest defence is that medicine must summarise under uncertainty, and expertise legitimately outweighs patient theories about causes. That defence protects clinical disagreement; it does not protect falsifying who held which belief. The strongest institutional defence is that the record accurately preserves a historical clinical opinion. That protects the existence of an opinion; it does not validate a false statement about the patient's mind. **The legitimacy of medical authority depends on its capacity to be corrected by evidence originating outside itself. A record that knowingly substitutes the author's patient for the patient encountered breaks that relation of trust before any injury calculation begins.**
