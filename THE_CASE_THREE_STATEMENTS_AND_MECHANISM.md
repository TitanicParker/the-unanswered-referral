# THE CASE: THE PROTEST, THE ALTERED MEANING, AND THE PERIPHERAL-NEUROPATHY FRAMEWORK

## Controlling ethical proposition — The premature construction of defensibility

**A clinical record earns its authority by faithfully representing an investigation and its unresolved limits. It does not make an incomplete investigation adequate by making the disposition appear defensible.** The patient's central allegation is that, following his 10 July 2017 protest, the clinician knowingly transformed an experiential distinction into apparent patient endorsement of a separate peripheral explanation, omitted the contrary protest and confirmed the completed account on 17 July. On that account, **defensibility was constructed before the care had earned it**.

The institutional obligation was not merely to produce a coherent explanation of why staff acted as they did; it was to investigate and safeguard the patient whose original GP referral connected abnormal movement with painful, clawing feet. Later review could not discharge that obligation by assessing only the treatment delivered within the contested representation. **The record must answer to the patient and the clinical evidence, and the institution's purpose is to protect the patient, not merely the apparent adequacy of its own decisions.**

This proposition identifies the ethical mechanism to be examined. It is not a claim that every medication decision was wrong, that peripheral neuropathy was impossible, or that the exact amount of avoidable physical harm has already been demonstrated. It is a claim about *the order in which clinical responsibility and its justification must arise*: investigation, reasoned disposition and faithful record first; defensibility grounded in that work thereafter.

**Prior knowledge → Direct protest → Deliberate alteration of meaning → Peripheral explanation → Omission of objection → Authorised record → Prolonged clinical consequences → Retrospective defence**

## Statement I — The direct confrontation and the rewritten patient

The GP referred a patient whose painful, clawing feet, abnormal gait, increased tone and developing neurological dysfunction required investigation together. The hospital's opening summary compressed that presentation, and the Neurology Department's complex-case meeting took place at 8:30 a.m. on 4 July and established the Parkinsonism treatment direction, which the neurologist communicated to the patient in person immediately afterwards.. On 10 July, the eighth day of admission, the patient confronted the treating neurologist about the failure to study his feet. The neurologist's answer was that they were going to podiatry. The patient was not introducing a new complaint: he was returning the substance of the original referral to the clinician responsible for answering it.

The completed clinical account did not preserve that confrontation. Instead, it turned the patient's distinction between *foot pain* and *muscular tension* into a stronger attribution that he had, **“in his own mind - markedly divorced”** the problems. That attribution sat beside the clinician's tentative explanation that the painful feet might be separate or represent **secondary peripheral neuropathy**. The patient's own discernment was made to serve a clinical division he had not endorsed.

**The case is that, faced with a direct challenge to the adequacy of the admission, the neurologist deliberately altered the patient's meaning, reinforced a peripheral explanation, omitted the protest and authorised a clinical account that protected the established disposition rather than answering the referral.**

## Statement II — The manufacture of apparent clinical support

The clinical question was whether the painful foot deformity, toe clawing and recurrent pressure pathology were connected with the movement disorder, including its dystonic component. A patient can distinguish the sensation of pressure-related foot pain from muscular tension without deciding that the underlying causes are medically unrelated. Such a distinction may be fully consistent with dystonic muscle activity producing abnormal toe posture, altered loading, corns, calluses and pain. The GP's referral required precisely that relationship to be examined.

The written sequence moved in the opposite direction. The original referral presented the problem together; the opening summary narrowed it; the handwritten interview recorded different experiences; and the completed impression attributed an emphatic mental separation to the patient while entertaining peripheral neuropathy. The neurologist also used burning and leg-pain descriptions within the peripheral explanatory framework, whose precise provenance must be examined against the original notes. The completed account retained the clinician's speculation but not the patient's 10 July objection that his feet had not been adequately investigated.

**The case is that the neurologist converted firsthand symptom description into apparent patient endorsement of the neurologist's own causal hypothesis. The record thereby acquired support for a peripheral-neuropathy framework that the original interview did not supply, while the protest that challenged the separation disappeared from the authorised clinical history.**

## Statement III — The patient who carried the consequences

The significance of the 2017 record extended beyond discharge. Once the painful feet were framed as potentially peripheral to the movement disorder, the patient could continue receiving treatment for Parkinsonism and local treatment for painful lesions without the integrated neurological–mechanical explanation being resolved. The peripheral-neuropathy hypothesis was recorded as uncertain, not established as a proven diagnosis. Yet its presence supplied a durable explanatory route by which the feet could be considered apart from dystonic toe movements, abnormal posture and pressure-related pain.

The actual patient carried the ongoing symptoms: recurrent painful foot pathology, abnormal toe curling, impaired mobility and the continuing need for assessment and treatment. Later evidence of dystonic phenomena and persistent abnormal foot posture makes the original neurological contribution an essential question, not a question disposed of by a speculative neuropathy label. When the clinical history was subsequently defended, the recorded diagnosis, treatment and follow-up could appear coherent without demonstrating that the GP's original foot question had ever been answered.

**The case is that the neurologist knowingly preserved a separation of the patient's problems through a strengthened attribution of the patient's own words, omission of the direct protest and post-protest authorisation of the record. That representation helped sustain a peripheral explanation while the potential dystonic mechanism remained unresolved, and it later supplied the terms on which the adequacy of care was defended.**

## Contribution 1 — The authority to interpret versus the obligation to represent

**Question:** At what point does legitimate clinical interpretation become wrongful attribution of an interpretation to the patient? Distinguish what the patient felt, what the physician inferred, and what the patient was said to believe. Examine professional control over wording and the unequal institutional authority of the completed record.

**Independent contribution:**

### The distinction that makes authority legitimate

Clinical expertise confers authority over medical *interpretation*, not ownership of what another person experienced, asserted or understood. In a consultation the patient reports a lived event, the physician examines a body, and the physician develops an explanation. These are three different evidential acts. The ethical legitimacy of the record depends on leaving their origins distinguishable. The patient may be wrong about disease mechanism, but the clinician cannot correct an alleged diagnostic error by attributing a different belief to the patient. An accurate statement that the doctor thought the conditions separate cannot substitute for an inaccurate statement that the *patient* thought so.

The 3 July manuscript says that the patient began to appreciate foot pain and leg tension “weren't the same” as the pain diminished after less prolonged standing ([Document 7](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-7)). This is a report about experienced difference and divergent symptom courses. It supplies no necessary conclusion about independent causes. The completed account adds a second-order claim about the patient's thought: he had “in his own mind - markedly divorced” the two problems, next to “may have been completely separate or perhaps a secondary peripheral neuropathy” and “Not clear” ([Document 4](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-4)). The inference is transformed into an attribution. The point is not that the patient never distinguished the sensations; the manuscript shows that he did. The point is the *increment in attributed meaning* and its proximity to a causal hypothesis.

The most charitable reading is that “markedly divorced” means only that the patient's experiences seemed different to him. Ordinary clinical shorthand often compresses those distinctions. But if that is its intended sense, it cannot independently support the separate-neuropathy explanation: it adds no causal evidence beyond the original experiential report. If instead it implies that the patient regarded the underlying conditions as causally distinct, it requires an evidential basis the quoted manuscript does not supply. The clinician cannot obtain independent corroboration in either direction merely by interpreting his own summary generously.

The physician controls what becomes the durable record. The patient does not ordinarily control its technical language, transmission or later institutional standing. This asymmetry makes fidelity a substantive duty, not a matter of stylistic preference. S. Kay Toombs's *The Meaning of Illness* (1992) helps explain why experiential knowledge and clinical explanation are different perspectives; Rita Charon's *Narrative Medicine* (2006) explains why attending to the patient's narrative is part of clinical competence. Their implications converge here: professional authorship may organise the patient's narrative, but it must not annex the patient's voice. **The authority to interpret ends where a clinician's inference is knowingly passed off as the patient's independently held understanding.**

## Contribution 2 — The original referral and the ethics of answering the actual question

**Question:** What obligation does a specialist admission owe to the substantive clinical question in the referring GP's letter? Explore the difference between successfully diagnosing Parkinsonism and adequately investigating its possible relationship to abnormal toe posture, tissue loading and foot pain. Ask whether a referral can be *functionally unanswered* despite extensive clinical activity.

**Independent contribution:**

### The referral defines a problem, not merely a diagnostic destination

A specialist admission owes the referral a reasoned *disposition* of its material questions. It does not owe a guaranteed diagnosis or every investigation requested, and the neurologist may legitimately prioritise a serious movement disorder. It does owe intelligible consideration of the clinical relationship that motivated referral, including a decision about what remains uncertain, who will examine it and how the answer will reach the patient and GP. A correct diagnosis of Parkinsonism is not logically equivalent to answering whether abnormal muscular activity produced toe clawing, altered pressure and painful lesions.

The GP's 29 June letter describes bilateral foot problems, corns and calluses, stiff/festinant gait, marked tone and toes clawing when the patient sought comfort in bed ([Document 2](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-2)). Earlier GP notes likewise join gait, feet and muscle tension ([Document 1](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-1)). The hospital's opening précis retains movement initiation, stiffness, tone and cogwheeling but not the explicit painful-foot and clawing components ([Document 4](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-4)). This is not proof that no doctor knew of the feet—the neurologist later described them—but it is a change in the *question made salient* at the entrance to specialist reasoning.

The philosophical danger is **answer substitution**: the team may answer “What movement disorder is present?” and allow the answer to stand in for “What accounts for the joint presentation of disordered movement and painful deformed feet?” An elaborate admission can be diagnostically successful in one dimension and explanatory incomplete in another. A referral can therefore be *functionally unanswered* despite examinations, imaging, medication and referral onward. The decisive criterion is not the amount of work done but whether the initiating clinical problem received a reasoned answer or an explicit, owned plan for resolving it.

The case becomes more exact if framed in terms of responsibility rather than assuming every foot abnormality had to be fully explained as an inpatient. Podiatry may treat a callus; neurology may identify dystonic posture; the crucial integrative question is whether someone was responsible for their possible causal interaction. A referral to another service is adequate only to the extent that it addresses the question referred or expressly transfers the unresolved question with sufficient information and responsibility. **A pathway that treats each manifestation while leaving the mechanism between them unowned can look complete precisely because everyone has performed a task.**

## Contribution 3 — The 10 July confrontation as knowledge, notice and corrective opportunity

**Question:** What changed ethically and epistemically when the patient personally confronted the neurologist about his feet? Examine this as a direct challenge to the adequacy of an admission already shaped by a complex-case discussion, not merely another symptom report. Explain why the absence of the protest from the hospital record is not a logical disproof of the encounter.

**Independent contribution:**

### Notice of a failure is not the same epistemic event as reporting a symptom

The 10 July bedside encounter, taken as the patient's firsthand account states, was a direct challenge to the sufficiency of the admission. The patient had already supplied the foot history. He now confronted the responsible neurologist because discharge approached without, in his understanding, an adequate investigation of the feet. His “full NCT” and “bald tyres” analogy was an argument about the mismatch between an extensive general examination and a still-unanswered presenting problem, not an invitation simply to add a further symptom to the chart ([Document 38](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-38)).

The ethical transformation is from *possible oversight* to *received notice*. The clinician now knew that the patient disputed whether the actual reason for referral had been answered. That knowledge does not prove the patient's preferred investigation was required. It does create distinct obligations: to understand the challenge, reconsider whether the current explanation and plan adequately cover the feet, explain the disposition, and preserve materially significant disagreement and responsibility for follow-up. The clinician might have answered that neurologic causes had been considered and podiatry would assess mechanical pathology; a faithful account could have recorded that reasoning and the patient's objection. Directing the patient to podiatry, by itself, did not answer whether neurologically abnormal posture was generating the pathology requiring podiatry.

An objection about investigation sufficiency is *meta-clinical evidence*: it concerns the adequacy of the clinical process as applied to this patient. Its importance lies partly in the fact that it may reopen the definition of the problem. A doctor can disagree with it, but must not knowingly convert the record into a picture of tacit patient endorsement.

The protest's absence from the identified completed narrative does not logically disprove the encounter. A record cannot be treated as a perfect census of events when the complaint specifically concerns its selectivity. The patient's signed statement is affirmative firsthand testimony; the four students, ward records and earlier 2025 accounts are potential additional evidential routes, not preconditions for analysing what the encounter means. On the premise that it occurred, the subsequent 17 July authorisation raises a different question: why was a material challenge known before completion not preserved or substantively addressed in the authoritative synthesis? **The protest was an opportunity to correct the clinical problem-definition before the hospital's version became final.**

### The epistemic consequences of an early narrowed problem representation

A complex-case meeting can reason expertly from the information it receives and still reach a disposition insufficiently responsive to the full referral. Collective expertise does not cancel the epistemological effects of case framing. If the 4 July at 8:30 a.m. discussion worked principally from the compressed hospital précis, its participants would have confronted a problem accentuating initiation difficulty, rigidity and Parkinsonism rather than the full question about painful clawed feet, abnormal loading and neurological tone. This is an inquiry into *information architecture*. A summary determines what is cognitively available, salient and assigned responsibility. The ethical burden is not that meetings must reproduce every referral sentence. It is that the summary must preserve features whose omission could alter the decision or obscure an unresolved task. A multidisciplinary conclusion is only as responsive as the problem it has actually deliberated. A clinician with fuller firsthand knowledge bears an additional responsibility not to let a narrowed team summary substitute for that knowledge.

The reported 10 July protest then became a direct challenge to a disposition already formed. A professional response would reconsider whether the team's conclusion depended on an incomplete framing and, if necessary, return the question to an accountable clinical process.

The decisive documentary tests are the meeting agenda and pack, original referral in the inpatient chart, presentation notes, attendance, recorded differential diagnoses, podiatry instructions and discharge responsibilities. A strong opposing possibility is that the full referral and foot findings were discussed and a reasoned separation was adopted. Even then, that reason would have to be identified; the existence of a meeting alone does not supply it. ## Contribution 5 — The manufacture of apparent corroboration

**Question:** How could the written phrase **“in his own mind - markedly divorced”**, alongside a speculative peripheral-neuropathy explanation, make one clinician-authored inference look like two convergent sources of support? Examine the philosophical concepts of testimonial appropriation, evidential laundering and manufactured corroboration, distinguishing analytical metaphors from established professional or legal terms.

**Independent contribution:**

### A single source cannot corroborate itself by changing grammatical ownership

The three documentary stages should be treated as a chain of transformation, not three independent witnesses. Stage one: the patient distinguishes what foot pain feels like from what leg tension feels like, particularly after prolonged standing diminishes. Stage two: the typed history expresses those sensations as different “problems.” Stage three: the completed impression says the patient has “in his own mind - markedly divorced” them, immediately after the author's conjecture that the pain and corns/calluses “may have been completely separate or perhaps a secondary peripheral neuropathy” and the qualification “Not clear” ([Documents 7 and 4](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-7)). Each stage has the same informational origin unless an independent source of patient endorsement can be shown.

The fallacy is a shift in *source attribution*. A physician may infer a causal separation from symptom patterns. But if the inference is then phrased as the patient's emphatic belief, a reader may count professional judgment and patient understanding as converging evidence. That is manufactured *apparent* corroboration: not the creation of an additional observation, but the presentation of one interpretive move as though a second witness supports it.

“Evidential laundering” and “testimonial appropriation” are analytical descriptions of the mechanism, not formal clinical diagnoses or findings of dishonesty. They capture two losses. Provenance is lost when a clinician's inference is made to appear the patient's independent conclusion. Contestability is lost when the patient must dispute an official account that claims to speak for his own mind. Miranda Fricker's *Epistemic Injustice* (2007) explains how people may be wronged as knowers; Carel and Kidd, “Epistemic injustice in healthcare: a philosophical analysis” (*Medicine, Health Care and Philosophy*, 2014), show the heightened clinical relevance of professional credibility asymmetries. Strict testimonial injustice in Fricker's sense additionally involves identity prejudice; this case's mechanism can be wrongful misattribution without proving that extra element.

A charitable contrary reading holds that the phrase describes only subjective divergence, not etiological endorsement. That reading has a decisive consequence: the phrase then supplies *no independent evidence* for peripheral neuropathy. The stronger accusation holds where the clinician understood the statement would communicate causal patient endorsement and knowingly let it do so. **The more evidential work the patient-attributed phrase is asked to perform, the more important its fidelity becomes.**

## Contribution 6 — Peripheral neuropathy, dystonia and the separation of clinical responsibilities

**Question:** How might a provisional peripheral-neuropathy explanation and referral to podiatry allow neurological investigation, lesion treatment and pain control to proceed in parallel while the causal link between dystonic activity, toe posture, altered pressure and recurrent lesions remains unresolved? Distinguish the **hypothesised dystonic–mechanical pathway** from a subsequently established diagnosis and examine the evidence needed to test each link.

**Independent contribution:**

### Competing mechanisms and the hidden division between treating lesions and explaining their production

Peripheral neuropathy and a neurological–mechanical mechanism are not necessarily exclusive. Neuropathy can produce burning or altered sensation; structural factors can produce corns; abnormal motor activity can alter posture and loading; mixed mechanisms are possible. The 2017 completed impression tentatively suggested separate pathology or peripheral neuropathy and expressly said “Not clear” ([Document 4](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-4)). The error examined here is not the mere inclusion of a peripheral differential. It is allowing an unproved peripheral account to dispose of a connected problem without adequate inquiry into the motor–mechanical pathway.

The possible pathway has several causally distinct links: (1) Parkinsonian or dystonic muscular activity influences toe position and gait; (2) sustained or intermittent toe clawing changes plantar and digital contact pressures; (3) repeated loading contributes to localized callus, corn formation or irritation; (4) tissue pathology produces pain, which may feel different from the muscular activity that indirectly helped create it. Reduced prolonged standing may relieve pain at link three or four without altering link one. Thus the patient's differentiated sensations are compatible with an integrated cause, although compatibility does not itself prove that cause.

The neurologist recorded profound rigidity, abnormal gait with a dystonic appearance, pes planus and hammertoes. The later record includes persistent toe curling and variable responses to procyclidine, levodopa and pregabalin ([Documents 4, 12, 19 and 20](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-4)). To establish the actual lesion mechanism would require targeted dynamic motor and foot examination, lesion-location mapping, gait and pressure assessment, medication ON/OFF comparison where appropriate, evaluation of peripheral sensory signs, footwear and structural deformity, and a reasoned multidisciplinary attribution of responsibility. The point is not to prescribe a retrospective test battery as though 2017 expertise guaranteed a single answer, but to identify the *kind of question* an integrated pathway would have owned.

Podiatry can reduce painful pressure without resolving its origin. Neurology can treat movement disorder without deciding why the fifth metatarsal head repeatedly becomes painful. Pregabalin can reduce pain without correcting toe posture. The causally important boundary is therefore not between the disciplines but between treating outcomes and explaining the process producing them. **A divided care pathway is legitimate only if its divisions do not render the causal link nobody's responsibility.**

## Contribution 7 — The 17 July authorisation and responsibility for the completed account

**Question:** What does professional confirmation on 17 July require of a clinical author who, on the patient's account, received a material objection on 10 July? Distinguish the unknown first typing date from the known later confirmation date, and examine how documentation workflow affects duties of review, accuracy, amendment and disclosure.

**Independent contribution:**

### Final signature as epistemic adoption, not simply a timestamp

There are two distinct acts: composing a phrase and adopting a completed account as one's authoritative clinical representation. The first-draft timestamp for the disputed words is not established. The account's confirmation on 17 July follows the 10 July protest on the patient's firsthand chronology; the record also reflects a 13 July addendum concerning completed investigations ([Documents 4, 8 and 38](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-4)). No claim about post-protest insertion of a particular phrase is necessary to examine the duty attaching to later authorisation.

Confirmation gives institutional currency to the account. It communicates to the GP, other clinicians and future reviewers what the responsible specialist stands behind as the completed synthesis. Where the clinician has received a material objection before that act, he has knowledge that may qualify earlier drafting. A previously accurate draft can become materially misleading if a later encounter changes its completeness; an initially inaccurate draft can become a knowing misrepresentation when the author recognises the error yet authorises it unchanged.

The strongest workflow defence is that clinical summaries are iterative, dictated or electronically assembled; not every later discussion appears in a document intended principally to record diagnosis and treatment. The completed wording may have been written before the bedside exchange, and electronic “confirmation” may not represent deliberate rereading of every line. These facts are relevant to mental state. They do not erase professional accountability for a material statement expressly attributed to the patient's own mind when the author had personally received information bearing on the patient's acceptance of the disposition.

The ethical standard is proportional, not perfectionist. A discharge account need not become a transcript. But where the unresolved presenting problem is central, and where a direct objection to its investigation is known, a faithful handover should make clear what was challenged, what response was offered, what remained uncertain and who would act. The actual authorisation and amendment workflow, dictation dates, version history, recipients, final text and whether separate notes preserved the exchange are the relevant tests. **The ethical significance of 17 July is the conscious or professionally accountable adoption of the whole representation after the corrective opportunity, not a claim that every word was first typed then.**

## Contribution 8 — Diagnostic inheritance and the later patient

**Question:** How can an expressly uncertain hypothesis (“Not clear”) become an operational premise through repetition, pathway decisions, prescribing and clinical handover? Trace whether, where and how a neuropathy framework actually influenced later clinical decisions. Examine the significance of procyclidine response, GP re-referral, persistent toe curling, pain relief and later dystonic findings without assuming those observations individually prove one cause.

**Independent contribution:**

### An uncertain proposition can become operationally certain without ever being declared proven

The phrase “Not clear” is an explicit marker of diagnostic uncertainty. Yet uncertainty on paper does not ensure uncertainty survives in practice. A possibility can become a working classification, determine referrals and medication strategy, and be repeated in later clinical language until the original question becomes less visible. The relevant phenomenon is *diagnostic inheritance*: what travels forward is not always the epistemic caution accompanying the first formulation.

The 2017 account places peripheral neuropathy as a possible explanation for burning, corns and calluses while the neurologist treats Parkinsonism and arranges podiatry ([Document 4](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-4)). This supplies a possible path of influence, not proof that later clinicians read and adopted each word. The more exact longitudinal evidence is mixed. In 2018, withdrawal of procyclidine was followed by a return of trouble at the left outer-foot corn site and increased tension ([Document 12](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-12)). In 2019 the GP explicitly related Parkinsonian tone and gait to clawed painful feet ([Document 11](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-11)); the GP therefore did *not* simply inherit a separate-peripheral account. In March 2023 he urgently referred extreme bilateral burning foot/lower-leg pain and recalled pain improving with better Parkinsonism control; the nurse relayed the matter into the neurological pathway ([Document 17](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-17); [knowledge audit](RECIPIENT_AND_KNOWLEDGE_AUDIT.md)). In 2023 the neurologist entertained OFF dystonia ([Document 18](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-18)). In 2024 pregabalin reduced pain while toe curling persisted ([Document 19](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-19)). In 2025 the neurologist acknowledged pain relief with Sinemet while maintaining a substantially non-direct account of Parkinsonian pain and discussing peripheral mechanisms ([Document 20](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-20)).

These are not mutually exclusive clinical facts. Medication response does not specify causal anatomy. But they are *stress tests* of any rigid separation of motor pathology and painful-foot pathology. They call for an explanation of how movement, posture, mechanical loading and pain interrelate, rather than a binary classification of neuropathy versus Parkinson's disease.

The strongest demonstrable long-term proposition is that the uncertain peripheral framework remained clinically available while a fully articulated, owned investigation of the foot–movement–pressure relationship is not identified in the disclosed record. A stronger assertion—that a named later decision occurred *because* the 2017 phrase was read—requires the relevant note, handover, instruction or clinician account. The patient's ongoing pain is real; the precise additional injury caused by documentary inheritance must be tracked decision by decision. **The question is not whether care continued, but whether new evidence was permitted to reopen the original explanatory structure.**

## Contribution 9 — Professional self-validation and institutional review

**Question:** Can care be adequately defended by demonstrating its consistency with a record when the completeness and integrity of that very record are contested? Analyse circular justification, coherence versus correspondence, dependence on the original author's account and the distinction between organisational independence and independent examination of premises.

**Independent contribution:**

### A defence of decisions must not assume the truth of the representation that generated them

The clinical account can perform two jobs: guide future decisions and later serve as evidence that those decisions were justified. The danger is that its dual role obscures a dependence. If a clinician writes that two conditions are separate, treats them on parallel tracks, and later argues that the record of parallel treatment demonstrates appropriate care, the reasoning may prove consistency without proving the separation. The issue is *correspondence* between the account and the patient, not merely *coherence* within the account.

## Contribution 11 — Foundational ethical and philosophical framework

**Question:** Develop the strongest independently grounded theory using medical professional integrity, fiduciary responsibility, epistemic injustice, patient testimony, clinical epistemology, autonomy and the ethics of record-making. Explain why knowing misrepresentation could be an ethical wrong independently of proven physical injury, and how consequential harm becomes a further, separately testable question.

**Independent contribution:**

### Professional authority as a conditional epistemic trust

Medicine cannot operate without delegated interpretive authority. Its legitimacy rests on a fiduciary-like ethical relationship: the professional possesses expertise and documentary power because those powers are to be exercised for the person seeking care, not to render the professional's own decisions unchallengeable. This is a moral account of entrusted power, not an assertion of a particular legal fiduciary cause of action. The duties arising include candour about uncertainty, fidelity to testimony, responsiveness to material dissent and preservation of sufficient information for others to reassess decisions.

The philosophy of S. Kay Toombs (*The Meaning of Illness*, 1992) distinguishes the patient's lived body from the physician's object of examination. The two views are not competing descriptions of identical scope. One supplies knowledge of experienced alteration and circumstances; the other supplies causal hypotheses and clinical judgement. Ricoeur's work on narrative identity (*Oneself as Another*, 1992 English translation) further illuminates why a narrative about a person is not a neutral container: it shapes agency, continuity and responsibility. Clinical documentation can become a public-facing institutional identity for the patient, while the patient remains the only one who lives the consequences of its practical decisions.

Miranda Fricker (*Epistemic Injustice: Power and the Ethics of Knowing*, 2007) shows how people may be wronged in their status as knowers. Havi Carel and Ian James Kidd (“Epistemic injustice in healthcare: a philosophical analysis,” 2014) trace forms of such injury in medicine. Here the most exact concept is **epistemic misattribution**: the clinician's inference is represented as the patient's belief. This may interact with testimonial injustice, but one need not prove identity-based prejudice to identify the wrong of knowingly attributing to a patient a conviction the patient did not express. Charon's *Narrative Medicine* (2006) reinforces that clinical competence includes disciplined attention to the patient's account. Annemarie Mol's *The Logic of Care* (2008) helps explain why good care requires ongoing practical responsiveness rather than a single classificatory decision. Donald Schön's *The Reflective Practitioner* (1983) sharpens the duty to revisit the *framing* of a problem, not merely adapt interventions within an unchanged frame.

The Irish Medical Council, *Guide to Professional Conduct and Ethics for Registered Medical Practitioners*, 8th edition (2016), establishes professional norms concerning accurate records, communication and continuity relevant to the 2017 encounter. These standards reflect, but do not exhaust, the moral principle: a clinician cannot claim evidential authority simply by placing a statement in a signed record. Professional authorship creates a duty to the underlying evidence; it does not reverse the burden of truth.

Three layers of justification must remain separate. **Representational:** did the record fairly report observations, patient meaning and uncertainty? **Clinical:** were decisions justified for the *actual* patient on evidence available at the time? **Consequential:** did an inaccurate representation cause an identifiable change in care or injury? A knowingly false patient attribution is an ethical wrong at the first layer even if later treatment happened to be appropriate and no additional injury can be quantified. If the distortion also narrowed the diagnostic problem or redirected care, it becomes an additional practical wrong requiring a decision-specific causal account.

The strongest defence is that medicine must summarise under uncertainty, and expertise legitimately outweighs patient theories about causes. That defence protects clinical disagreement; it does not protect falsifying who held which belief. The strongest institutional defence is that the record accurately preserves a historical clinical opinion. That protects the existence of an opinion; it does not validate a false statement about the patient's mind. **The legitimacy of medical authority depends on its capacity to be corrected by evidence originating outside itself. A record that knowingly substitutes the author's patient for the patient encountered breaks that relation of trust before any injury calculation begins.**

## Contribution 14 — The institutional information failure: from compression to the substitution of an answer

### The hospital's responsibility is defined by the question it received

The twenty formulations supplied on 18 November 2026 bring a structural insight into view. The hospital was not merely an intermediary that supplied premises for an individual doctor's diagnosis. It received a clinical question. The GP's referral linked painful and clawing feet with increased tone, gait disturbance and developing neurological dysfunction ([Documents 1–2](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-2)). The hospital's opening précis did not retain the explicit painful-foot and clawing detail ([Document 4](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-4)). The resulting clinical question visible at the point of institutional entry was narrower than the one transmitted. This is a loss of *problem fidelity*.

A health institution owes more than accurate copying. It must preserve enough of the reason for referral to enable its specialists to undertake and document an adequate disposition of the referred problem. The relevant form of fidelity is *functional*: what aspects of the referral remained capable of influencing the investigation and the subsequent assignment of responsibility? The danger is not simply that missing words give a sparse narrative. It is that the narrower presentation can determine the boundaries of what the institution understands itself to have undertaken.

The neurologist's later direct examination prevents the hospital compression from becoming a complete explanation of his knowledge. He recorded foot pathology and dystonic-looking gait. The institutional and individual duties therefore converged: the hospital had received a joined-up question, and the physician had independently acquired facts making the relationship clinically salient. The senior meeting at 8:30 a.m. on 4 July 2017, and the patient's report of the subsequent communication about levodopa, show a movement-disorder management direction had become a live treatment commitment. Whatever its information pack contained, that treatment commitment was not logically an answer to whether dystonic toe posture produced altered loading and painful lesions.

The philosophical defect is **substitution of the object of inquiry**. One question—what neurological movement disorder is present?—was treated in depth. Another—what relationship exists between that disorder and the painful, clawing feet?—could be left under a peripheral and podiatric disposition. Both activities could be legitimate in isolation, yet the institution could still fail its responsibility to explain or allocate the relationship that justified the original referral. The ethical wrong becomes more serious when an authority charged with answering question A achieves and documents a defensible answer to question B, then subsequently presents its work on B as though it discharged the duty arising from A.

### The 10 July protest as decompression and corrective notice

The patient's 10 July confrontation was an act of *decompression*: he restored, orally and directly, the significance of the foot problem whose prominence had been lost in the hospital's initial account. He was not merely expressing dissatisfaction. He was telling the responsible clinician that, despite the extensive admission, a substantial element of the original referral remained unanswered. The reported answer about podiatry addressed a service route for foot care, not the neurological mechanism connecting toe clawing, gait and focal pressure pathology ([Document 38](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-38)).

The distinction between a failure that could have arisen before complete information reached a clinician and a failure that persisted after direct notice is ethically decisive. The initial compression may explain how the problem was initially framed; after the protest, the doctor knew the patient expressly contested the resulting disposition. The patient's intervention restored the missing question to the clinician's attention. This generated an opportunity to reopen the reasoning, record an explanation for maintaining the decision, and identify responsibility for unresolved foot–movement causation.

A further, original insight follows: the protest put the institution's **information chain itself** in issue. Had the admission answered the question transmitted by the GP, or the narrower problem preserved in the hospital's opening representation? The patient's bedside objection was a challenge to both the adequacy of clinical investigation and the adequacy of clinical information preservation. A faithful final account could have exposed that mismatch and made it corrigible. A knowingly misleading final account could conceal it.

### From institutional omission to patient-attributed separation

The two documentary transformations are not redundant. The opening hospital précis narrowed the *problem conveyed*. The completed impression altered the *epistemic ownership of the explanation*. The first made the painful-foot question less salient; the second attributed a stronger division to the patient himself. The neurologist's tentative neuropathy possibility—“Not clear”—remained medically provisional, but its placement beside the assertion that the patient had “in his own mind - markedly divorced” the problems could make the separation appear jointly supported by clinician and patient ([Documents 4 and 7](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-4)).

This is more than ordinary diagnostic anchoring. It is a possible transfer of apparent responsibility for the clinical division. The record no longer makes separate disposition appear to arise only from a neurologist's uncertain hypothesis. It can appear to follow, in part, from the patient's own understanding of his problems. On the patient's account of deliberate alteration, that transfer was knowingly false.

At the same time, the identified completed account does not preserve the 10 July objection that directly challenged the adequacy of separate disposal. The positive patient-attributed statement remains; the negative patient-originating challenge disappears. This is an asymmetry in *whose testimony was permitted to acquire documentary authority*. The physician's preferred use of the patient's account was preserved while the patient's challenge to the physician's approach was excluded.

The mechanism therefore has three levels: information compression before the decision, testimonial appropriation during the account's construction, and retrospective defence after the account acquired authority. **The institution received one problem; an authoritative record made a narrower disposition appear clinically and personally legitimate; later practice could inherit the resulting answer without repairing the original question.**

### The second-order accountability failure

In the latter case organisational procedure can reproduce rather than correct epistemic error.

This yields a strong governing principle: **an institution may not vindicate the discharge of an obligation by defending its performance on a materially different task that emerged from its own incomplete representation of the original obligation.** The review's legitimacy depends not solely on its authorship or formality but on its responsiveness to the original task and disputed evidential foundations.

The full PAR and review materials would establish precisely what was or was not tested. But the normative question does not wait upon access to those papers. If the reviewing institution accepted the 2017 account as the authoritative baseline while the allegation concerned its knowing distortion, it would have treated the contested conclusion as its own premise. That is circular review, not independent adjudication of record integrity.

## Contribution 16 — Governing ethical synthesis: the authority to answer cannot become the authority to redefine the question

The full mechanism now extends across two distinct acts of professional formation and two opportunities for correction. **First**, the GP's integrated question was compressed into a narrower hospital presentation. **Second**, the patient personally restored the omitted foot problem to the neurologist on 10 July, when a movement-disorder management direction was already established. **Third**, the completed account set an uncertain peripheral hypothesis beside an emphatic separation attributed to the patient, while the protest was absent. **Fourth**, the account was confirmed on 17 July after that corrective encounter. **Fifth**, the record could operate as an inherited framework in which Parkinsonism, pain and pressure lesions were treated in parallel while their possible neurological–mechanical relationship remained unowned. **Sixth**, later defence and review risked judging care within the resulting record rather than asking whether it faithfully represented the original patient.

The most important ethical distinction is between *disposition* and *answer*. A clinician may appropriately allocate unresolved work to other specialists. What he may not do is knowingly create the appearance that an unresolved question was substantively answered, or that the patient endorsed the separation, when the patient directly objected to the investigation's sufficiency. Clinical authority legitimates interpretation and responsible delegation, not the manufacture of patient agreement or the conversion of a provisional hypothesis into retrospectively self-validating evidence.

The other important distinction is between *the record's capacity to explain past decisions* and *its authority to justify those decisions*. A misleading record may explain why later clinicians acted on an inherited separation. It cannot independently prove that they were right to do so. Nor can the long treatment history justify the record's fidelity merely because the care followed the framework it transmitted. The patient remains the actual object of professional responsibility even when his recorded counterpart is more coherent, more compliant or more compatible with established management pathways.

The account of knowing distortion establishes a moral wrong independently of whether a different investigation in 2017 would have prevented later painful lesions. Causation of additional physical harm is a further question; the injury to faithful representation and professional answerability occurs at the act of knowing misattribution and authorisation. An institution that later defends the care without independently testing the original representation risks compounding that injury by allowing its own records to define the limits of what may be challenged.

**Revised governing proposition:** The hospital was entrusted with the neurological question the GP actually referred. The patient restored its missing foot component directly to the specialist before discharge. Clinical authority could legitimately answer, qualify or responsibly allocate that question; it could not knowingly displace it by attributing an unproved causal separation to the patient's understanding, omitting his objection and authorising an account that subsequently became the basis for professional and institutional self-defence. **An institution cannot satisfy an obligation by constructing, treating and defending a narrower version of the problem entrusted to it.**



## Contribution 17 — Premature defensibility: the record as a substitute for the work it purports to justify

### The difference between justification, defensibility and safeguarding

Clinical *justification* is an evidential relationship: reasons drawn from examination, history, relevant uncertainty and professional judgment support an action toward a real person. *Defensibility* is a second-order relationship: reasons can be articulated to sustain that action before another reader or reviewer. The concepts can coincide, and defensibility is often a proper consequence of responsible medical practice. But their order matters. The reasons must answer to the evidence and clinical responsibility before they can legitimately justify what the record says was done. A narrative does not acquire correspondence with the patient by becoming internally coherent.

The phrase **premature construction of defensibility** identifies the reversal of that order. A clinician has an unresolved problem and an existing disposition. Instead of addressing the remaining gap and documenting its limits, he produces an account whose phrasing, attribution and selectivity make the disposition appear adequately supported. The record is no longer merely a retrospective account of professional reasoning or a prospective handover. It also operates as a shield for the reasoning it claims to describe.

A discharge summary is particularly capable of this reversal because it closes one institutional episode and frames the next. Its summaries of patient understanding, diagnosis, advice and follow-up become the terms in which later clinicians encounter the case. In principle, a summary should preserve the explanatory achievements *and* the outstanding questions of admission. If the author knowingly presents an unfinished causal question as a sufficiently separated peripheral problem, the document can grant the practical status of closure to a question that remained open. The professional signature confers authority on the narrative; it does not supply the missing clinical reasoning.

The fundamental distinction is between **care that is defensible because it was responsibly investigated and represented**, and **care that appears responsible because its author constructed a defensible representation**. The first is the ordinary legitimacy of clinical documentation. The second is the alleged misuse of documentation in this case.

### Why the 10 July confrontation changes the ethical character of the account

The GP's 29 June referral made painful, clawing feet part of a neurological presentation ([Document 2](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-2)). The admission proceeded to a Parkinsonism management direction following the 4 July complex-case meeting; the patient's account places communication of the decision about levodopa immediately after that discussion. The treating neurologist independently documented foot deformities and a dystonic-looking gait ([Document 4](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-4)). On 10 July the patient directly challenged why the hospital was nearing discharge without adequate investigation of the feet ([Document 38](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-38)). The original referral's unresolved connection between movement, foot posture, pressure and pain had returned personally to the decision-maker.

That confrontation was not solely a request for another service. It was a demand for clinical *answerability*: had the hospital answered the problem it had been asked to examine? The neurologist's reported response about podiatry offered a pathway for managing painful pressure lesions. It did not, without further reasoning, establish the causal role or irrelevance of neurologically abnormal toe posturing. The patient was therefore identifying a potential failure not just in symptom treatment but in the definition of what counted as having completed the admission.

On the allegation of deliberate misrepresentation, the subsequent account resolved the problem of *justification* more decisively than the problem of *causation*. The clinician's tentative suggestion of independent peripheral pathology (“Not clear”) appeared next to the patient's supposed emphatic separation: “in his own mind - markedly divorced” ([Document 4](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-4)). His own provisional inference was thus capable of acquiring apparent confirmation from the patient's understanding. The identified completed narrative did not preserve the protest that challenged the sufficiency of the disposition. The authorisation of 17 July came after that notice. The timing matters because the author was adopting the institutional representation after encountering the actual person's objection, independently of when the first draft was typed.

The result described by the patient is **anticipatory vindication**: the completed account furnished grounds on which the admission could later be defended at the very point when a central question remained uninvestigated or unallocated. It made a clinical pathway appear legitimate by changing what the patient was said to understand and by not conveying that he had disputed its sufficiency.

### The ethical purpose of the discharge document

Medical documentation has an unavoidable dual use: it guides future care and permits subsequent examination of previous care. Neither function is intrinsically suspect. The ethical boundary is crossed when the second function—the desire to make past decisions hard to challenge—controls the first—the obligation faithfully to transmit the clinical state of the actual patient.

A proper discharge summary could acknowledge a Parkinsonian syndrome while identifying an unresolved contribution of abnormal motor activity to painful pressure pathology, the rationale for podiatry and any further responsibility for reassessment. It could record the patient's objection without treating that objection as a medically decisive diagnosis. There is no conflict between professional independence and the faithful recording of disagreement. The ability to make a defensible clinical decision is strengthened, not undermined, by accurate acknowledgment of uncertainty and the patient's contrary perspective.

On the stated case, the completed account instead made the patient appear to endorse the partition whose sufficiency he challenged. This is why the misattribution is central rather than incidental. It furnished a *justificatory premise*: the separated pathway appeared consistent not only with the clinician's differential but with the patient's own sense of his illness. When that premise was knowingly inaccurate, the document's apparent defensibility was purchased at the cost of truthfulness.

The strongest philosophical conclusion is independent of whether all subsequent medical decisions were harmful. **If the author knowingly substituted an account of patient agreement for the patient's actual objection in order to legitimate an unfinished disposition, the ethical wrong was already complete at authorisation.** Any preventable lesion, pain episode or delayed reassessment is a further causal issue. The representational wrong does not require downstream harm to become wrong.

### A better test than simply asking whether the treatment can be defended

A responsible assessment distinguishes four questions. **Was the original integrated foot–neurological problem investigated or expressly allocated for resolution?** **Did the final record preserve what the patient actually said and the material limits of the examination?** **Did later care remain responsive to evidence that challenged the initial separation?** **Did the retrospective defence independently examine the preceding questions rather than simply establish consistency with the recorded framework?**

These questions prevent the existence of substantial neurological work from becoming a surrogate answer to the feet. They also prevent a patient's later suffering from being treated as automatic proof of a single pathophysiology. The point is a more demanding standard of clinical responsibility: the account must be adequate to the actual patient *before* the institution may rely on it as a demonstration of adequate care.

**Defensibility is not accountability. Defensibility becomes legitimate only when the reasons advanced can survive independent examination of the patient, the original referral, the information available, and the unresolved questions—including evidence the author did not preserve in the final account.**

## Contribution 18 — Safeguarding versus institutional self-protection: the longitudinal ethical failure

### The patient remains present after the institutional episode closes

The closing of an admission and the confirmation of its discharge summary are administrative and clinical transitions, not a moral termination of responsibility for the consequences of care. The patient's later foot lesions, toe curling, impaired function and episodes of extreme pain were events in the continuing course of an actual illness. They could not be made less significant by the success of an institutional account in explaining earlier decisions.

This gives the ethical analysis its proper object. The purpose of medical governance is not chiefly to produce a historical verdict on whether clinicians can defend themselves. It is to protect patients through responsive investigation, continuity, learning and correction. When new symptoms or treatment responses complicate an inherited explanation, the system should remain able to reconsider that explanation. A clinically reasonable decision in 2017 does not by itself resolve what should be done in 2019, 2023 or 2025.

The longitudinal record repeatedly posed questions relevant to an integrated mechanism. After procyclidine withdrawal, the patient reported a return of trouble at the left outer-foot corn site and increased tension ([Document 12](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-12)). The GP linked painful clawed feet to abnormal gait and tone in 2019 ([Document 11](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-11)). The March 2023 urgent referral described extreme burning pain, psychological distress and a “death wish,” showing an urgent safeguarding concern independent of the ultimate pain mechanism ([Document 17](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-17)). Subsequent neurology entertained OFF dystonia, while pain relief from pregabalin did not abolish toe curling, and Sinemet-related pain relief was recorded ([Documents 18–20](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-18)). These data are not proof of every link in the dystonic–mechanical pathway. They are reasons why the original separation could not legitimately become immune to ongoing inquiry.

To safeguard the patient would mean addressing both immediate suffering and the unresolved relationship among abnormal movement, toe posture, pressure and pain as clinical circumstances required. It also means recognizing when knowledge, expertise or continuity of care is insufficient and determining who will provide appropriate specialist input. The patient's report that the neurologist acknowledged not being a movement-disorder specialist strengthens the ethical significance of *recognizing limits and securing appropriate input*; that acknowledgement should be attributed to the patient's account rather than converted into an independently authenticated quotation.

### Defending past reasoning and meeting current needs are different responsibilities

The clinician may legitimately explain why a particular treatment was chosen. There may have been significant and genuine concerns about early levodopa and dyskinesia. Such considerations bear on specific prescribing decisions. They are not substitutes for an adequate account of the original neurological–foot problem or for safeguarding the patient when the subsequent clinical course raises further concern.

The March 2025 clinical response defended the treatment strategy through these considerations ([Document 22](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-22)). The hospital later reported a no-deficit conclusion, emphasizing the caution taken with Sinemet ([Document 31](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-31)). That communicated rationale shows how the history could be defended on medication terms without necessarily answering the allegation of false patient attribution or an unowned neurological–mechanical question. The full PAR would determine what other matters were independently examined.

The relevant ethical failure is not that a clinician or institution offered a defence at all. Accountability requires explanations. It is that **a defence of prior decisions can displace the patient-protective inquiry that a complaint and continuing clinical deterioration ought to provoke**. If the outcome is reached by accepting the contested record as the baseline, the review can make the clinician's history more secure without making the patient's condition safer or the original question better understood.

This is the second opportunity for correction after the protest. In 2017, the clinician could have responded to the bedside challenge before completing the account. In 2025, the institution could have restored the full GP referral, compared the handwritten and completed accounts, examined the patient's protest and traced the later movement/foot evidence. An institutional process that defends the documented care without performing that independent source examination would reproduce the initial substitution at a higher level of authority.

### The distinction between protecting a patient and protecting an institutional position

The phrase “safeguarding the institution” must be treated as an account of the alleged function and motive, not as a neutral description of all hospital reviews. Institutions have legitimate reasons to defend correct decisions, and a finding that care was appropriate is not inherently wrongful. But if a knowingly misleading representation is maintained because it makes an unresolved disposition defensible, institutional processes have been diverted from their patient-protective purpose.

The distinction is functional. Protecting the patient asks: what is happening to this person, what remains clinically uncertain, what risks require response, who has responsibility, and how can the explanation change when evidence changes? Protecting a prior position asks: can our decisions be reconciled with what our record says? The second question is legitimate only when it remains subordinate to the first and when the record itself is open to independent challenge.

In that light the patient's formulation—“The record was made defensible before the care had earned its defensibility”—contains an exact moral charge. It claims that the appearance of responsible conduct was fashioned through an account that displaced an unresolved duty. The later no-deficit finding risks becoming a further layer of the same structure if it treats coherence within the authored record as equivalent to adequacy toward the person.

**A hospital's ethical legitimacy depends on improving the correspondence between its knowledge, its actions and the patient's real condition. It cannot attain that legitimacy by making its account more difficult to challenge while leaving the patient exposed to unaddressed clinical uncertainty and suffering.**

## Contribution 19 — Final synthesis: the construction of defensibility at the expense of the patient

The central allegation now has a clearer philosophical structure. The hospital received a connected neurological problem but represented it more narrowly at admission. A senior discussion established a direction for movement-disorder care. The patient subsequently restored the missing painful-foot question to the treating clinician by direct protest on 10 July. That encounter put both the adequacy of the investigation and the fidelity of the hospital's information chain in issue.

On the patient's account, the neurologist's response was not to reopen the causal question but to preserve an established disposition through an authoritative representation. The handwritten distinction between foot pain and muscular tension was transformed into a stronger claim attributed to the patient's mind; a provisional peripheral hypothesis stood beside it; the protest was absent; and the completed narrative was authorised on 17 July. The specific ethical mechanism is the creation of an apparent source of independent patient agreement to make an uncertain clinical division look more justified than the investigation had established.

The significance of the discharge summary is therefore both retrospective and prospective. It recorded the institution's version of its work and shaped what later clinicians would understand had already been resolved or appropriately allocated. It became the “line in the sand” in the precise sense that a contested, unfinished explanatory relationship could now travel forward in the authoritative form of a clinical disposition. **The account made the care appear defensible before the underlying question had earned a clinical answer.**

The following years carried genuine clinical treatment alongside persistent foot pathology, abnormal movement and severe periods of distress. Those later observations were opportunities for the clinical explanation to become more responsive to the patient; they were not answered by the mere persistence of a peripheral diagnostic possibility. The institutional obligation to safeguard included attention to immediate pain and psychological risk, appropriate expertise, and a reasoned clinical ownership of the possible motor–mechanical pathway.

The 2025 dispute presented a second chance to correct the original narrowing. A clinician's defence of medication choices and the hospital's communicated no-deficit finding cannot independently demonstrate that the 2017 account was faithful to the patient's words or that the complete GP referral was answered. When the truth of the record is challenged, a review must not assume it as the premise of its conclusion. Otherwise professional and institutional authority can validate the treatment of a patient constructed in the record while leaving the actual person's unanswered question outside the review's scope.

The strongest case, therefore, is not simply that the neurologist made an incorrect diagnosis or failed to order a test. It is that **the power to interpret and document the patient was allegedly used knowingly to manufacture defensibility for an unresolved clinical disposition; the resulting narrative acquired long-term practical authority; and subsequent defence risked substituting internal coherence for faithful accountability to the patient.**

**Final governing proposition:** Clinical decisions become defensible by being grounded in sound investigation, faithful representation, transparent uncertainty, appropriate follow-up and responsive care. **They do not become justified because a professional has constructed a record capable of defending them.** The hospital's primary responsibility was to investigate and safeguard the actual patient. It was not to safeguard the appearance of adequacy in its own decisions.
