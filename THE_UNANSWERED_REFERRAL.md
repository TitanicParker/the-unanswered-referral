# The Unanswered Referral — From the July 2017 Clinical Record to the Hospital's Nine-Year Defence

*Case overview | 18 November 2026*

A man came to his GP because his feet hurt, his gait was abnormal, and ordinary working life was becoming difficult. The feet were not an incidental complication discovered in a patient with an established neurological diagnosis. They were already visibly abnormal when he joined the practice in April 2017. The GP examined mallet toes, pressure lesions and calluses. Over the next several weeks, a more troubling pattern emerged: difficulty initiating movement, abnormal muscle tone, toe clawing in bed, worsening dexterity and increasing inability to perform his job. By late June, the GP sought urgent hospital assessment of a developing neurological presentation in which the feet remained prominent. The request was not simply for a diagnostic label. It was for specialist assistance in understanding and managing a collection of problems that appeared to be converging. [1]

The hospital's response was substantial. A neurologist diagnosed young-onset Parkinsonism, documented striking movement abnormalities, arranged investigations, consulted colleagues and devised a treatment strategy. The patient received neurological medicines and a podiatry referral. The resulting record is extensive and often thoughtful. None of those facts should be discounted. Yet an important distinction runs through this case: **a hospital can answer the question it recognises most clearly while leaving another question in the referral inadequately examined.** The relevant question is whether the abnormal neurological activity contributed to the patient's painful foot posture, toe clawing, loading and recurring lesions—and, if the relationship remained uncertain, who was responsible for pursuing it.

The sharper distinction is between **an unanswered diagnostic question and an unowned clinical responsibility**. Medicine may reasonably leave a mechanism uncertain. What requires explanation is whether the service that recognised the possible relationship identified who would test it, how the results would return to the clinical team, and what would trigger reconsideration if symptoms persisted. An unresolved possibility is not, by itself, a lapse in care; an unresolved possibility left between specialties without an accountable follow-up may be.

That question acquired a second life in the record. The neurologist's handwritten interview preserved a history of longstanding tension and painful feet. The completed clinical impression proposed that the foot symptoms might be separate or represent peripheral neuropathy, while attributing to the patient an emphatic mental distinction between foot pain and leg tension. According to the patient's later firsthand account, he had directly challenged the inadequate examination of his feet shortly before discharge; the completed record does not preserve that objection. Years later, the same neurologist responded to a complaint by defending the diagnosis and the caution exercised with dopaminergic treatment. The hospital's subsequent review reported no deficit in care. The danger is that **the hospital's authoritative account of what it did became, in later proceedings, an answer to the different question of whether it had done enough.** [2][3]

This essay concerns that movement of authority—from referral to specialist interpretation, from interpretation to management, and from management history to institutional defence. Its case is not that no one cared for the patient or that no treatment ever changed. It is that an unresolved component of the original presentation may have become administratively and clinically manageable without ever receiving a clearly owned, integrated explanation. The later facts do not erase the genuine work carried out in 2017. They reveal why the distinction between work done and question answered has become indispensable.

## The question the GP actually sent

On 10 April 2017 the GP recorded bilateral foot pain and an abnormal gait, with marked mallet toes, abnormal nails, calluses and pressure areas. At that stage, podiatry, blood tests and old records were reasonable routes of investigation. But the April findings were not a new disease onset. They were established foot and gait difficulties now coming under the observation of a new GP. By 8 June, discussion had moved beyond the feet as a local problem. The GP noted difficulty initiating movement and explicitly contemplated a neurology opinion on the patient's “gait, feet and ‘muscle tension’.” An occupational-health doctor independently echoed concern about gait on 12 June. On 23 June, the GP documented markedly increased tone in both legs and brisk upper-limb reflexes. By 29 June, deterioration of dexterity and movement was threatening the patient's employment. The urgent referral described a stiff, festinant gait, increased tone, movement-initiation problems, inability to relax and toe clawing when trying to find comfort in bed. [1]

A lawyer should not read this as a claim that the GP had diagnosed neurological foot dystonia or already established the mechanism of the corns. The referral was significant precisely because the GP *had not* determined the relationship. Local pressure lesions could have mechanical causes; gait abnormalities could themselves alter loading; a movement disorder could contribute to foot posturing; multiple causes could coexist. The combination called for specialist assessment because the features were increasingly difficult to separate by ordinary primary-care examination. The neurologist was free to conclude that the problems were partly or wholly independent. What matters is whether the record shows that independence was examined rather than merely entertained.

The GP had independently gathered information. He had examined the feet, watched the movement problem develop, considered prior investigations, consulted occupational health and responded to the impact on work. That evidence preceded the hospital's account and cannot be made subordinate to it simply because the hospital later issued a specialist letter. The original referral is therefore a fixed point against which the adequacy of the admission can be tested. It is not an accusation retrospectively constructed in 2025. It is the contemporaneous account of why a patient was sent to hospital. [1]

## What the neurologist learned—and what he treated

The neurologist's own assessment supplied reasons to take the relationship seriously. He observed a gait with an appearance of foot drop that was “somewhat suggestive of a dystonic posture,” while explicitly stating that it was **not true foot drop**. He recorded pes planus and hammertoes. His history reached back beyond the current presentation. The patient described bodily tension from around 2004, longstanding foot abnormalities treated from approximately 2009 or 2010, and attendance at the same hospital in 2014 because of physical tension in his arms and legs. He considered his deteriorating condition in 2017 to be a maturation of those earlier symptoms. The neurologist carried a roughly thirteen-year symptom history into the DaTscan request and into the request for Professor Healy's opinion. This was not a doctor unaware of chronic abnormal movement or a patient with a newly discovered unrelated foot complaint. [2][4]

The treatment is equally important. During admission, clonazepam was commenced to relieve leg tension; the contemporaneous record does not establish the exact first administered dose or night without the medication chart. The clinician considered maintaining it long-term unless advised otherwise, but also contemplated that tolerable nocturnal procyclidine might make clonazepam unnecessary. In other words, he treated the tension as a clinical problem under neurological management and foresaw that one Parkinsonism-directed drug might displace another medicine used for the same difficult symptom. This is not evidence that clonazepam was continuously swallowed every night thereafter. It is evidence of continuity in *what was being treated*. The symptom belonged to the long history the neurologist himself had reconstructed. [2]

There was also genuine diagnostic achievement. The investigations supported Parkinsonism. The clinician considered early onset, possible recessive inheritance and risk from introducing levodopa-related treatments too early. The later DaTscan was abnormal. Colleagues were involved. The clinical account expressly acknowledged the possibility of eventual levodopa treatment and even that its delay might later be regretted. This was a managed strategy, not a literal instruction never to change treatment. Similarly, the observation that a genetic diagnosis would generally be unlikely to change management concerned the practical effect of molecular identification on treatment, not necessarily the value of revisiting how a patient's specific foot symptoms arose. [2]

Here lies the first crucial asymmetry. The neurologist recognised an abnormal motor system, documented a potentially dystonic posture, and treated longstanding leg tension. He also saw structural abnormalities capable of causing painful pressure. But the final impression did not give an equivalent account of how those findings interacted. It suggested that the corns, calluses and burning **“may have been completely separate or perhaps a secondary peripheral neuropathy”** and added that the relationship was **“Not clear.”** The statement was candidly provisional. It was not a proven neuropathy diagnosis, and alcohol association was only a proposed possibility. Yet the management arrangements allowed the possibility to serve as a practical division: neurological treatment for the movement disorder, podiatry for the foot lesions, and no clearly identified neurological owner of the relationship between them. [2]

The difference is not simply semantic. The neurologist could legitimately discharge a patient with an unresolved causal differential if reasonable further assessment was planned, risk was addressed and responsibility allocated. The available discharge material identifies symptomatic neurological management and foot-care referrals but does not identify a named clinician or agreed pathway charged with resolving whether neurological posture or tone was producing or aggravating painful pressure. That is an **ownership gap to investigate**, not proof that the relationship could or should have been definitively diagnosed during this admission.

Podiatry was necessary and legitimate. Indeed, the GP himself contacted podiatry during the admission to seek priority care, and the hospital account refers to referral efforts. But treatment of a lesion and assessment of the forces causing it are not identical services. A corn can be debrided without establishing whether involuntary toe clawing continually recreates the pressure. An orthotic intervention can reduce pain without explaining a movement disorder. The foot-care pathway cannot simply be counted as a completed investigation of the neurological-foot relationship unless the actual assessment, conclusions and communication show that it was. That is the first document the hospital should now be asked to identify: **where, at discharge, was that connection investigated, answered or explicitly handed over for investigation?** [1][2]

## When a difference in sensation became a difference in the record

The account of the patient's words is where the clinical and documentary histories begin to overlap. The original handwritten interview is unusually valuable because it permits comparison with the completed typed account. Its third page lists corns, calluses and fallen arches among problems that followed the earlier bodily tension. The patient had received treatment for these foot difficulties for years. After leaving bar work in 2012, some pain associated with standing receded; the tension did not. The handwriting records that he began to recognise that foot pain and leg tension **“weren't the same.”** The first two handwritten pages also preserve the 2014 presentation and the patient's understanding that the current condition was its maturation. The published line-by-line transcription is editorially normalised, so the photographed manuscript must control disputed readings. [4]

There is an important discipline here. The typed history says that foot pain and tension were “not the same problem.” The manuscript already contains a comparable distinction, so the phrase cannot honestly be presented as invented by a typist. But a patient can experience two sensations as different without believing they have unrelated medical causes. Pain can diminish when standing decreases while neurological tension continues; that difference may reveal distinct contributing mechanisms without establishing independent diseases. Phenomenology is not aetiology. The distinction mattered precisely because the neurologist was charged with examining the relationship, not merely recording the patient's attempt to describe it.

The completed impression went further. Immediately after proposing separate symptoms or a peripheral-neuropathy explanation, it stated that the patient had **“in his own mind - markedly divorced”** leg tension from the corns, calluses and pains in the feet. Those are not neutral repetitions of the shorthand manuscript. “Markedly” intensifies the degree of separation; “in his own mind” places that separation within the patient's beliefs. The words remain clinician-authored, although framed as a description of the patient. Their position beside the clinician's causal possibility invites the reader to see the proposed separation as supported by the patient's own understanding. [2][4]

This matters because the discharge account was not an ordinary conversation. It became a professional record capable of influencing the GP, subsequent neurologists and eventual institutional reviewers. A clinician's interpretation of a patient's description can be entirely legitimate if identified as such and reasonably supported. The concern here is that the line between the patient's observation and the clinician's inference is insufficiently visible. The medical record may make the patient appear to have endorsed a division that was, on the available handwritten evidence, at most a hypothesis for investigation. That is a testable attribution problem, not proof of forged words or dishonesty.

The omission of what the patient says happened just before discharge makes the issue sharper. He recounts that on Monday 10 July 2017 he asked four medical students to summon the neurologist and challenged the inadequate investigation of his painful feet. The completed clinical account does not record such a protest or its resolution. The encounter is direct patient testimony rather than an independently corroborated event; some later retellings contained a one-day date discrepancy, and ward records and potential witnesses need to be examined. But if the account is established, it would show that the neurologist knew before discharge that the patient disputed the adequacy of the foot investigation. [5]

The chronology must not be misused. The patient was discharged on 11 July. An addendum was finalised on 13 July, after the DaTscan, and the discharge summary was confirmed on 17 July. Those dates establish that the completed record was authorised after the reported confrontation. They do **not** establish when any particular phrase was first drafted. There is a difference between newly writing a defensive interpretation and preserving or approving an earlier one despite new contrary information. The latter would remain consequential even if the words “markedly divorced” originated before the protest. The crucial unresolved questions are whether the encounter occurred, what the clinician said, what he did with the objection, and why the completed account preserved the emphatic patient-attribution but no corresponding challenge. [2][5]

The most serious hypothesis is not that every element of the discharge note was manufactured after the event. It is more economical: **the final account may have stabilised an already established clinical disposition by assigning unusual explanatory weight to the patient's earlier symptom distinction, while not communicating a later objection to the sufficiency of the investigation.** Confirmation would require both witness evidence and the document's creation and revision history. Yet the less intention-dependent problem already exists: the completed account conveyed a stronger patient-attributed separation than the manuscript alone demonstrates, and the clinician's causal conclusion remained uncertain.

## What the discharge account returned to the GP

The effects of the record become clearer when followed back into primary care. The electronic discharge message identified Parkinson's disease as the primary diagnosis, emphasised bradykinesia and difficulty initiating movement, described clonazepam for tension and noted podiatry referral. The fuller consultant account carried the more expansive reasoning about possible separate peripheral symptoms. In mid-July the GP recorded that he was awaiting formal correspondence; on 18 July he noted extensive specialist correspondence, a diagnosis of early Parkinsonism and a positive DaTscan. The hospital had provided a major diagnostic advance and a specialist treatment pathway. The GP had to incorporate that authoritative result into continuing care. Yet the information transferred did not clearly say: *this foot–movement relationship remains a neurological question; this clinician will assess it; this is the trigger for re-referral; and this is what primary care and podiatry should report back*. The difference between naming a destination for symptomatic treatment and naming an owner for causal investigation is central to what the hospital returned to primary care. [1][2]

Yet he did not simply surrender his original understanding. This is one of the most revealing findings in the later record and a necessary correction to an over-simple anchoring theory. In March 2019 the GP's podiatry re-referral described foot issues **“in relating to increased tone and abnormal gait relating to Parkinson's disease”** and added that very young-onset Parkinsonism had problems with **“clawing feet” and pain**. The original physical pathology persisted in the podiatry records, including recurrent callus around the left fifth metatarsophalangeal region, clawed toes, padding, debridement, toe props and insole interventions. The GP was still connecting feet, movement and tone. [6]

The problem was therefore not that the specialist record compelled primary care to believe the feet were neurologically irrelevant. It was that the GP could preserve the connection without possessing the specialist authority or resources to resolve it. Referral to podiatry could address pressure damage. Repeat letters could communicate persistent suffering. Primary care could initiate symptomatic medicine. But the neurological interpretation and complex movement-disorder prescribing remained substantially in specialist hands. The original discharge account had created a specialist-led disposition in which the GP could act upon parts of the presentation without necessarily obtaining a full answer to the relationship underlying them.

This does not amount to a formal prohibition imposed by the neurologist. In complicated young-onset Parkinsonism, specialist involvement in dopaminergic prescribing is clinically understandable. What the case shows is a narrower and more consequential dependency: **if the missing explanation was neurological, primary care needed the specialist service that had originated the management framework to reopen it.** The GP's continued recognition of a link did not compel such reopening. The information state was resilient not because medication doses were unchangeable, but because the premises dividing neurological management from painful-foot management could remain unexamined while those doses changed.

## The later findings that should have unsettled any easy partition

Several subsequent events acquire their importance by returning to the original question rather than by creating an entirely new one. In September 2017 Professor Healy considered PARK2 probable and recommended appropriate genetic investigation. In April 2018 the treating neurologist himself recorded a suspected PRKN/PARK2 disorder and the patient's report that stopping procyclidine had been followed by a rapid return of symptoms at the original outer-left-foot corn site. That report must not be inflated into objective proof that a corn physically formed overnight or that withdrawal established a causal mechanism. It was a patient-reported, medication-associated recurrence at a specific historical site, contemporaneously known to the treating clinician. Alongside the dystonic-looking 2017 gait, it was a reason to revisit how neurological treatment affected the foot problem. [7]

The 2019 GP account adds independent corroboration of the question's persistence, not proof of its answer. The GP wrote that abnormal gait and increased tone were relevant to the feet. Podiatry documented persistent physical abnormalities. The different sources converge on the fact that painful lesions and altered lower-limb positioning remained clinically important. They do not alone demonstrate how much of the pain was neurological, mechanical or neuropathic. Their significance is that an explanation treating those categories as separate required continued examination rather than passive inheritance.

The crisis in March 2023 is an even stronger test of the practical arrangement. In an urgent referral dated 26 March, the GP described severe bilateral burning pain in the feet and lower legs, profound functional distress and reported thoughts of death associated with the suffering. He documented that pregabalin had begun at 25 mg twice daily and escalated through prescribers. He also supplied an analogy from an earlier episode: pain affecting the arm had settled when Parkinsonism was better controlled. The GP was not merely requesting another analgesic. He was asking the neurologist to engage with a difficult presentation and offering evidence that pain mechanisms might respond to movement-disorder treatment. [8]

On 28 March the Parkinson's nurse confirmed receipt of the patient's urgent concerns, the GP's introduction of pregabalin and persisting debilitating pain despite high doses. The contemporaneous specialist-contact record then describes a cancellation-list approach because of clinic pressures, with follow-up through the GP in the meantime. This did not mean nothing subsequently happened. Neurology did reassess symptoms, consider possible Fabry disease and other explanations, discuss treatment states and adjust medication. But the immediate response demonstrates the institutional dependency: the GP who had escalated an urgent neurological problem remained the person to whom the patient was directed while awaiting specialist capacity. More fundamentally, this was an opportunity to clarify **which service owned the persistent unresolved interaction** among toe curling, abnormal loading, pain and the neurological treatment state. A cancellation-list arrangement might be a reasonable short-term access measure; it would not itself identify who accepted responsibility for answering that question or provide a durable plan for doing so. The documented death-wish warning also requires examination of what safety assessment and timely clinical response were actually arranged; its presence in the referral does not by itself establish all subsequent safety actions. [8]

The 2023–24 records are a warning against caricature. The neurologist was not inflexibly reciting a single alcohol-neuropathy diagnosis. He acknowledged that peripheral neuropathic symptoms can accompany Parkinsonism, explored Fabry disease and discussed the relationship between pain and medication effects. The patient's views about whether Parkinsonism or a separate foot problem dominated his disability were also represented differently from the way his supposed mental separation had appeared in 2017. In a later clinic account the clinician portrayed a disagreement in which the patient considered the feet a major separate problem while the neurologist saw a broader Parkinsonian explanation. That shifting attribution invites caution about treating any one clinician-authored description as a timeless statement of the patient's beliefs. [9]

An especially revealing 2024 observation was that pregabalin relieved much foot pain while toe curling remained during activity. Analgesic response and abnormal movement were not the same outcome. That does not prove one caused the other; it makes the need to understand their interaction harder to dismiss. Genuine changes in prescribing, symptoms and differential diagnosis can coexist with the absence of a completed explanation of why abnormal toe position and painful recurrent lesions occurred together. The longitudinal question is not whether doctors were busy. It is whether their accumulated information was ever converted into an accountable answer about the original feet.

In 2025 the GP continued to assist, but expressly stated that he could not independently adjust levodopa without specialist guidance. Following a late-2025 specialist assessment of dystonia, he again welcomed the new attention while explaining that new neurological treatment required specialist prescribing and monitoring. These statements show a practical constraint, not loss of judgment. The GP continued recognising difficulty and seeking help; the specialist framework still governed major therapeutic changes. The patient could therefore encounter a persistent gulf between having his suffering recognised in primary care and obtaining the particular specialist reassessment needed to change its neurological management. [10]

The 2026 molecular report supplies further context, but not a retrospective shortcut. It identified two pathogenic variants in *PRKN*, a gene associated with recessive young-onset Parkinson disease. The report's causative interpretation was conditional on their being in a compound-heterozygous arrangement; segregation or other informative family testing was recommended. The later dystonia assessment and PRKN findings increase the relevance of the longstanding abnormal posture, and make the earlier PARK2 suspicions more prescient. They do not prove that all historical foot pain was dystonic. Crucially, they are not necessary to create the original question. The 2017 GP referral and neurologist's examination did that already. [11]

## From defending treatment to defending the history of treatment

The transition in March 2025 is decisive because the person who created the original specialist account became the person explaining the adequacy of its consequences. The patient's formal complaint of 19 March, titled *Diagnostic Anchoring, Bias, and Failure to Reassess (2017–2025)*, questioned the persistent framing of severe foot pain through possible peripheral neuropathy and the failure to explore or sufficiently act upon treatment-responsive alternatives. The reported profound benefit from Sinemet CR gave the complaint urgency. The patient sought an independent review of the clinical reasoning, access to records, an explanation and preservation of material. The complaint itself had imperfections of perspective and chronology; some contemporaneous prescribing was not fully appreciated when it was written. Those limitations matter, but they do not change the central question it actually posed. [12]

The neurologist's response of 25 March contained important medical reasoning. Parkinsonism had been recognised; the patient had been treated over years; levodopa carried special risks for younger people, including dyskinesia; the clinician knew that Sinemet could lessen pain; and other possible disorders had been investigated. He argued that Parkinsonism can magnify the experience of pain arising from other causes. His emphasis on cautious escalation had contemporary support, and the complaint had expressly raised medication response, so it would be misleading to call the rebuttal irrelevant. The problem is the *reach* of the answer. A defensible reason for delaying or gradually escalating Sinemet is not an explanation of why abnormal foot posture, painful pressure lesions and longstanding leg tension were partitioned at the original admission. [13]

The rebuttal opens by speaking of **“peripheral neuropathic pain symptomatologies”** irrespective of underlying cause. This wording retains a neuropathic category without claiming its cause definitively established. The clinician explains that Parkinsonism may magnify pain from other sources, an intelligible clinical possibility. But this explains how an established Parkinsonian state might amplify pain *if another generator exists*. It does not demonstrate what generated this patient's recurrent focal lesions, toe curling and painful loading, nor how their neurological contribution was examined. A plausible general mechanism is not a case-specific causal finding.

A small but revealing example concerns pregabalin. In the original March 2023 GP referral and nurse communication, pregabalin originated in general practice in response to severe pain. In the 2025 rebuttal, the neurologist described Lyrica as having been introduced partly to spare further Sinemet escalation. Later specialist management could genuinely have adopted that Sinemet-sparing rationale. It does not follow that the GP's initial prescribing decision was made for that reason. The rebuttal compresses a series of separate decisions into a more coherent, neurologist-led treatment narrative than the contemporaneous origin alone establishes. This is evidence of retrospective *smoothing*, not proof of a knowing falsehood. The distinction illustrates the wider danger: once a treatment pathway has evolved over years, the explanation given later may describe the strategy it eventually served rather than the uncertainty under which it began. [8][13]

The rebuttal also invoked a second specialist opinion and investigations for rare overlap disorders. Those were real. Yet Professor Healy's September 2017 view specifically raised PARK2, and the treating neurologist's April 2018 account coupled that suspicion with a patient-reported recurrence at the original foot site following neurological medicine withdrawal. The rebuttal did not bring those facts into a sustained account of the foot–movement relationship. The proof of specialist involvement is not the same as proof that the right question was referred, considered and answered. The proof of an alternative-cause test is not the same as proof of a biomechanical neurological-foot examination. [7][13]

This produces the principal complaint–answer mismatch. The patient complained that the diagnosis and management framework had failed to account for prolonged painful feet. The doctor responded most fully that the Parkinsonism diagnosis and management strategy had been clinically thoughtful. Both statements can be true. They concern different questions. An institution that accepts the second as answering the first risks defending decisions made *within* a framework without examining whether the framework adequately defined the problem.

It matters, too, what the March complaint did **not** yet contain. The detailed 10 July protest account was not a feature of the original March 2025 submission; the surviving correspondence placed it before the hospital later that year. It would be unfair to charge the neurologist with consciously omitting from his 25 March answer a bedside event he had not then been specifically asked to address. The later institutional review faced a different information environment. By that stage, the patient had raised the protest and had disputed accuracy and use of the historical record. Different knowledge dates produce different responsibilities. [5][12]

## When the institution reviewed its own authoritative account

The hospital initiated a Preliminary Assessment Report and brought it to a Serious Incident Management Team meeting on 27 August 2025. The signed outcome, dated 3 September, concluded that there had been **“no deficit in the care provided.”** Its communicated reasoning centred on caution with Sinemet in a young patient, side-effect risk, the consideration of alternative diagnoses and the fact that Sinemet had been prescribed from 2021. This was a broad institutional conclusion supported in the letter by a narrower account of prescribing considerations. [14]

There is nothing inherently improper about a hospital examining its past clinical decisions or about relying on contemporaneous notes. The difficulty arises when the notes' completeness and attribution are themselves disputed. If the discharge record is used as the authoritative statement of what the patient understood and what clinicians investigated, it cannot also be treated as independently proving that those representations were adequate. The original GP referral, handwritten interview, disputed typed impression and later primary-care records provide external tests of its narrative. A genuinely independent review would need to show how those sources were compared, not merely that the completed consultant account was available for inspection.

Before the SIMT meeting the institution had received relevant information-rights communications. The June 2025 Article 16 requests challenged aspects of the record. A 7 July hospital-facing request raised the reported protest. The 4 August Article 18 correspondence expressly questioned reliance on disputed clinical data in the impending review and requested source versions and an audit trail. These communications establish hospital-level notice; they do not establish that every member of the SIMT personally saw the notices, that restriction automatically barred the review, or that the hospital acted unlawfully. The point is evidential. A review commenced after the institution was told the historical account was contested should be able to explain what disputed evidence it used, how it tested it and who knew of the challenge. [5][14][15]

The full PAR and its source inventory are not established in the reviewed material. That limitation is substantive, not ceremonial. The signed letter might be a compressed description of a much wider inquiry. If the underlying report shows a careful comparison of the original referral, handwritten notes, clinician's impression, podiatry evidence, subsequent treatment and information notices, the assessment of institutional independence would materially change. Counsel should neither invent that deeper work nor assume it was absent. The justified present conclusion is narrower and forceful: **the hospital's disclosed reasons do not demonstrate the investigation needed to support a comprehensive no-deficit finding about the original painful-foot presentation.** [14]

The institution's subsequent record-accuracy decision illustrates a related risk. In September 2025 it defended retention of historical professional opinion because the record should preserve what clinicians believed at the time and explain later care. That principle has force. Historical opinions should not be silently deleted simply because later knowledge changes. But this complaint asks whether the notes faithfully represented the patient's words and whether an uncertain explanatory hypothesis was properly characterised. Accurate preservation of *an opinion having existed* does not, by itself, establish the accuracy of the opinion's attributed factual premises. An annotation preserving the original entry while recording a material dispute is conceptually different from erasing history. The hospital's response did not visibly dispose of that separate attribution question. [15]

In January 2026 the Quality and Patient Safety department stated that the December 2025 dystonia diagnosis did not require reopening earlier governance determinations or amending historical records. Once again, there is a defensible distinction between judging 2017 clinicians against 2017 knowledge and pretending the 2026 genetic report was available to them. But the original question did not arise in 2026. The GP had identified the foot–gait–tone problem in June 2017; the neurologist had described dystonic-looking posture and treated leg tension that July. A later specialist diagnosis could illuminate the significance of those existing facts. Simply categorising it as later information did not explain whether the earlier review had adequately considered the contemporaneous evidence. [15]

This is the possible institutional circularity at the heart of the case. The 2017 account became the record of the admission. Later management occurred within and around the categories it established, even as treatment and diagnostic thinking evolved. The treating neurologist then used the management history to defend himself against an allegation about the underlying clinical interpretation. The hospital reported a no-deficit result, principally explaining that decision through the defended treatment strategy. Without an independently demonstrated examination of the original referral and the record's construction, the final institutional conclusion risks confirming the authority of the very account whose sufficiency the patient disputed.

The allegation is not that every later clinician blindly copied the same neuropathy label. The GP did not, and the neurologist performed real further investigations. It is instead that **a clinical problem can remain without an identifiable owner while an apparently coherent history of management grows around it.** The more complete that management history appears, the easier it becomes to mistake continuity of care for completeness of explanation.

## What this history asks counsel to prove

A fourth question sits within the first: **clinical ownership**. If a definitive mechanism was not available, was there a documented allocation of responsibility to Neurology, Podiatry, a joint assessment or another named service, with a method for relaying new findings and reconsidering the working explanation? It is possible to provide competent component care in each service while leaving the relationship between the components without an owner. That possibility requires inspection of the actual referrals, handovers and follow-up decisions, not an inference from the word “uncertain” alone.

Three distinct matters must remain separate. First is clinical adequacy: whether, judged against what was known and expected in 2017, the hospital reasonably investigated or allocated the foot–posture–pain question. Second is documentary fidelity: whether the completed clinical account accurately distinguished the patient's experience and objection from the neurologist's interpretation. Third is governance independence: whether the 2025–26 institutional responses genuinely tested the adequacy and construction of the original record rather than only the reasonableness of downstream treatment. Evidence supporting one cannot automatically establish another.

These questions also stand at different evidential levels. The GP's original words, the neurological findings, clonazepam plan, provisional peripheral explanation, 2019 GP referral, 2023 urgent escalation and signed SIMT conclusion are documented. The patient's 10 July protest is testimony supported by its subsequent written emergence, but the encounter and response need independent witness or clinical-record testing. The proposition that the neurologist deliberately shaped or preserved the note to make the admission appear complete is a serious inference, not an established fact. The clinical gap and complaint–answer mismatch need not wait for that inference to be proved. [1]–[15]

It is tempting to cast the case as the discovery of a single mistaken diagnosis. That would be too small and, in some respects, misleading. Parkinsonism was diagnosed with substantial clinical support. The feet were treated. Levodopa caution had real rationales. Later pain treatment changed. The more fundamental possibility is a **mismatch between the clinical problem received and the explanatory responsibility discharged**. What began as a connected GP referral became a specialist disposition allocating different parts of the presentation to neurological management and foot care, without a plainly documented answer about their interaction. The resulting narrative acquired professional authority. That authority shaped which questions could be regarded as already addressed and which would be re-raised only as symptoms became harder to manage.

The GP's 2019 referral is important because it resists any simplistic claim that the original question disappeared from every clinician's mind. It did not. The GP continued to connect abnormal tone and clawing with Parkinsonism. The 2023 urgent referral shows him acting forcefully and supplying a neurological pain-treatment analogy. The 2025–26 correspondence shows him willing to help but dependent on specialist guidance for further neurological prescribing. The practical constraint was not intellectual submission to the discharge letter. It was that **clinical recognition in primary care could not by itself deliver the specialist answer or treatment change**. The unresolved question remained lodged at the boundary of specialist and general care. [6][8][10]

Counsel need not argue that the clinician knew the feet were definitely dystonic in 2017. That would demand a certainty the evidence does not provide. Nor need counsel prove that peripheral neuropathy was medically impossible. Instead, the hospital should be asked to show the work: the targeted examination, discussion of dystonic posture versus mechanical or peripheral contributors, rationale for separating care pathways, and assigned follow-up. If this material exists, it can be produced and evaluated against the clinical standard of the time. If it does not, the hospital must explain why the evidence before the neurologist did not warrant such investigation or allocation. An assertion that Parkinsonism was recognised and managed carefully will not answer that narrower, original question.

The same logic governs the March 2025 response. An answer about dyskinesia risks may be excellent and still incomplete. It can justify a particular treatment decision without demonstrating the adequacy of the earlier problem-definition. The same logic governs SIMT. A multidisciplinary review may be genuine and still have an insufficiently disclosed basis for the breadth of its conclusion. The right test is not whether an impressive process existed; it is whether the process confronted the actual contested source evidence and answered the question it raised. Those are issues on which independent clinical expertise and the underlying PAR could significantly narrow or enlarge the case.

## Conclusion: the difference between an account, an answer and an owner

Return to the GP's June 2017 referral. It presented abnormal and painful feet within a progressively disabling neurological picture. Return to the hospital examination. It identified Parkinsonism, described dystonic-looking movement and treated longstanding tension. Return to the completed account. It left the cause of the feet uncertain but gave a pronounced patient-attributed separation a place beside a possible peripheral explanation. Return to the patient. He says he objected before discharge that the feet had not been adequately investigated. That objection is not preserved in the completed narrative. Return, finally, to 2025. The treating neurologist defended the logic of Parkinsonism management, and the institution concluded that care had shown no deficit.

The evidence does not compel the conclusion that every clinical decision was wrong or that anyone acted dishonestly. It supports something both more specific and more difficult to answer: **the hospital may have reached a sound diagnosis while leaving a related clinical mechanism inadequately assessed, then treated the resulting disposition as an adequate account of the whole admission.** Over the years, genuine treatment and further investigation accumulated around that disposition. When the patient challenged its origin, the defence of those later decisions could make the original gap less visible rather than close it.

The reported 10 July protest, if corroborated, sharpens that ownership question. A patient who directly tells the responsible consultant that the feet have not been investigated is not merely supplying another symptom. He is asking who will take responsibility for an apparently unfinished part of the admission. Recording his objection and naming the next clinical owner could have preserved the issue for the GP and any later reviewer even if discharge proceeded. The absence of that exchange from the completed account does not prove refusal, but it is material to whether the clinical responsibility was properly carried forward.

The final question is therefore not whether the hospital can produce an intelligent explanation for levodopa caution, for podiatry referral, or for considering neuropathy. It can. The question is whether it can point to the place where the GP's connected foot–posture–tone–pain referral received its own clinical answer—or a clear, accountable assignment for continuing inquiry, and then demonstrate **how that responsibility was discharged**. This is not a demand for diagnostic certainty; it is a demand for a traceable clinical decision about who was going to find out, how, and what was to happen if the pain persisted. **If it cannot, the case is not simply that care was imperfect. It is that the hospital's completed account may have acquired the status of an answer to a question it had never demonstrably resolved.**

That is what an independent review must test. And until it is tested, defending the authority of the record is not the same thing as defending the adequacy of the care.

## The Documentary Questions That Decide the Case

The strongest next evidence is not another retrospective opinion about the general treatment of young-onset Parkinsonism. It is source material capable of resolving these exact uncertainties:

1. **Original scope and accountable clinical ownership.** The native 29 June referral, any internal referral précis, intake screens, complete ward notes, targeted foot and movement examinations, podiatry referral and report, complex-case meeting records, medication chart and neurology discharge/follow-up plan. Identify any named neurological, podiatric or joint owner of the foot–posture relationship; the question referred to that service; required feedback to the GP; expected review date or trigger; and evidence the task was completed or reasonably closed. If no owner existed, explain why further integration was not required under the applicable 2017 standard.
2. **The July 2017 record's construction.** The three handwritten interview pages, dictated/typed drafts, audit logs and version timestamps for the history, impression and discharge correspondence; the precise evidence for “markedly divorced”; original transmissions and recipients. The 13 and 17 July timestamps alone cannot fix the date of first drafting.
3. **The reported protest.** Ward and student rosters, contemporaneous progress notes, discharge discussions, potential witness accounts and the clinician's response. These determine whether the clinician knew the patient challenged the foot investigation before authorising the final account.
4. **Longitudinal ownership and the 2023 return of responsibility.** Original April 2018 neurology note; 2019 GP and podiatry records and proof of what Neurology received; 2023 urgent referral, triage and safety records; 2024 assessment of persistent toe curling; specialist-to-GP communications. Identify what changed after each trigger, who accepted responsibility for the unresolved foot–movement mechanism, how the GP was informed, and whether the severe 2023 presentation resulted in an explicit specialist plan rather than an indefinite return to symptom treatment. The test is what happened to the clinical question, not merely whether analgesia was offered.
5. **The 2025 defence and review.** Native 19 March complaint and attachment; 25 March rebuttal and its enclosures; full PAR, terms of reference, evidence pack, reviewer identities, minutes and findings; distribution of July and August dispute notices. These establish whether the hospital independently tested the original record or relied on its conclusions.
6. **The later diagnostic reconciliation.** Native December 2025 dystonia assessment, complete 2026 PRKN laboratory report and any allelic/segregation clarification, together with the hospital's actual response. These cannot supply hindsight as a 2017 duty, but can test whether the questions visible in 2017 were subsequently revisited.
