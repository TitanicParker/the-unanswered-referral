# The Man Who Wasn't in His Medical Record

*In 2017, Liam Moloney entered a Galway teaching hospital with a disorder of movement and painfully abnormal feet. The treatment transformed his life. Eight years later, another prescription made him reconsider the history of his illness — and the account through which medicine had understood it.*

**An exclusive investigative essay | November 2026 | Unpublished editorial draft**

## I. The first 150 footsteps

There is a number in Liam Moloney's medical record that tells the reader rather more about his life than a great many of its technical descriptions. It appears in the account of a neurological consultation in March 2025, among observations about involuntary movements, prescriptions and the management of Parkinson's disease. The number is 150.

“The first 150 footsteps are torture.”

His neurologist, Dr Thomas Monaghan, was recording what it was like for Moloney to get out of bed. The man hobbled. His feet were so sore that it was difficult to tie his runners. The consultation addressed a familiar feature of his mornings: after the Parkinson's medication reached his system, the experience of the feet improved. The man who had struggled to begin his day would go on to walk over rocks on a beach. Between these two versions of the morning lay not a change in his anatomy that anyone had recorded, but a change in his experience of his body as medication began to take effect.

The record also described a less tractable aspect of his illness. Increasing a drug that made movement easier could produce excessive movement. The neurologist did not have the luxury of prescribing relief in isolation from its consequences. Moloney was relatively young for someone living with Parkinsonism, had already been treated for years, and was experiencing some dyskinesia — involuntary movements that can complicate treatment. The doctor's task was to negotiate the difference between too little movement and too much, between benefit and harm. The patient's task was to get through the day.

In March, a change was made to the night-time medication. Controlled-release Sinemet, a preparation of levodopa with carbidopa, was to be taken at bedtime. It was a modest intervention in the context of a complicated course of treatment; it was to be introduced cautiously. For Moloney, what followed was anything but modest. He dates the turning point to 14 March. Five days later, he submitted a formal complaint about his care. In a written response on 25 March, Monaghan acknowledged that the new treatment had provided “profound relief currently.” The two accounts differ in the way they present the initiation of the trial, a detail that needs to be resolved from the prescribing and communication records. They do not differ on the essential point that substantial relief had been obtained.

Moloney's complaint was not the work of a man who had spent the previous eight years maintaining a dossier on his doctor. On his account, he had done almost the opposite. He had gone to hospital in 2017, received a diagnosis of Parkinsonism, found great benefit in the first treatment and continued in the care of people whose understanding of his illness he broadly accepted. He had wanted to be well. He had not wanted to become an expert in the documentary history of his own case.

Now he began to wonder about the years for which relief had not been forthcoming. His first complaint went further than the medical evidence, taken by itself, could sustain: he believed the new response disproved the earlier explanation of his pain. A drug that relieves pain does not necessarily establish its cause. Levodopa can affect both movement and the perception of pain, and different kinds of pain can coexist in the same patient. Monaghan was to make precisely that point in his defence. It is a reasonable point, and one to which this story will return.

But a question remained. Had the feet previously improved when a neurological drug was introduced, and deteriorated when it was removed? The documents suggest that the significant observation had already been made in 2017 and recorded by the treating neurologist in April 2018.

By the time Moloney began to read his historical notes in detail, his complaint was acquiring a second subject. He was no longer looking only for an explanation of the pain. He was looking for the point at which his illness had become an account of his illness, and for the distinction between the two. He would find the origins of that account in an ordinary summer admission to a Galway hospital, and in a few lines of handwriting.

## II. Before there was a name

The beginning of a neurological illness is often recognised only after the illness has been named. A person whose feet hurt after a shift at work has an obvious explanation for their hurting. Years later, those experiences may be reconsidered as parts of a longer history, though neither patient nor doctor can be certain that every earlier complaint belonged to the disease eventually diagnosed.

Moloney's own history, as recorded in 2017, reached back to approximately 2004. He described an unusual physical tension that affected his legs and body. The history was not straightforward. He sometimes sought a posture in which he could feel tension in order to relax, a description that was paradoxical even to him. There were emotional circumstances at the time, and later accounts refer to pain in his feet beginning around 2004 or 2005. Years before any established neurological diagnosis, he had undergone treatment for foot problems, including corns, calluses and fallen arches. He had worked in bars, where prolonged standing offered an everyday explanation for painful feet.

Leaving that work in 2012 altered the balance of his symptoms. With much less standing, the pain in his feet became less prominent. The tension had not disappeared. He became more aware of it as an experience distinguishable from the pain. It is a perfectly intelligible piece of personal history, although its medical significance is not self-evident. Two sensations may be different because their causes are different; they may also be different manifestations of related processes. Reduced pressure on a painful part of the foot may relieve local pain without doing anything to the muscular forces that have contributed to the pressure. The patient's ability to distinguish the sensations is useful information. It is not a differential diagnosis.

In 2014 he presented to hospital with physical tension in his arms and legs. According to the history later taken by Monaghan, he was not seen by Neurology on that occasion. The 2017 handwritten interview recorded Moloney's belief that his present condition was the “maturation” of the earlier one. He was trying to tell the doctor a story about continuity. He had lived through the years in question. He could describe them, but he could not provide the neurological explanation for them.

It was in primary care that the separate elements of the presentation began to acquire a new significance. Dr Aaron Leo Brennan, a GP at Galway East Medical Practice, saw Moloney in the spring of 2017. The April notes include bilateral foot pain, toe abnormalities, calluses and pressure areas, and difficulty with gait. These were visible, physical problems. They were not simply a description of burning pain in feet that appeared otherwise normal. As the weeks went on, other signs became increasingly conspicuous. The patient had difficulty initiating movement. His muscle tone was abnormal. His manual dexterity was deteriorating. He was having trouble carrying out the duties of his job.

Brennan's note of 8 June contains a concise formulation of what he wanted specialist medicine to consider: “gait, feet and ‘muscle tension’.” The phrase is worth keeping in mind through the whole of the subsequent history. It is not a diagnostic assertion that all three had a single cause. It is the recognition that they were present in the same patient, at the same time, and required an opinion that could make sense of their relationship. An occupational-health physician's concern reinforced the case for referral. By 23 June the GP was recording pronounced abnormalities of tone; by 29 June he had arranged urgent hospital assessment.

The referral is an unusually useful document in a case about what information was available to whom. Brennan described a stiff, festinant gait — the small, accelerating steps associated with certain movement disorders — alongside bilateral foot problems. Moloney had himself previously attributed the way he walked to the pain from his corns and calluses. The GP recorded his difficulty relaxing the muscles in bed and that he clawed his toes. He noted increasing stiffness and loss of dexterity. He also recorded relevant alcohol history. He was concerned about a possible Parkinsonian disorder or another underlying diagnosis.

The GP was handing on a clinical question that had become too complex for the setting in which he was trying to answer it. A specialist referral exists not simply to move a patient into a different building, but to place a question within reach of a different body of expertise.

There are two representations of the question in the available hospital material. The first is the GP's actual referral. The second is a shortened hospital description of the reason for referral: a 39-year-old with difficulty initiating movement, increased tone and stiffness in all four limbs, and cogwheeling at the wrist. Cogwheeling is a ratchet-like resistance felt when a limb is moved during examination. The GP had expressed the observation cautiously — “?Some cogwheeling at the wrist.” In the hospital précis it appears without the qualification. More significant than that small change is the disappearance from the précis of the explicit painful-foot and toe-clawing history.

A précis is not obliged to reproduce a letter in full, and there is no evidence the short version was the only information the team could access. But a presentation that includes gait, feet and tone is not quite the same proposition as one primarily describing a Parkinsonian motor syndrome. That difference matters when examining the scope of the assessment that followed.

On Monday, 3 July 2017, the patient entered the hospital's specialist clinical environment. The foot history that had been omitted from the shortened referral would, in large part, be recovered by the doctor who saw him. The question is what the doctor did with it once he had recovered it.

## III. Monday in Galway

Galway University Hospitals, encompassing University Hospital Galway and Merlin Park, is a major public teaching and specialist-care service associated with what was then NUI Galway, now the University of Galway. In this institution, clinical treatment, specialist consultation, research and the education of future doctors met.

The man who took Moloney's history on 3 July was Thomas Monaghan, a consultant neurologist. In his later account to the Medical Council he described a career in neurology dating from 2002 and practice at University Hospital Galway since 2012. He was not claiming to be a specialist in every branch of movement-disorder medicine. He was a general neurologist with experience, exercising specialist responsibility for a patient and able to consult colleagues. There was relevant expertise around him. Among those colleagues was Timothy Counihan, whose public academic work in the years before the admission included Parkinson's motor manifestations, atypical Parkinsonism, gait and genetics. In June 2015, Counihan and three colleagues in Galway — Patrick Browne, Patrick Cullinane and Aoife Corcoran — had published an article on Parkinson’s genetics, including the Parkin gene. In February 2017, Counihan and Browne were among the authors of an international study on the detection of freezing of gait. The department’s interest in the genetics and mechanics of impaired movement was not an inference drawn from its reputation; it was visible in its published work. The precise academic titles held by the individual doctors in 2017 are less important than the fact of the available knowledge.

This academic context does not establish a standard of care or what any individual knew of this patient's feet. It does establish that the relevant field of knowledge was not inaccessible to medicine in Galway in 2017.

The handwritten interview of 3 July describes a man whose arms and legs were no longer synchronised when he walked, whose writing had become illegible and whose balance was deteriorating. He struggled to coordinate the handling of cardboard boxes at work. The notes move backwards through his earlier hospital attendance, the bodily tension and the history of foot treatment.

The physical examination was striking. The completed clinical account described significant rigidity and bradykinesia — slowness or difficulty in producing movement — and abnormal gait. It also described an apparent foot drop. The doctor immediately qualified that appearance: the posture was “somewhat suggestive of a dystonic posture - NOT FOOT DROP.” That observation matters because the clinician was not simply looking at a person walking badly on sore feet. He was identifying a particular abnormality of movement in the lower limb and considering its neurological nature.

Dystonia is a disorder of muscle contraction that can produce involuntary twisting, abnormal postures or movements. In a foot, it can manifest in ways that affect the toes or the direction in which the foot is held. If the posture changes the distribution of force while standing or walking, it is at least possible that it contributes to the formation or recurrence of painful pressure lesions. That is a mechanism to investigate, not a retrospective diagnosis of Moloney's feet. A person can have dystonia, fallen arches, a mechanical corn and peripheral nerve pain in combinations that are not readily disentangled. The clinical problem consists in distinguishing them, and sometimes in understanding how they interact.

The record of the admission also identifies a neurological treatment directed at the longstanding leg tension: clonazepam at night. Moloney recalls that it began on the Monday, before the senior discussion the following day. The surviving clinical text confirms inpatient commencement and records an episode of sweating after treatment, but does not independently establish the precise time of the first dose or its relationship to the meeting. That qualification should not obscure what the documented prescription signifies. A symptom going back years, which the man had struggled to explain, was now a symptom for which the neurological service was taking therapeutic responsibility.

The admission included extensive investigation. Imaging, laboratory studies, a dopamine transporter scan — generally referred to as a DaTscan — and collegiate discussion all formed part of a serious attempt to establish the nature of the movement disorder. A DaTscan can help assess whether there is a loss of function in the dopamine-related system implicated in degenerative Parkinsonism; it does not tell a doctor the cause of each pain in a patient's feet. The team came to suspect young-onset Parkinson's disease, possibly a genetic form. The diagnosis was important, and the fact of the investigation should be acknowledged without reservation.

One of the decision-making events identified in the record was the Neurology Complex Case Meeting. The patient places it early on Tuesday, 4 July. Its exact date, attendance, presentation materials and discussion are not independently established by minutes in the disclosed archive. The completed account confirms that colleagues considered the case. Monaghan's later correspondence also associates Counihan with a preference to postpone the introduction of levodopa if practicable. The advice was directed towards a genuine problem in the management of younger patients with Parkinsonism: how to obtain symptom relief without bringing forward difficult treatment complications, including dyskinesias and motor fluctuations. The preferred initial approach used alternatives such as rasagiline and procyclidine.

There was nothing irrational in recognising that a patient of 39 presented a different set of treatment problems from a patient presenting in old age. But the decision about how and when to introduce dopamine replacement was not, in itself, a decision about the cause of painful toe-clawing. To decide that one drug should be deferred does not necessarily decide whether a particular painful posture is dystonic, whether recurrent corns have a motor contribution, or which discipline should assess that contribution. A multidisciplinary decision can be authoritative in relation to the question actually put to it and silent in relation to a question that was not put to it.

What, then, was supplied to the meeting? Did its advice address painful lower-limb posturing, or chiefly the management of young-onset Parkinsonism? Without knowing the scope of the discussion, one cannot know the scope of the authority its conclusions should be accorded.

Had a different doctor taken the history on Monday, or somebody put a different question to the meeting, the plan might have been different. That counterfactual cannot establish that an earlier discharge would have been safe. But an eventual clinical trajectory is not inevitable simply because it has been authorised. The questions clinicians ask help determine the answers they find.

By the following Monday, according to Moloney, he had become concerned that an important part of the question had survived the investigation. The man who had come to hospital with painful feet and an abnormal way of walking believed that he was about to leave it with a diagnosis of Parkinsonism, but without a satisfactory answer concerning the feet.

## IV. The second Monday

What happened on 10 July 2017 is not established by an independent contemporary account. It is told by Moloney, who says he was there, in a later signed statement and in his account of the admission. It is a scene at the centre of his case and, for that reason, it deserves to be told with the limits of the evidence visible.

He recalls learning that his discharge was imminent. He had been in hospital for a week. He approached four medical students and asked that the consultant be summoned. When Monaghan came, Moloney says, he challenged the adequacy of the assessment of his feet. He recalls using a comparison with the National Car Test, the NCT: what was the point of a comprehensive inspection if the bald tyres, the very problem that had drawn attention to the vehicle, had not been addressed? He was not proposing that an automobile and the human nervous system were analogous in any serious scientific respect. He was looking for a way of making a simple objection intelligible to people who had just completed a complicated investigation.

The analogy has one advantage over the retrospective language of medical complaints. It conveys what a patient might be capable of knowing before he possesses the clinical knowledge that will be needed to explain his symptoms. He could not know which gene was implicated. He could not necessarily distinguish neuropathy from dystonia, or be expected to decide what drugs ought to be prescribed. What he could recognise was the difference between the set of problems that had brought him to the hospital and the problem he understood the hospital to have solved.

He recalls the consultant responding in a way that directed the matter towards podiatry. The exact exchange, and the doctor's recollection of it, are not available from any independent source in the archive. The four students, if they can be identified, are a potential source of evidence. Their presence is also relevant to the setting. A teaching hospital is an institution in which clinical judgment is observed and acquired by people learning to become doctors. If the encounter happened as described, it was a moment in which a patient's objection to the adequacy of his care arose in the course of that education. No conclusion about what the students saw, understood or learned should be drawn without speaking to them.

That timing is what gives the episode its importance. There are many ways in which a clinical question can be obscured over a week of investigations. A shortened referral may give prominence to some features rather than others. A complex movement disorder may command most of a consultant's attention. A patient may struggle to articulate a history that extends over a decade. None of that requires an explanation in terms of bad faith. It describes the ordinary conditions under which medicine is practised. But a patient drawing attention to an apparent discrepancy, before the disposition of the case has become final, is something more than another symptom in the history. He is providing feedback on the work that has been done.

It would have been possible for a doctor to disagree with the objection and still take it seriously. He could have explained why the foot findings had been adequately assessed; he could have explained that their cause remained uncertain and why no further inpatient testing was justified. He could have examined them again, or sought another opinion, or made a specific arrangement for continuing assessment. Some of those responses might have been more appropriate than others. The point is not to prescribe a consultation from a distance. It is that a challenge to a proposed discharge can be an occasion for clinical reasoning rather than an obstacle to the completion of paperwork.

The complete admission account available for examination does not contain an identifiable description of the 10 July exchange or a targeted response to the objection as Moloney describes it. That is a finding about the text. It does not establish that no conversation occurred, or that another note containing it does not exist. Neither does it establish that a paragraph was written after the protest for the purpose of defeating it. The account was confirmed on 17 July, after the discharge on 11 July, but confirmation is not the same thing as first composition. The electronic history of its creation and amendment would be important evidence on that point.

A contemporary record has enormous evidential advantages over a memory recovered years later. But the author chooses which observations to preserve; the person being recorded generally has no opportunity to put his own contemporaneous account beside it. Where the dispute concerns the process of recording, silence in the record cannot be an independent resolution of that dispute.

If the encounter occurred, the facts known on the second Monday differed from those known on the first. There was now, in addition to the referral and examination, an explicit objection to the sufficiency of the investigation. That is a new circumstance in which the standard of care would have to be assessed.

To see the importance of that objection, one must examine what the doctor actually wrote.

## V. The record before the record

The early history is about loss of function and a sensation of tension. There is an earlier presentation to hospital. There is a belief that the condition has developed over time. Then there is an account of the feet: corns, calluses, fallen arches, treatment in earlier years. The change of occupation in 2012 is followed by a description of a change in the relative prominence of two experiences. The line on which much of the later dispute turns is legible in its essential wording: “Began to be aware that foot pain and leg tension weren't the same”. The photographed page also contains a parenthetical reference to leg pain settling. The exact punctuation and arrangement of the handwriting should be preserved in a definitive transcription; it is not necessary to resolve every uncertain letter to recognise the distinction the doctor has recorded.

Now compare the typed clinical history. Here the observation has become a complete explanatory sentence: “Therefore in 2012 he began to realise that the foot pain and the leg tension were not the same problem because: as the leg pain settled --- he was more aware of the tension”. The connective words — therefore, because — belong to the typed account. They organise the material into an explanation of the patient's recognition of something. The phrase “not the same problem” has a slightly different resonance from the statement that two sensations are not the same.

Clinical notes are routinely expanded from shorthand; the handwriting is not itself a verbatim transcript. The question is not whether transformation took place, but whether its effect was faithful to the information on which the clinician relied.

In the clinical Impression, the distinction assumes a different character. Monaghan is considering an explanation for the leg symptoms, including corns, calluses and burning. They “may have been completely separate or perhaps a secondary peripheral neuropathy”, the account says, perhaps associated in some way with earlier substantial alcohol consumption. The paragraph is explicitly tentative. It concludes, in part, “Not clear”. It is followed by this statement: “However he has in his own mind - markedly divorced the feelings of tension in the legs which was/is the predominant problem -from the ‘corns and callouses’ issues and Pains in the feet”.

There are three things happening in the passage. The clinician is entertaining a clinical possibility. He is characterising the patient's understanding of the relationship between the symptoms. And he is assessing their relative importance. The first is a matter for neurological reasoning. The second involves a factual representation of another person's position. The third involves an evaluative judgment that might be informed by both clinical findings and the patient's account. They are related but not interchangeable. If the patient is represented as strongly separating the problems, that representation can appear to lend weight to the clinician's hypothesis of separation. Yet the two propositions originate in the same clinician-mediated encounter. They are not independent corroboration of each other.

The sensation of pressure on a sore toe is different from the sensation of muscle tension. It does not follow that the forces affecting the toe have nothing to do with the muscles. Equally, a patient with Parkinsonism may have a separate peripheral neuropathy. The description of the sensations cannot decide either proposition.

The qualifying language in Monaghan's Impression deserves to be taken seriously. It is incorrect to say that the document definitively diagnosed alcoholic neuropathy. It does not. A significant alcohol history is medically relevant and an alternative peripheral explanation was reasonable to entertain. The record contains a further point worth examining: the typed history records that there was no improvement off alcohol, whereas the Impression, in the discussion of a possibly alcohol-associated peripheral process, includes the words “Not clear as this has improved.” It is not clear what “this” refers to or when the improvement occurred. The two statements cannot fairly be described as an established contradiction about the same symptom over the same period. But the account does not supply the explanatory connection that would make them easy to reconcile. That is a question for the author, not a gap for the journalist to fill with conjecture.

The Impression also acknowledges that the corns and calluses remain a major problem but says that “in reality” they are much less of an issue because the patient has been on his feet less. That clinical assessment of their relative importance requires reconciliation with the more recent deterioration documented by the GP, rather than only the improvement that followed the change of occupation in 2012.

In medicine, uncertainty is an entirely respectable outcome of an admission. A hospital is not required to settle every question before a patient leaves. The important practical considerations are the magnitude of the uncertainty, its possible consequences and the arrangements for dealing with it. Monaghan's extensive recommendations dealt with medication, further assessment and support. Podiatry was a meaningful part of the disposition; the GP had also pursued priority foot care during the admission. It would be wrong to suggest that nothing was arranged for the feet. But treating a corn or a callus is not necessarily the same as investigating the forces that cause it to recur. The reviewed record does not clearly identify the person or service tasked with reconciling the neurological observations and the painful-foot findings, or the clinical process by which that reconciliation would be made.

The final plan in the longer account refers the reader backwards: “As per above.” If the unresolved question was intended to be pursued after discharge, the reader needs to locate the responsibility for pursuing it. If it was regarded as sufficiently resolved for clinical purposes, the reader needs to know how.

An admission offers a historical opportunity to observe pain, posture, pressure, movement and the effects of treatment under particular circumstances. Which observations were reasonably indicated is a question for independent clinical expertise. But those circumstances cannot now be reproduced. If relevant observations were never made, later knowledge cannot recover them; if they were not transmitted, they may be unavailable to the clinician relying on the surviving account.

This is a peculiar form of evidential disadvantage. The patient who later alleges that an important part of his illness was insufficiently assessed may need the very information that an adequate assessment would have generated. If the historical examination was insufficient, the lack of a record of it may make it more difficult to demonstrate the significance of the insufficiency. That does not establish that anyone intended the absence. It explains why the question of what was assessed, and what was recorded, matters independently of anyone's motives.

The representation did not remain within the hospital. On 14 July the GP was still awaiting formal correspondence; on 17 July the available primary-care record again refers to its absence. On 18 July, Brennan recorded extensive correspondence from Monaghan, a diagnosis of early Parkinsonism, positive scan and clinical findings, with further testing awaited. Parkinson's disease entered the active diagnostic history. The available record does not establish that every page was transmitted on the same date or that the GP accepted every proposition in it. It establishes something important nevertheless: the specialist account had become part of the information on which the continuing care of the patient would proceed.

A medical record has a prospective function: it helps determine what the reader does tomorrow. A later clinician sees the consultant's account of the patient's understanding, not necessarily the line of handwriting from which it was developed. If the representation is sound, this process is useful. If materially incomplete, its authority may travel further than its evidential foundation.

Moloney did not spend the next months examining that distinction. He had a neurological illness, a course of treatment and a reason to expect his life to improve. And it did.

## VI. The year medicine worked

In September 2017, Monaghan obtained a further specialist opinion from Daniel Healy, now listed as a professor and consultant neurologist at Beaumont Hospital in Dublin. Healy’s professional work in Parkinson’s genetics long predated this consultation: in 2008 he had led a major international study of LRRK2-associated Parkinson’s disease published in The Lancet Neurology. LRRK2 is not the gene eventually implicated in Moloney’s illness, but the work illustrates the expertise to which the Galway team had turned. The point of approaching an external expert was not to ask somebody unfamiliar with the field to endorse a local decision. It was to engage expertise relevant to an unusually young presentation and an uncertain cause. Monaghan had expressed the Galway preference in a memorable line: “Myself and Tim are keen to hold back on Levodopa insofar as maybe possible unless you'd advise otherwise?”

The legible parts of Healy's letter of 12 September contain a specific proposal: “I think probably has Park II and I would suggest testing that initially. If it is negative I would go on to test a panel.” PARK2 is the older designation associated with the Parkin gene, now generally referred to as PRKN. The specialist had identified a plausible genetic explanation and suggested a route by which it might be tested. He also discussed the management of young patients with dopamine replacement and the difficulties that can accompany excessive treatment. It would misrepresent his opinion to turn that discussion into an instruction that Moloney must immediately be put on levodopa. It is entirely possible for two doctors to agree about a suspected genetic disease while having different approaches to its treatment. The complete letter and the information Healy was supplied are important for establishing the intended scope of the advice.

But the letter records something that has a more immediate bearing on the patient's life: a dramatic motor improvement on the existing treatment with procyclidine and rasagiline. Here was medicine performing its proper work. A man whose ability to walk and work had been deteriorating was experiencing an extraordinary change. He did not need to know whether the explanation would eventually be identified as PRKN-related disease to appreciate the fact that he was getting better.

Procyclidine is an anticholinergic drug. Its effects on the systems involved in movement are different from those of levodopa, and its use can be limited by side-effects. Rasagiline, another drug in the initial regimen, acts on dopamine metabolism. There is no need to assume that procyclidine was solely responsible for all the improvement. The withdrawal of that particular drug, however, supplied an opportunity to observe what happened when one component of the treatment was taken away.

In September or October the patient was advised to interrupt procyclidine because of problems with his vision. The episode has two particularly useful documentary traces. The first was written at the time, before the later complaint was imaginable. In an email of 4 October, Moloney contrasted his experience on treatment with his experience during the interruption. “My walk is back together,” he wrote of the improvement. Of the deterioration, he wrote: “I am feeling the tension building up, my feet are restless and my walk is falling apart....but my eyesight is back to normal.”

An October communication to his employer supplies a similar contemporaneous self-report, not a second clinical observer. Its value lies in its date: the patient was writing about his treatment while it was happening, for a purpose unrelated to the eventual dispute.

The second trace is in a consultation with Monaghan on 24 April 2018. The neurologist recorded the history of stopping the medication in the autumn. One detail is quite extraordinary in its relevance to the original referral: “the first thing he noticed changing and changing rapidly was the return on the foot, of the outside left corn.”

It is important to understand precisely what kind of evidence this is. The clinician recorded the patient's report. There is no measurement of the size of a corn immediately before and after withdrawal, nor proof that a skin lesion formed afresh in a matter of hours. The later recollection is that the symptoms in the region of the longstanding outside-left-foot lesion returned. The particular wording of the clinical record must be examined with the author rather than silently corrected to accommodate either interpretation. What the note establishes is that the doctor received and preserved information about a rapid change at a specific foot site associated, in the patient's history, with the removal of a neurological medication.

In July, Monaghan had expressed uncertainty about the connection between painful feet and abnormal movement. He now had a report linking a change in neurological treatment to a change at a particular foot site. It might be explained by altered tone, gait, pressure or pain perception, or a combination. The significance of the observation was that it was relevant to the uncertainty already recorded, and ought to be considered when later management was reviewed.

The 2018 consultation is not a record of failure. It contains a doctor and patient discussing the considerable benefits of treatment, its limitations and possible ways of increasing those benefits. Moloney described the relief from tension as fantastic, and spoke of the pleasure of being able to walk a short distance without it. He had foot cramps and other difficulties; toe supports helped. The consultation was detailed and attentive. There were reasons to be careful about more procyclidine because of the visual effects. There were also reasons, in Monaghan's judgment, not to introduce levodopa or another dopamine agonist too quickly. “Obviously he could be very much better if we would actually give him the levodopa or another dopamine agonist,” the neurologist wrote, before explaining that “we do not want to do that – at least for quite some time.”

The question of genetics was raised again. Monaghan acknowledged that he and Healy suspected PARK2/PRKN. He explained his own preference for delaying testing, partly because he did not think it would alter current management and partly because more developed genetic knowledge might make later sequencing more useful. He also offered to arrange the focused test or panel if Moloney wanted it. The discussion is important evidence against a crude account in which the genetic possibility was simply suppressed. The alternative existed, was discussed in the correspondence and was available to the patient. The question for an independent specialist is whether clarification would have been clinically useful then, including for the interpretation of the foot symptoms, rather than merely interesting for its own sake.

In Moloney’s present account, the early treatment had also relieved the longstanding foot pain; he remembers 2018 as the best physical condition he had experienced since 2004. That retrospective description is consistent with the substantial improvement documented in the earlier specialist correspondence and in the April consultation, though it should not be mistaken for a formal outcome score. It is the point at which the story changes direction. The problem for the years that follow is no longer simply that medicine might have failed to establish an explanation in July. Medicine had acquired useful information by treating the man. It had found a way of making his condition considerably more tolerable, and had received an indication of what happened when the treatment was interrupted.

A good result is a clinical achievement. It is also information to carry forward. When the drug that produces it becomes difficult to tolerate, the problem is how to preserve the benefit within the constraints of safe treatment. Parkinsonism can worsen under good care; the relevant question is what the history of therapeutic responsiveness contributes to the management of that deterioration.

Moloney went forward in the ordinary way of a person who finds that treatment helps him. The doctor had the record. The patient had his life.

## VII. The years between

Take the feet. The podiatry record documents recurrent lesions in the region of the left fifth metatarsophalangeal joint, near the outside of the forefoot. There were treatments to remove or redistribute pressure, insoles, toe supports and other interventions. Clawing was recorded. These notes are evidence of care, and of the persistence of physical abnormalities. They are also evidence of a part of the patient's illness being observed by a discipline different from the one responsible for prescribing neurological medication.

In March 2019, Brennan again referred Moloney for podiatric attention. The language of that referral is striking for anyone who imagines the GP had simply adopted a view of the feet as a disease apart from the movement disorder. He associated the foot problems with increased tone and abnormal Parkinsonian gait, and referred to clawing and pain. The available transcription even includes a tick against the absence of neuropathy in the podiatric risk classification; that is not the equivalent of a specialist nerve-conduction investigation, and cannot rule out a peripheral disorder. But the GP's formulation is clear enough. The connection he had wanted investigated in 2017 remained alive in his understanding of the patient's condition.

It is not established that Monaghan received the 2019 re-referral or the whole community podiatry record. Information can exist in one part of a healthcare service without being transmitted to another. That is why actual delivery and incorporation of information matter.

Other observations were indisputably within the treating neurologist's own record. In October 2020 he described the apparent foot-drop-like throw of the lower limb as Parkinsonian, noting normal power in the relevant movements. He considered the quality of the gait generally good despite some abnormal movement in the feet, and noted their sensitivity and urge to move them. The consultation also addressed the limitations of existing treatment and the possible further use of a dopamine agonist. The record does not depict a patient abandoned to his illness. It depicts ongoing specialist management, with a neurological interpretation of particular foot movements.

By 2021 the difficulty of obtaining sufficient benefit from the existing regimen, including the problems associated with procyclidine, was becoming more pressing. Levodopa was introduced. In subsequent years doses and preparations were adjusted, including Stalevo, which combines levodopa and carbidopa with entacapone. The precise chronology of individual prescriptions should be checked against the dispensing and clinic records; the central fact is that the original strategy of postponement had given way to treatment with the very class of medication the doctors had been cautious about starting. In other words, the 2017 advice was not an immutable prohibition. The therapeutic regime adapted as the illness and tolerability changed.

The question is how the information from 2017 and 2018 about the modifiability of the feet was used in that adaptation. An expert would have to distinguish old pain from new pain, the effects of illness from those of treatment, and pain produced by abnormal movement from pain exacerbated by reduced motor control.

The story becomes darker in March 2023, not because the patient has begun to suspect his doctor, but because his condition has become very difficult to live with. On 26 March the GP issued an urgent referral to Neurology describing severe burning pain in the feet and lower legs, and distress sufficiently serious for him to record that Moloney expressed a wish for death. It is impossible to read that referral merely as another entry in a list of consultations. The safety of the man, as well as the explanation of his symptoms, was at issue. The GP had initiated pregabalin, a drug used for certain types of pain. The dose had been increased considerably. In the referral, Brennan also referred to earlier improvement in pain with better control of Parkinsonism.

This last detail matters almost as much as the urgency of the language. The GP was not only seeking help with severe pain. He was supplying information that might affect the specialist understanding of its relationship to neurological treatment. The movement-disorder nurse subsequently communicated concerns to the consultant, according to the correspondence indexed in the archive. In September 2023 a nursing communication stated: “It sounds like his Parkinson’s is being undertreated and the leg pain and shuffling is off-related.” In Parkinson's management, an OFF period is one in which the benefit of medication is reduced or has worn off and symptoms re-emerge. The suggestion was specific. It was another occasion on which a possible relationship between medication state, impaired movement and painful legs had entered clinical communication.

There are limitations to what can be concluded about the 2023 crisis. The archive does not contain the complete native urgent-referral pack, the full immediate risk-management record or every part of the correspondence. It would be improper to assert that no safety assessment or intervention occurred. There was further specialist care. Monaghan's 2023 consultation considered the difficulties in considerable detail, including possible OFF dystonia, the relationship between walking and medication, and the patient's report that pregabalin had helped. He considered other possible causes, including a peripheral pain process and the significance of the sudden severe exacerbation. Other investigations were contemplated or pursued. He did not dismiss the entire illness as psychological, and he took account of the complexity of interpreting symptoms in the presence of other medications and alcohol. These are material facts, not incidental qualifications to be tucked away from the reader.

One explanation increasingly prominent in the neurologist's account was the effect of Parkinson's disease on the experience of pain from other causes. When motor treatment wears off, he explained, pain that originates elsewhere may become more severe. Giving levodopa may make such pain more tolerable without treating its underlying anatomical cause. In this understanding, the patient's response to neurological medication is quite compatible with the presence of a separate peripheral or mechanical source of pain. The explanation is clinically plausible. It is not defeated merely by pointing to the fact of pain relief.

Its adequacy as an explanation of this individual history depends on more than its general plausibility, however. The man's feet did not present exclusively as painful, normally positioned extremities. The history included clawing, observed abnormal movement, recurrent focal pressure lesions, and changes in symptoms with alterations in neurological medication. Pain amplification and a motor contribution to the production of pain need not be mutually exclusive. The question is whether the latter possibility was adequately assessed, and whether there was an explanation that connected the observations rather than leaving them in separate parts of the record. A reader can accept the doctor's account of OFF-related pain amplification and still ask where the possible contribution of abnormal foot posture to the underlying pain was examined.

By 2024 the records indicate that pregabalin was providing substantial benefit, although toe curling remained a problem. Moloney subsequently reduced the drug. The reasons for the reduction, including his broader sense of improvement and the discussions he had with his GP, appear in the 2025 consultation. His foot pain was severe again. In March, Monaghan noted the response when the Parkinson's medication reached his system. He discussed the possibility of increasing treatment and the risks of doing so, the merits of pregabalin, and the patient's own suggestion that the absence of medication overnight might be important. The bedtime controlled-release prescription followed.

A patient described the time at which his symptoms were worst. His doctor considered the pharmacological circumstances of that time. Treatment was adjusted, with considerable benefit. The question is why that relief had proved difficult to achieve earlier, in light of the information already available.

That does not mean prescribing a particular drug in 2017 would necessarily have prevented the crisis of 2023. It means the adequacy of later management should be considered in the light of what the earlier treatment had taught medicine about this particular patient.

In the spring of 2025, Moloney began to ask questions of that kind. The answers would introduce him to an institution rather different from the clinical service he had been attending.

## VIII. The price of reconsideration

The letter Moloney submitted on 19 March 2025 raised concerns about diagnostic anchoring, failure to reassess and the possible effect of hierarchy on treatment choices. It was explicit that he was not presuming malice or individual intent. The historical records had not yet been systematically examined by him. The initial complaint was made in the light of his experience of relief, and contained the understandable but medically overconfident conclusion that the relief disproved the earlier working explanation. It sought an independent review and disclosure of the records.

Monaghan's response of 25 March made a substantial defence. The risks of dyskinesia in a young patient were real, the neurologist explained. He had tried to postpone treatment with levodopa but had used it for some years, and its effects on pain had been recognised. The pain was not necessarily caused by alcohol-related peripheral neuropathy; Parkinsonism could amplify pain of other origins. Pregabalin had been useful, though incompletely so, and had been reduced by the patient. The recent adjustment of Sinemet had produced the profound relief that was at the centre of the complaint. The doctor expressed sympathy for what Moloney had endured.

There is one passage from the earlier clinical correspondence that Monaghan reproduced in his response. It stated that the patient had a good understanding of the reasons the doctors wished to defer levodopa and contemplated the possibility that, in time, “we may regret that we did not start sooner.” This is a striking acknowledgement of uncertainty in advance of the outcome. It is not an admission of negligence. It shows that the original strategy was understood to involve a balance of competing possibilities, including the possibility that earlier treatment might have been beneficial. The proper retrospective question is not whether it was conceivable that the doctors would regret the decision. They had themselves considered that possibility. It is what later evidence was used to decide whether the time for revising the balance had arrived.

Understanding why a clinician wishes to defer levodopa is not the same as believing the whole presenting problem has been adequately investigated. The account of understanding cannot, by itself, answer the reported objection about the feet.

The role of the senior colleague became a separate point of dispute. In 2017 Monaghan had told Healy of the inclination he and Tim shared regarding levodopa. In May 2025 Counihan replied to Moloney: “As I have not been involved in your care, I have forwarded your communication to your consultant Dr Thomas Monaghan”. On 29 July the Medical Council notified Moloney of a decision not to investigate his complaint about Counihan, referring to his not being directly involved in the patient's care and finding no clinical concern in relation to him that would reach the relevant threshold. The decision was a significant outcome for Counihan and must be reported as such. The separate Monaghan complaint was identified for further handling; the available decision does not establish its eventual resolution.

Monaghan’s own submission to the Medical Council described responsibility for the treatment as his and indicated that Counihan’s input had not added to what he already understood. He said that his strategy had been sincerely intended to protect the patient from treatment complications, and that he had asked the hospital to furnish Moloney with his complete working file and unedited response to the complaint. He defended the clinical choices and wrote, with a candour worth preserving, “I accept I may be wrong - but I think and hope that my management was correct.” A doctor may sincerely hold that position. The question of whether his management was correct is quite different from the question of whether he sincerely believed it to be so.

Collegial advice is not necessarily direct patient care. That distinction does not determine what information an adviser received, what influence an opinion had or how its scope was understood. The later accounts may be entirely compatible; the original meeting evidence would help establish that.

The hospital's complaints machinery had meanwhile taken a different route. The Quality and Patient Safety Department initially classified the matter as one involving clinical judgment and referred it back to the clinician for his observations. In a letter of 30 May, Aoife Nolan, the coordinator and complaints officer, informed Moloney that, because he remained dissatisfied, his case would be considered through the hospital's Incident Management Framework. A Preliminary Assessment Report was being completed for the Serious Incident Management Team. On 13 June she explained the distinction between a complaint about clinical judgment and a separate incident-management review, and said the latter would be undertaken independently. She also asked him to refrain from forwarding more material for the time being, citing the quantity of correspondence and her workload. Her stated role was to coordinate the process, not to make its clinical findings.

The report was presented on 27 August. On 3 September, a letter signed by Nolan and Professor Tim O’Brien, Clinical Director for Medicine, communicated the outcome of the multidisciplinary review. O’Brien is an established professor of medicine and consultant endocrinologist; his clinical and academic work had been recognised by the Royal College of Physicians of Ireland with its Bryan Alton Medal in 2023. The meeting was chaired, according to the outcome letter, by the regional clinical director for Quality and Patient Safety, Dr Ramona McLoughlin (whose first name is rendered differently in the available transcription). The review found “no deficit in the care provided”. Its disclosed rationale identified the risks of early Sinemet, consideration of alternative diagnoses and the introduction of Sinemet in 2021. It is the scope of the reasoning behind that conclusion, as much as its authority, that matters. The difficulty is that the complete Preliminary Assessment Report, its source inventory, the precise matters put to its authors and the material recording the deliberation have not been available for examination in this investigation. The independence of a review is not a matter of its title alone. It is a quality of the work done, including the independence with which the assertions of interested parties are tested against their evidential origins.

Here the dispute begins to acquire a significance beyond the fate of a particular prescription. There is a considerable difference between the cost of reconsidering an uncertain clinical question in July 2017 and the cost of reconsidering it after eight years of management and a review undertaken in response to a complaint. The cost is not necessarily a sum of money. It includes clinical time, organisational work, the potential effects on professional reputations, the scrutiny of earlier practices and the possible need to revise a governance decision. There may also be implications for legal liability, though none has been established by the documents examined here.

Professional pride can be an asset to an institution. It can make clinicians diligent, conscientious and unwilling to do inadequate work. An institution's wish to use its resources well can be entirely consistent with good public service. The question is whether these interests remain subordinate to the possibility of correction when the correction may be costly to both. It is a question about the design of accountability, not a claim that the people in this case acted out of pride or financial calculation. The documents do not establish such a motive.

The institutional risk arises when the defence of an earlier decision depends on the reliability of the account produced by that decision, and reopening the account also means reconsidering the work of those who subsequently validated it. Preventing that risk requires going behind the authorised account to its sources.

By September 2025, Moloney had begun to ask for just that.

## IX. A record that judges itself

On 6 September 2025 the hospital's subject access coordinator, Nuala Madden, communicated a decision on Moloney's request regarding clinical records. The letter referred to an application for erasure, while reproducing requests to rectify and annotate aspects of the record. Its substantive reasoning was that a historical medical opinion remains an accurate record of the fact that the opinion was held at a particular time, even if it is later superseded. Such opinions are valuable to clinicians precisely because they explain the basis on which earlier decisions were made. The hospital considered preservation necessary for continuity of care and refused the request.

There is much to be said for the principle. A record of a doctor believing something in 2017 should not be rewritten as though the doctor had believed something different. It would be a loss of valuable historical information to replace a provisional diagnosis with a later diagnosis and obliterate the development of the clinician's thinking. A patient is not entitled to a retrospectively idealised medical history merely because the original opinions proved mistaken. The difficulty is that a dispute about the accuracy of a factual statement attributed to a patient is not necessarily the same kind of dispute as one about the clinical opinion of a doctor.

If the disputed words faithfully captured the patient's understanding, they would be evidence of it. If they were instead an interpretation that went beyond his statement, they would be evidence of the clinician's interpretation. And if he later objected to the adequacy of the investigation, the record should not be treated as necessarily conveying his whole position merely because it contains an earlier characterisation of it.

The distinction is vital because the medical record has two lives. During treatment, it is an instrument for organising knowledge about a patient and deciding what to do with him. After a complaint, it can be evidence explaining why the treatment was appropriate. It is quite capable of performing both functions properly, and ordinarily does. But if a material part of the representation is disputed, a reviewer cannot determine its accuracy simply by showing that treatment proceeded in accordance with it. Consistency between the record and the actions it generated proves consistency; it does not, without more, prove that the underlying representation was sound.

There is no basis in the available documents for asserting that the entire record was deliberately made to look reassuring, or that the hospital has relied exclusively on its own notes to resolve the complaint. The absence of the underlying review materials prevents that conclusion. The risk of circularity, however, is identifiable. If the 2017 note is treated as a sufficient description of the clinical question; if the later care is considered appropriate in light of that description; and if the adequacy of the description is inferred from the consistency of the care, nothing in the process need independently test the proposition that initiated it.

On 26 January 2026, correspondence on behalf of Quality and Patient Safety maintained that a diagnosis of dystonia in December 2025 did not undermine the previous review, which was properly concerned with the contemporaneous information available to clinicians. Updated diagnoses, it said, did not require a retrospective amendment of the record or the reopening of the governance process. There is a principle in that response with which a fair inquiry should agree: earlier medical care should not be judged solely by what was learned later. But the most important evidence raised by Moloney's historical case is evidence of what was already available to the clinicians in 2017 and 2018. The dystonic-looking gait, the painful clawing feet, the response to treatment, the withdrawal episode: none depended for its existence on a diagnosis made in December 2025.

On 4 February 2026, a letter to Brennan from Seán O’Riordan, a consultant neurologist at St Vincent’s University Hospital in Dublin with subspecialist expertise in movement disorders including dystonia, reported the result of genetic testing. Two pathogenic variants of the Parkin gene, compound heterozygous, accounted for the patient's Parkinsonism and dystonia. This supplied a degree of etiological certainty of a kind that had not existed in the earlier record. The opinion expressed by Healy in 2017 now appeared in a new light. The diagnosis does not establish that every painful lesion Moloney ever had was caused by dystonia, or that the doctors were obliged to have obtained that result in 2017. It does invite a careful examination of the relevance of a known possibility to the decisions that were made while it remained unconfirmed.

The patient's later information requests were directed more specifically at the objects upon which the story depended. On 31 August 2026 he sought the provenance of the historical clinical synthesis and the records underlying the hospital review. On 2 September he sought to have a signed supplementary statement durably attached to the disputed clinical history rather than to have the historical document destroyed. The Data Protection Commission had correspondence concerning the matter; the archive records further complaints in October. No final finding of an infringement by the Commission is established in the material reviewed. What the requests illuminate is the difference between altering history and allowing a disputed history to be seen in its proper evidential context.

Moloney is not entitled to have every disagreement determined in his favour. Establishing clinical negligence would require independent expert assessment of the applicable standard, conduct and causation. A dispute over attributed personal information raises distinct issues. His practical difficulty, however, is that the less complete the original representation, the harder it may be to demonstrate that incompleteness.

For most of the years now under examination, he was not keeping a parallel record to test the authorised account. That is an ordinary condition of the trust on which medicine depends. The better conclusion is not that every patient should attend a consultation as a prospective litigant, but that the system should be capable of independently testing its own account when a substantive challenge is made.

## X. The unanswered question

The most revealing document in this story may not be the one announcing the genetic result. It may be the much less remarkable note written by a GP on 8 June 2017. There was no established diagnosis in it, no claim to specialised neurological expertise, no prediction of a course of treatment. There was an interest in an opinion about three things: gait, feet and muscle tension.

Around that formulation accumulated an admission, a specialist diagnosis, collegial advice, an external opinion, years of prescriptions, podiatric treatment, a severe deterioration and further relief. Then came the formal complaint, professional defence, incident-management review, regulatory correspondence and genetic confirmation.

All this activity achieved a great deal. It gave Moloney a diagnosis and, at times, extraordinary improvement. What is not identifiable in the material examined here is a sufficiently clear answer to a more particular question: how the relationship between his painful, clawing feet and his movement disorder was assessed, resolved or assigned for continuing investigation, and how subsequent evidence informed its reconsideration.

There is a date in July when, on his account, the patient tried to put that question to the hospital himself. There is a date in April 2018 when the doctor's own notes show that the feet had entered the account of the response to treatment. Those dates matter quite independently of the genetic confirmation. They are opportunities to examine what medicine knew when its decisions were being made, rather than only what it knows now.

In a sense, the story began with one man and has ended with two representations of him. There is the man living with a body which had given him pleasure, difficulty and pain, capable at one time of remarkable improvement and at another of immense distress. There is the authoritative clinical account by which others came to understand that body. The two must correspond closely enough for treatment to meet his needs. They must also correspond closely enough for a subsequent review of that treatment to be meaningful.

The hospital's records may ultimately explain why Liam Moloney was treated as he was. The question that remains is whether they adequately establish why he ought to have been treated that way.

It was his GP who had asked medicine to consider the relationship between the feet, the gait and the tension in his muscles. It was the patient, he says, who tried to ask again before leaving hospital. And it is still possible, nearly a decade later, to ask for the dated record in which that question received its answer.
