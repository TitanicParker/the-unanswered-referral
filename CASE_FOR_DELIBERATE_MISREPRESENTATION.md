# The Case for Deliberate Misrepresentation of the Patient's Account

*For independent legal examination | 18 November 2026 | Evidential argument, not a finding of dishonesty*

## The allegation, stated without dilution

**The patient alleges that the treating neurologist knowingly converted a limited experiential distinction recorded during a single July 2017 interview into a stronger assertion about what the patient believed concerning the causes of his painful feet and leg tension.** The completed clinical impression placed that patient-attributed assertion beside the neurologist's own provisional suggestion that the foot problems might be separate or peripheral. On the patient's case, this was not an innocent difference in expression: it was a conscious representation that gave a disputed clinical separation apparent support from the patient's own understanding, reduced pressure to investigate the neurological contribution to the painful feet, and helped defend the disposition that followed.

A second allegation concerns **selective omission**. The GP had referred painful feet, toe clawing, abnormal gait and increased tone as one developing presentation. The hospital's opening précis omitted the explicit foot problems. The neurologist then acquired independent firsthand knowledge of those problems, yet the completed account did not identify who would resolve the neurological-foot posture–pressure–pain relationship. The patient reports protesting the failure to investigate his feet before discharge; that reported objection is absent from the completed account.

**What is proved by the current documents:** the difference between the original GP referral and the hospital précis; the fact of the handwritten and typed representations of the same interview; the stronger formulation in the completed impression; the qualified separate/peripheral hypothesis; and the absence of the reported protest in the identified completed narrative.

**What remains for proof:** that the change in meaning was knowingly false rather than a sincerely held clinical interpretation; the author and circumstances of the referral compression; when each typed phrase was composed; what was shared at the complex-case meeting; and whether the reported protest occurred as recalled, what was said, and how the neurologist responded.

The case for intent is therefore **a reasoned inference from documents and circumstances**, not an established finding. The task for a litigator is to test that inference against the original records, author and witnesses.

## I. The starting evidence fixes the original clinical problem

On 29 June 2017 the GP sent the urgent referral reproduced as [Document 2](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-2). It joined the patient's painful, physically abnormal feet and toe clawing to disordered movement, gait, stiffness, increased tone and worsening ability to work. The GP's earlier examinations in [Document 1](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-1) similarly joined the feet, gait and muscle tension.

The hospital's short opening referral description, reproduced within [Document 4](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-4), retained difficulty initiating movement, stiffness, tone and wrist cogwheeling while omitting the GP's explicit bilateral painful-foot, callus and clawing details.

The significance is not that the hospital necessarily hid the original referral: the full letter may have been in the file and the neurologist subsequently interviewed the patient independently. The significance is **the direction of the first documented recasting**. The hospital's summary presented a narrower motor problem than the GP's written reason for referral.

The precise author of the compression, its timing, whether it reached the neurologist or complex-case meeting as the only referral account, and whether the full original was displayed must be obtained. Deliberateness cannot be attributed to the treating neurologist simply because the hospital's intake précis is narrower.

## II. The clinician's firsthand knowledge prevents an explanation based only on the précis

The patient was interviewed during the 3 July admission. [Documents 5–7](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-5) preserve three handwritten pages attributed to the clinician; the separate [photograph of page 1](document_05_manuscript.jpg), [page 2](document_06_manuscript.jpg) and [page 3](document_07_manuscript.jpg) permit independent comparison with editorial transcriptions.

These notes preserve longstanding tension, prior medical attendances, podiatric problems and a change in foot pain with reduced prolonged standing. The handwritten account records that the patient recognised foot pain and leg tension **“weren't the same”** as their courses became easier to distinguish.

The user has identified the patient's opening words in that interview as **“Where do I begin?”**. This should be verified directly against the original page-1 photograph before it is used as an independently established verbatim manuscript quotation. Its evidential importance is as the beginning of the firsthand history, not as a rhetorical flourish.

Other parts of the clinician's record recognise pes planus, hammertoes, a markedly abnormal gait appearing potentially dystonic rather than foot drop, and leg tension treated with clonazepam. The clinician was therefore not ignorant of a physical foot and movement problem. The presence of genuine examination, investigation, neurological diagnosis and podiatry referral must remain acknowledged.

The question becomes **what the clinician did with what he actually knew**, rather than whether he had ever heard about the feet.

## III. The decisive alteration is a change in attributed meaning

Compare three distinct stages, without treating them as independent witnesses:

| Stage | Words or documented sense | Evidential character | Question |
|---|---|---|---|
| Handwritten interview, [Docs 5–7](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-5) | Foot pain and leg tension **“weren't the same”** after the pain's course changed with less prolonged standing | Contemporaneous clinician's shorthand record of patient history | Does the patient distinguish experiences, or expressly assert distinct medical causes? |
| Typed clinical history, [Doc 4](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-4) | The symptoms were **“not the same problem”** | Formal clinician-authored rendition of the same interview | Does **problem** carry a broader implied separation than the handwritten distinction? |
| Completed clinical impression, [Doc 4](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-4) | Symptoms **“may have been completely separate or perhaps a secondary peripheral neuropathy”**; **“Not clear”**; patient **“in his own mind - markedly divorced”** leg tension from foot pains/corns and calluses | Clinician's qualified etiological hypothesis followed by an emphatic assertion attributed to the patient's thinking | What evidence authorises attributing an emphatic causal separation to the patient? |

The handwritten distinction is real. The allegation must **not** suggest that the typed account invented all mention of a difference between pain and tension. That would be contradicted by the primary notes.

Instead, identify the **increment in meaning**:

1. The patient can experience different sensations and notice that their severity changes differently over time.
2. The clinician can legitimately hypothesise that the sensations have distinct causes.
3. But the clinician cannot, without adequate basis, convert his own causal hypothesis into a statement that the **patient himself** had emphatically separated the underlying problems.
4. The wording **“in his own mind”** is important because it identifies the patient's understanding as the purported source of support. **“Markedly”** intensifies that attribution.
5. The placement alongside **“may have been completely separate”** invites readers to understand the clinician's tentative separation as consistent with, even supported by, the patient's own belief.

**The exact proposition for examination:** Was the author reporting a belief the patient actually expressed, or was he knowingly presenting his own interpretation as the patient's belief?

It is possible for a clinical impression to be sincere and nevertheless contain an inaccurate attribution; an inaccurate attribution alone does not prove conscious falsity. To establish intent, examine the authorial choices, contemporaneous alternatives, recording standards, corrections, communications and responses to challenge.

## IV. Why a knowing change has a plausible practical purpose

The hypothesis of deliberate misrepresentation is stronger if one can identify **what the new wording accomplished**.

The clinician's examination linked movement abnormalities and foot posture, but the final impression entertained a separate or peripheral explanation for painful corns, calluses and burning. Podiatry was arranged, and treatment of leg tension was pursued. Those acts are real clinical care; the issue is whether the neurological–foot relationship was examined or assigned a responsible investigator.

A strong patient-attributed separation could make the divided disposition appear to rest not only on the clinician's uncertain hypothesis, but on the patient's own view of his symptoms. It could therefore reduce the apparent need to explain why toe posturing, abnormal movement, recurring focal pressure and pain were not investigated together.

This is a **motive-and-function inference**, not direct proof that the author intended those effects. Its strength depends on showing that the author recognised the neurological-foot question as live, that the attribution lacked adequate source support, and that the disputed text was used or relied upon in reaching or defending the disposition.

The most testable question is not whether the author could have thought neuropathy possible. He plainly could: he qualified it as **“Not clear.”** It is why the patient's own mind was used to strengthen the impression of division when the handwritten interview, at most, recorded differentiable symptom experiences.

## V. The patient alleges an opposing message before discharge

The signed [2026 supplementary statement](Article_16_Supplementary_Statement_July_2017.pdf) recounts a bedside challenge concerning the failure to investigate the feet, allegedly brought to the neurologist through four medical students on **10 July 2017**. Earlier written reconstructions from June 2025 used both 10 and 11 July; this discrepancy is not to be concealed. The earliest accounts are relevant to credibility because they predate the later signed statement, but repeated accounts by the same patient are not independent eyewitness corroboration.

If this encounter occurred as recalled, it introduces **contrary live information**: the patient was not simply acquiescing in the apparent separation or the sufficiency of investigation. He was directly contesting it before discharge. A consultant who personally heard that objection might be expected to recognise that the patient's stance was more complicated than a statement of confident internal separation.

The completed clinical account does not preserve that protest. It was confirmed after discharge; a DaTscan-related addendum was finalised **13 July**, and the summary was confirmed **17 July**. **These dates do not establish when “markedly divorced” was first drafted.** A phrase drafted before the protest could nevertheless be retained at authorisation; alternatively, the phrase may have been drafted after. The choice cannot be proven without native version metadata and witness examination.

The argument for intent becomes considerably stronger **if** corroboration establishes that the clinician heard and understood the complaint, that it directly contradicted his attribution, and that he knowingly authorised a final account leaving the contrary position out.

## VI. The complex-case meeting is a critical knowledge checkpoint—not yet a proved causal link

The completed consultant document states the patient was **“Seen at the Neurology Complex case meeting with my colleagues”**. **Earlier research already placed that meeting at approximately 4 July 2017**, on the basis of the patient's chronology. The [existing Orientation for Counsel](https://github.com/TitanicParker/Ms-Tansey/blob/main/Orientation_for_Counsel_Rewritten%281%29.md) calls this **“The 4 July decision”**; the [earlier Master Case for Counsel](https://github.com/TitanicParker/prima-facie/blob/main/MASTER_CASE_FOR_COUNSEL.md) records **“About 4 July”**. That is a substantive working date, not a date independently established by the native meeting register. The clinician's differential and treatment plan reflect consultation with colleagues. **The meeting was therefore placed about a day after the 3 July interview, and before the patient's reported 10 July protest.**

That makes the meeting's **exact date, source material and evidential input** essential. The working date is **approximately 4 July**, not an entirely undated event. The interview was on 3 July; the typed account's original drafting time is not known. Do not assume that the exact “markedly divorced” wording was in existence **before** the meeting or that it influenced colleagues.

What must be determined is:

- Confirm the working date of **about 4 July** against the native meeting agenda or clinical log, and establish whether any typed history or impression had been created by then.
- Did it receive the full GP referral or only an abbreviated referral account?
- Were the three handwritten interview pages, typed history or only a verbal synopsis presented?
- Did any participant examine the feet or discuss a possible contribution from dystonic posture to clawing and painful loading?
- Was any responsibility assigned for investigating that relationship, and where was that recorded?
- Did the later typed impression describe a view reached at the meeting, or was it framed without such discussion?

This early placement intensifies the question of **what picture of the feet reached the senior discussion**, while still not proving the typed patient-attribution existed by then. The meeting could either corroborate an intentional framing theory **or weaken it**. A contemporaneous independent discussion recognising the unresolved foot link and assigning follow-up would materially alter the picture. Its source pack is therefore a high-value request.

## VII. Downstream records test the explanation's practical consequences

The completed specialist account was professional correspondence capable of influencing subsequent understanding. The GP's longitudinal note records receiving **extensive specialist correspondence on 18 July 2017**, including the Parkinsonism diagnosis; it does not establish the precise pages read or acceptance of the contentious wording. See [Document 1](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-1).

Importantly, in **2019** the GP independently associated painful clawed feet with Parkinsonism, abnormal gait and increased tone. See [Document 11](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-11). This evidence defeats any blanket assertion that the specialist's wording convinced everyone that the feet were neurologically unrelated. It also establishes that the integrated question remained visible to at least one clinician.

In **2023**, the GP sent an urgent neurological referral concerning severe foot/lower-leg pain; the movement-disorder nurse reported concerns to the consultant while expressly not knowing whether he had seen that full GP referral. See [Document 17](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-17) (only page 1 of 2 in the local compilation), [Document 18](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-18), and the [recipient-and-knowledge audit](RECIPIENT_AND_KNOWLEDGE_AUDIT.md).

These later events do not show that every clinician adopted a false separation. They show a recurring question of **specialist ownership and information transmission** despite longitudinal foot care and neurological treatment.

The 2025 clinician response, [Document 22](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-22), defended much of the Parkinsonism treatment strategy. A defence of carefully managed dopamine treatment may be clinically coherent yet fail to demonstrate where the **original integrated foot mechanism** was investigated or properly handed over. If the same initial author was also defending the implications of his account, independent review of the original handwritten and referral evidence was particularly important.

The 2025 hospital outcome, [Documents 31–32](Margaret_Tansey.html#document-31), reported no deficit. However, the full PAR, evidence list and SIMT minutes are not in the current local bundle. **The available outcome does not show whether reviewers compared the original GP referral, handwriting, typed attribution and reported protest; it does not prove that they failed to do so.**

## VIII. The affirmative case for intent: a cumulative forensic inference

Counsel can advance deliberate misrepresentation as the **primary contested hypothesis**, grounded in these cumulative indicators:

| Indicator | Established source fact | Why it may support intent | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Direction of the documentary progression** | GP's integrated feet-and-movement referral becomes a motor-focused opening précis, followed by a differentiated specialist disposition | The same original foot question is narrowed at successive documentary stages | The précis may have been written by someone else and is not itself evidence of this neurologist's motive |
| **The clinician's independent knowledge** | Handwritten history and examination include feet, abnormal posture and tension | The author cannot explain the final clinical account solely by saying the opening précis omitted those features | The author might sincerely believe the feet had multiple or separate causes |
| **Shift in attributed meaning** | Manuscript “weren't the same”; typed “not the same problem”; impression “in his own mind - markedly divorced” | The strongest wording makes the patient's mind appear to reinforce the clinician's division | The full interview could have included unrecorded remarks; shorthand need not contain every utterance |
| **Placement beside a qualified hypothesis** | “May have been completely separate” and “Not clear” immediately precede the emphatic patient attribution | The attributed certainty may do explanatory work the clinician's own hypothesis could not | Narrative proximity alone does not prove deliberate rhetorical design |
| **Reported direct challenge** | Patient recalls a specific bedside protest; absent from completed account | If corroborated, knowing omission of a contrary patient position would make the attribution harder to justify as innocent | Event, content, author knowledge and clinical response await independent verification |
| **Later responsibility gap** | Available discharge/follow-up record does not identify a clearly accountable investigation of the integrated foot–movement–pressure problem | Strengthened separation may have made a divided pathway appear adequate | Competent care may still have occurred and standard care may not require the proposed investigation |
| **Retrospective defence** | The same clinician defended historical care in 2025; the reported institutional outcome was no deficit | Potential for an original account to become its own supporting evidence | The complete reviewer source inventory is missing; later defence is not proof of original dishonest intention |

**Cumulative argument:** It is reasonably open to allege that the author moved from one actual interview to an emphatic attribution of patient agreement with a separation that served the clinical disposition. The supported question is whether he **knew** that attribution overstated what the patient meant. Neither the wording difference nor the later course alone resolves the author's mental state.

## IX. The strongest anticipated defence—and the answers that must be sought

**Defence 1: “The patient really did distinguish the symptoms.”** Correct. The handwriting records a distinction. **Reply:** ask whether the distinction concerned felt symptoms and their timing or an endorsed belief in separate causes. Require the contemporaneous evidential basis for attributing a *marked* separation of the problems to the patient's mind.

**Defence 2: “Clinical summaries necessarily interpret rather than transcribe.”** Correct. **Reply:** the issue is not the right to interpret, but whether the author's interpretation was sufficiently distinguished from a factual assertion about what the patient believed, especially when placed alongside the author's differential diagnosis.

**Defence 3: “Separate peripheral pathology was plausible.”** Correct; the impression itself says uncertain. **Reply:** a plausible hypothesis does not prove that the patient endorsed it, nor does it by itself show who undertook responsibility for testing its relationship to abnormal toe posture and gait.

**Defence 4: “The feet were examined and podiatry was arranged.”** Correct. **Reply:** do not contend that nothing was done; request the examination or handover that assessed the neurological contribution to clawing, recurring pressure lesions and pain. Podiatric treatment and neurological assessment have overlapping but not identical purposes.

**Defence 5: “The protest was not recorded because it never occurred or was not material.”** Possible. **Reply:** obtain student placement/ward/teaching evidence, early dated reports, any clinician recollection, ward entries and disclosure search methodology. Test independently rather than presume either outcome.

**Defence 6: “No one was misled; the GP remained aware.”** The GP's 2019 referral supports independent awareness. **Reply:** distinguish a failure to deceive all downstream clinicians from the narrower possible misrepresentation of the patient's original position and subsequent question of who possessed specialist responsibility.

**Defence 7: “The hospital independently reviewed the care.”** It may have. **Reply:** request the full PAR, document/version inventory and meeting papers to see whether the primary source conflict was examined. A short outcome cannot prove the composition of the underlying investigation either way.

These defences should be addressed directly. The case gains credibility by conceding what the records genuinely show while demanding answers where the clinician-authored account is most vulnerable.

## X. Five decisive methods of proof

1. **A diplomatic manuscript-to-typed collation.** Place every foot-related handwritten line beside the exact typed-history and impression passages, noting all alterations of subject, certainty, causation, attribution and chronology. Use the original photographs as the control, not OCR alone. Obtain independent transcription where readings are ambiguous.
2. **An authorial and version-history audit.** Obtain the native handwritten scans, dictation/secretarial instructions, electronic document object, first typed draft, intermediate revisions, authorship, creation/edit timestamps and signed/dispatched copies. Distinguish initial drafting from later approval. Identify when the exact patient-attribution was inserted and retained.
3. **Complex-case meeting source reconstruction.** Confirm the **approximately 4 July** working date from native records; obtain attendees, notes and the original material supplied. Establish whether a narrower narrative or the handwritten patient evidence shaped the team's discussion and the documented follow-up assignment.
4. **Independent protest corroboration.** Obtain ward/consultant rosters, medical-student placements, teaching-supervision records and any surviving communications; assess the early June and July 2025 accounts transparently, including the 10/11 July discrepancy. Seek a response from the clinician to the specific alleged exchange.
5. **2025 review-source audit.** Obtain the entire PAR, source inventory and SIMT papers. Test whether the reviewing institution independently compared the GP referral, opening précis, handwritten notes, typed attribution and protest notice before accepting the clinician's historical explanation.

## XI. The examination that should be put to the clinician

A focused examination can proceed in this order:

1. Do you accept that the original GP referral included painful feet, toe clawing and neurological movement concerns as part of one presentation?
2. Who prepared the admission précis, and when did you first see the full GP referral?
3. Do you recognise these three manuscript pages as your contemporaneous notes of the patient's interview?
4. What did the patient mean, in your understanding at the time, when you recorded that foot pain and tension **“weren't the same”**?
5. Did the patient expressly tell you that he believed the feet and movement disorder were medically unrelated? Where is that statement documented?
6. Who authored **“in his own mind - markedly divorced”**? Was this a quotation from the patient or your own formulation?
7. What additional patient words or clinical observations justified the stronger formulation?
8. Why did that attribution appear alongside your suggestion of potentially separate symptoms or peripheral neuropathy, while the relationship itself was expressly **“Not clear”**?
9. What foot-related material did you present to the complex-case meeting, and what decisions were taken there about the foot–movement relationship?
10. Were you asked before discharge to investigate the feet further? What did you say and do, and what contemporaneous record documents your response?
11. Who was made responsible for examining whether abnormal neurological movement contributed to toe clawing, loading and painful recurrent lesions?
12. What records did you supply to the GP, movement-disorder team and 2025 hospital reviewers, and did you draw their attention to the difference between handwritten and typed accounts?

These questions should be answered against actual source exhibits and dates, not recollection alone.

## Proposed statement for counsel

> The patient alleges deliberate misrepresentation in the July 2017 specialist clinical account. The original handwritten interview recorded a limited difference between the experiences and trajectories of foot pain and leg tension. The clinician's completed typed narrative progressed to describing the symptoms as different problems, and the ensuing impression placed an uncertain suggestion of separate or peripheral pathology beside an emphatic assertion that the patient had, “in his own mind - markedly divorced,” the symptoms. The patient disputes having endorsed a medical separation of causes. He contends that the attribution was knowingly strengthened to support a divided clinical disposition of an integrated presentation originally referred by his GP, and that his reported pre-discharge objection to the inadequate foot investigation was omitted from the completed record.
>
> The claim is supported by an unusually direct comparison between the GP's referral, the hospital's abbreviated précis, the original handwritten interview, the typed account, and the ultimate clinical impression. Intent is disputed and requires determination from native drafting evidence, contemporaneous communications, the complex-case meeting record, the clinician's explanation and independent evidence concerning the reported objection. The existence of real Parkinsonism investigations and podiatric care is accepted; it does not, without more, answer the fidelity of the attributed patient position or the allocation of responsibility for the foot–movement relationship.

## Conclusion

**The decisive issue is not whether the neurologist used the words “weren't the same” correctly. He had a handwritten basis for reporting a distinction. The issue is whether he knowingly transformed that distinction into an unwarranted account of the patient's belief, thereby presenting his own disputed clinical separation as one the patient himself had emphatically adopted.**

The present documents support **advancing and investigating** that allegation, not declaring it judicially established. The most serious challenge to an innocent-summary explanation is the combination of the strengthened patient-attribution, its placement beside the clinician's speculative separation, the original GP's integrated referral, the neurologist's own foot findings, and the patient's reported contrary protest before the completed account was authorised. A native source and witness investigation can test those links one by one.

**The requested finding for independent counsel is whether the cumulative evidence supports a knowing, material misrepresentation of the patient's meaning, and what consequences, if any, that misrepresentation had for the clinical disposition, downstream communication, and the hospital's later review.**
