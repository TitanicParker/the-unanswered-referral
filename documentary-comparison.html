Document 2 · 29 June 2017

The GP's urgent referral

The full referral describes a developing presentation involving:

  • Bilateral foot complaints, corns and calluses.
  • Toe clawing while trying to get comfortable in bed.
  • Abnormal gait, initiation difficulty and increasing muscle tone.
  • Stiffness, deteriorating movement and difficulty functioning at work.
  • Concern about Parkinsonism or another neurological cause.

The feet are part of the problem being referred, not an incidental finding added later.

The receiving summary

The abbreviated account records predominantly motor findings:

  • Difficulty initiating movement.
  • Increased tone and stiffness in all four limbs.

Not reproduced in this précis: the GP's explicit bilateral foot pain, corns and calluses, and toe-clawing details.

The hospital record elsewhere contains foot findings. This is a comparison of the opening summary, not a claim that the neurologist was unaware of the feet.

The interpretive step to test

“Weren't the same” already appears in the handwritten history; the distinction was not invented by the typed record. But distinguishable sensations and different symptom courses do not, without further clinical assessment, establish unrelated medical causes. The later “markedly divorced” wording is a stronger clinician-authored attribution of the patient's understanding, appearing beside the clinician's own qualified causal possibilities.

The protest

The patient later stated that he asked four medical students to summon the treating neurologist and challenged the adequacy of the foot investigation. The completed account does not record that exchange.

Evidence status: firsthand retrospective testimony; the students, exact exchange and clinical response have not been independently corroborated in the assembled record. An earlier 2025 private account used 11 July rather than 10 July, a discrepancy requiring transparent treatment.

What the dates do and do not prove

Discharge occurred on 11 July. An addendum was finalised on 13 July; the completed account was confirmed on 17 July.

Those dates show approval after the reported encounter, not when the disputed wording was first written. Neither deliberate editing nor deliberate suppression can be inferred from approval dates alone.

For independent examination

What this comparison asks the hospital to explain

  1. Why did the opening hospital précis not reproduce the foot pain and toe-clawing details in the full GP referral? Who prepared it, and who saw the complete referral?
  2. What exact words appear on the three handwritten pages, and which passages of the typed history reproduce them faithfully or add interpretation?
  3. What supports attributing a markedly divorced understanding to the patient, rather than identifying that interpretation as the clinician's own?
  4. At the complex-case discussion, placed at about 4 July, what did colleagues receive: the full GP referral, the hospital précis, the handwritten interview or a verbal summary? What did they decide about the feet?
  5. What neurological, podiatric or joint assessment examined the relationship between abnormal movement, foot posture, pressure lesions and pain? Who was responsible for pursuing it?
  6. If the reported pre-discharge objection occurred, what response was made and what information entered the record or was transmitted to the GP? What do the native version history and possible witnesses establish?
The central documentary question

How did the feet move from being an explicit part of the GP's referral, to being absent from the hospital's opening summary, to being represented through a stronger attributed separation in the completed clinical impression—and where was responsibility for investigating their relationship to abnormal neurological movement recorded?

The apparent sequence identifies questions to test; it does not prove why the wording changed or determine a legal or clinical breach.

What is firmly documented

The original referral, abbreviated précis, handwritten pages, typed account, qualified neuropathy possibility, and their differing wording can be compared directly. The neurologist also documented foot abnormalities, recognised a potentially dystonic gait, and arranged care; this was not a complete absence of clinical attention.

What remains unproved

That the omission changed any individual clinician's understanding; that the patient's words were deliberately distorted; the first drafting time of the typed phrases; independent corroboration of the protest; or that the unresolved foot mechanism fell below the applicable standard of care.
