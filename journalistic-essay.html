The Hospital Had Its Chance

In July 2017, a man asked his neurologist to investigate the feet on which he stood. The question had already been asked by his GP. The discharge summary that went back into medicine did not carry the patient’s objection. Nine years later, after an extraordinary recovery, years of pain and another extraordinary recovery, he is trying to establish what happened to the man who walked into Galway.

I. The question that went into Galway

On the second Monday of his admission to University Hospital Galway, Liam Moloney asked four medical students to fetch his neurologist. He had been in the hospital for a week. He had been examined, questioned and introduced to the prospect of living with a serious neurological condition. He had learned a great deal. But he could not understand why the investigation appeared to be approaching its conclusion without having adequately addressed the problem of his feet.

He used the analogy of the National Car Test. What was the point of all the work that had gone into the inspection if the car was going to come out with bald tyres?

He had not been asking for a particular drug or a particular diagnosis. His feet hurt, his toes clawed, his gait was abnormal. He had spent seven days in a hospital capable of examining all three. He wanted the work done.

That was 10 July 2017. By the next day he would be out of the hospital. A week after the confrontation, on 17 July at half past seven in the evening, the consultant's completed account was confirmed. It contained a remarkable amount of information. It offered a lengthy description of the patient's history, numerous considerations of diagnosis and treatment, an assessment of his intellectual capacities and an account of his understanding of the condition. It did not contain his protest.

The consultant knew about the challenge. The document did not. That was the difference between the circumstances in which Monaghan left the admission and those in which the rest of medicine was to receive the patient. The difference would survive the admission, the treatment, the improvement and the suffering that followed. It is the reason Moloney is writing about his life now.

The first doctor in the story was Aaron Brennan, a GP in Galway. On 10 April 2017 he examined a man with an abnormal gait, bilateral foot pain, mallet toes, abnormal nails, calluses and pressure areas. Moloney wondered whether an old episode of sciatica had left him walking habitually in a certain way. Brennan did not simply adopt the explanation. He examined him, arranged investigations and pursued the problem. The patient had a story about his body; the GP had a body to investigate. Over the following weeks, the distinction would prove enormously important.

Over the next two months Brennan's observations became more revealing. The problem was not merely the appearance of the feet. There was difficulty initiating movement. There was peculiar tension. By 8 June he wanted a neurological opinion about the patient's “gait, feet and ‘muscle tension’”. The wording has the economical intelligence of a clinician who has recognised a problem beyond the limits of his own inquiry. Three features were to be considered in relation to each other. On 23 June there was markedly increased tone and brisk reflex activity; by the end of June the patient's deteriorating dexterity was affecting his work. On 29 June Brennan arranged urgent hospital assessment. The feet were there in the opening paragraph, not buried in an incidental account of his medical history. The referral described the clawing of the toes and the difficulty relaxing his muscles, particularly in bed. Possible Parkinsonian or other disease was the question of diagnosis. The description of the patient was the reason for asking it.

Something happened to the description when it reached the hospital's abbreviated reason for referral. The receiving document carried difficulty initiating movement, increased tone and stiffness in all four limbs, and wrist cogwheeling. The feet and the clawing toes were absent; even the tentative quality of Brennan's observation of cogwheeling had been lost. A question about a connected physical presentation had become a more recognisable presentation of Parkinsonism.

The hospital précis had lost the feet, but the consultant recovered them. Thomas Monaghan heard the history himself. He examined the patient and generated a finding more specifically relevant to Brennan’s concern than the GP’s letter had been capable of generating. The loss of information in the précis can no longer explain what happened once the neurologist had acquired it independently.

The man in front of him was thirty-nine. His description of what had been happening to his body stretched back approximately thirteen years. In 2004 he had become aware of a strange need to place his body in positions in which he could feel tension, as though the presence of tension were necessary to achieve the experience of relaxation. Painful feet, corns and calluses entered the history; he remembered receiving treatment around 2009 and 2010. After leaving bar work in 2012, he was standing less and the pain in his feet became less prominent. The tension persisted. In 2014 he had attended hospital with tension in his arms and legs but had not seen a neurologist. By 2017 his writing, balance, arm movement and dexterity were affected. Handling cardboard boxes at work had become difficult. He felt his current state was the maturation of the earlier symptoms.

All this was available to the consultant at the beginning of the admission. It was not the retrospective reconstruction of a man in possession of a genetic diagnosis. It was the history a doctor took on 3 July 2017.

The consultant found that the gait was strikingly abnormal. There was no arm swing. There was gross rigidity and bradykinesia. The lower-limb movement had a foot-drop appearance, but it was not foot drop. Monaghan wrote that it was somewhat suggestive of a dystonic posture. He recorded pes planus, hammertoes and mild pitting oedema. He filmed the examination with the patient's permission.

The GP’s question was now the neurologist’s own. The doctor had seen an abnormality of neurological movement in a man referred with abnormal foot posture, painful pressure areas and clawing toes. He had the patient, the clinical finding and the history. What was missing was the work that joined them together.

At the same time Monaghan took a step which is easily overlooked because it is so ordinary: he prescribed clonazepam at night to relieve the tension in the legs. It was his own therapeutic intervention into the longstanding phenomenon. The patient places this intervention on the first Monday, before the senior case discussion. The completed record establishes that it commenced during the admission and that sweating followed administration, prompting the consultant to consider Parkinsonian dysautonomia. The exact first-dose clock time is not needed to understand what the decision meant. Monaghan had recognised a longstanding physical symptom as a problem for neurological treatment. His subsequent discussion of whether nighttime procyclidine might replace clonazepam continued that recognition. He was not merely awaiting a colleague's judgment on whether the tension belonged to neurology. He was treating it.

The hospital's complex-case meeting is placed in the working chronology around 4 July. It brought access to senior expertise, including Timothy Counihan, whose interest in the genetics and management of Parkinson's disease was directly relevant to a young man with an unusual presentation. The completed account records a probable idiopathic Parkinsonian diagnosis with atypical features and the advice to exercise great care over dopamine agonists and levodopa. The risks of treatment in a younger patient deserved consideration. But the referral, the history, the examination and the treatment of tension had generated a question independent of the timing of levodopa. The meeting's authority cannot establish that this other question was examined unless it can be shown that it was placed before the meeting. The underlying meeting material matters because the expertise of a doctor is not a substitute for the information with which he is supplied.

Galway was conducting an extensive investigation into Parkinsonism. The work included brain and cervical imaging, laboratory investigation, physiotherapy and occupational therapy input, the planning of a DaTscan and the discussion of a difficult future medication strategy. This was precisely the kind of hospital in which the patient might expect a sophisticated inquiry to be undertaken. The question is why, in the middle of all this activity, an examination of the relationship between the feet and the observed motor phenomenon remained so difficult to locate.

II. What the account made of the patient

The history that Monaghan took on 3 July survives in handwriting. On its first page is a question: “Where do I begin?” The answer takes the doctor through the man's life, from peculiar muscular tension to difficulty with walking and employment. The history has the untidiness of a genuine attempt to describe something that has developed over years. It is not organised around an established diagnosis. The patient has not yet learned the specialist's language. He is supplying the material from which the specialist will form an understanding.

On the third manuscript page the account moves into the history of the feet. It describes the period after leaving bar work, when the pain became less prominent. A short sentence records that the patient began to be aware that foot pain and leg tension “weren't the same”. A parenthetical entry separately refers to the leg pain settling. Those words deserve to be kept in their original form. The patient was distinguishing experiences. The distinction between the physical discomfort of standing on a corn and the sensation of sustained muscular tension is a clinically useful thing to communicate. It does not require the person communicating it to have a theory of whether the two phenomena have the same origin.

The typed version gave this material a more elaborate structure. “Therefore” introduced the proposition that in 2012 the patient began to realise the foot pain and tension were not the same problem. “Because” supplied an explanatory relationship to the change in the prominence of pain and tension. The completed Impression went further still. It said the patient had “in his own mind - markedly divorced” the feelings of tension in his legs from the corns, calluses and pains in his feet. The physical foot abnormalities remained a major problem, the neurologist conceded, but “in reality” they were much less of an issue because he had been on his feet less.

In the space between those documents the patient had become the apparent author of an increasingly emphatic separation. The original distinction was real. The medical implication placed upon it is the point at issue. That implication matters because the sentence immediately preceding the attribution entertained a different explanation for the feet: their symptoms might have been completely separate, or perhaps secondary peripheral neuropathy, possibly associated in some respect with the patient's earlier alcohol intake. The neurologist's own hypothesis was uncertain. “Not clear,” he wrote.

The record had already noted a reduction and cessation of alcohol without improvement. The corresponding typed history preserves that report. In the Impression the phrase “as this has improved” appears beside the peripheral hypothesis, without specifying the referent and period clearly enough to explain the relationship between the two entries. The problem is not solved by supplying the neurologist with a meaning that the document itself does not establish. One can read the words and see how much work they are being asked to do. Improvement in pain associated with less standing, lack of improvement following abstinence, and a suggestion of secondary neuropathy are different observations and propositions. They are not one observation merely because the account places them in proximity.

The effect of the completed passage is much greater than the tentative clinical language might suggest. The neuropathy is a possibility; the separation in the patient's mind is stated as a fact. The latter lends apparent support to the former. The neurologist's hypothesis acquires something that looks like independent information from the person experiencing the symptoms. Yet the apparent independence disappears when one returns to the handwritten source. Both formulations have their origin in the same interview, and the stronger attribution is the consultant's.

The completed account has to be read with 10 July in mind. The patient had stood before its author and challenged the investigation of the very problem that the account proposed to separate. The document that went out afterwards contains the separation, not the challenge.

Look at the work actually documented on the feet. Corns and calluses. Pes planus. Hammertoes. Mild pitting oedema. A significant observation of dystonic-looking movement. The GP had initiated the podiatry route and on 6 July prioritised it in light of the emerging diagnosis; the hospital asked him to arrange outpatient podiatry, recording that an SHO had tried to arrange it during the admission. What did all this establish about the relationship between clawing, painful loading, muscular tension and abnormal movement? The account does not supply the answer. It supplies the visible abnormalities, an onward arrangement for treatment and a possible peripheral explanation.

The neurologist had very little dedicated investigation of the feet to draw upon when confronted with the complaint that he had not investigated them. He had, however, the history he had taken on the first Monday. The patient had described leaving bar work, standing less and becoming more aware of tension as the pain subsided. Those observations were made to carry a remarkable explanatory burden. In the completed Impression the patient himself had markedly divorced the problems, and the corns and calluses were “in reality” less of an issue. The medical question remained open. The plan was extensive about medication, counselling and support, but the original question received no equivalent allocation of work. At its conclusion: “As per above.”

The discharge account praised Moloney’s intelligence, insight and ability to ask appropriate questions. It was intended, in part, to consolidate the discussions of the admission for the patient himself. The question he had actually asked on the second Monday, the one that mattered most to this history, was not among them.

III. The second Monday, and the information that went out

The protest was the GP referral a second time, delivered in person. Moloney was not introducing a new symptom or asking Monaghan to investigate an unfamiliar problem. He was telling the neurologist that the work he had undertaken did not sufficiently address the problem already sent to him. The first referral had required an investigation. The second had required a corrective investigation. Neither yielded a documented neurological answer to the feet.

Moloney remembers being told, in substance, that arranging podiatry was as straightforward for him as it was for the consultant. He had not spent a week in a teaching hospital because he needed someone to remove a corn. The GP had already arranged a route to that kind of care. He wanted to know what the abnormal functioning of his body was doing to his feet. Four medical students had been there; their evidence remains to be obtained.

Before the conversation, the limited investigation could have been an oversight. After it, the neurologist knew the patient considered it inadequate. The protest made the existing problem impossible to treat as something that had merely escaped attention. There was still time to examine the feet, explain the work, ask a colleague or arrange a defined further investigation. There was still time to put that work into the account.

It is worth being exact about the moment. The man was still in hospital. The doctor did not need to recover a specimen of the patient's past physical state from an archive. He could see the way the man stood, the way he initiated movement and the condition of his toes. He could examine the location of the pressure lesions in relation to the posture. He could determine what the patient meant by the tension and investigate how much of the posture was dynamic, how much fixed, and which features changed with the treatments already being contemplated. He did not need to be able to answer everything on that Monday. What he needed was to ensure that the question would leave the hospital with an answer or with a named responsibility for obtaining one.

Instead, the completed account represented the patient as having himself markedly separated the problems. It neither preserved the challenge nor identified a response to it. The consultant’s knowledge had moved on; the account he authorised had not caught up. It stood downstream of the knowledge from which it should have been written.

The protest placed two tasks before the neurologist: address the deficient investigation and accurately communicate its state. What the document eventually provided was a representation of a patient whose own understanding appeared to support the existing disposition. The careful articulation of that representation could not perform the examination the patient had sought.

Moloney left on 11 July. The DaTscan was performed on 13 July. The completed account was confirmed on 17 July at 19:30. Whatever the first-draft date of the words “markedly divorced,” they were authorised after the challenge. The final account was capable of carrying new information; it included the subsequent scan result. It did not carry the protest. The version history remains important, but the chronology of the confirmed document is already revealing.

Brennan had to work from the specialist’s account. The movement-disorders nurse had to participate in the treatment strategy that emerged from it. Moloney had to live with the medical understanding it communicated. None was furnished through that summary with the consultant’s knowledge that the adequacy of the foot investigation had been challenged. The neurologist had created the informational circumstances in which these people were to work. They did not possess his experience of the admission merely because they possessed his description of it.

That is the material disparity. The account conveyed an understanding attributed to the patient and omitted his challenge to the adequacy of the work. Its recipients were not being invited to question the sufficiency of the investigation; they were being given the considered account of a specialist. The patient cannot be expected to compensate for that disparity by repeatedly telling every subsequent clinician what the neurologist had not told them. The neurologist was the person who knew both the clinical event and the representation that went out in its place.

Brennan's own records show the practical movement of this information. In the days following discharge, the GP was awaiting formal correspondence; on 18 July he recorded extensive communication from Monaghan, early Parkinsonism, a positive DaTscan and further investigation. That is evidence of the receipt of the specialist's account, not a certificate that Brennan read every line. Nor was the GP deprived of his own clinical judgment: he would continue to make observations connecting the feet to movement and tone. But he had been sent a specialist-led understanding of the condition within which the treatment of the feet could proceed separately from the neurological treatment of the man. The original concern had not been returned to him as a contested or conspicuously unfinished investigation.

The risk of this arrangement was not that everybody would believe a particular diagnosis was certain. It was that they would behave as though an unresolved question had been sufficiently allocated. A hypothetical peripheral explanation could remain hypothetical on the page and nevertheless determine the division of work. Podiatry would attend to lesions. Neurology would attend to the movement disorder. The GP would see the man and treat his pain. The missing piece was the responsibility for bringing those activities into an explanation of the feet. The consultant had known of the difficulty of that division while the patient was still under his immediate care. The information state he created for others did not reflect it.

There was another loss. The living clinical baseline of July 2017 could not be reproduced simply by recovering the discharge summary. Years of disease progression, medication, altered activity and local treatment would intervene. If a comparative assessment of the feet in different motor states was indicated, the opportunity for performing that assessment in the circumstances of the original admission was lost when those circumstances ended. The later patient would not be able to produce the body the neurologist could have examined. Yet the neurologist's explanation would remain available in a document which could acquire the status of historical evidence. The evidential imbalance began long before there was any complaint.

IV. The year in which medicine knew how well he could be

And then the hospital made him well. This is the fact that transforms the rest of the story. Moloney was not waiting for another opportunity to argue with his neurologist. He was recovering.

Monaghan consulted Daniel Healy, the Beaumont neurologist with expertise in Parkinsonism and genetics. In the correspondence he expressed the shared treatment preference plainly: “Myself and Tim are keen to hold back on Levodopa insofar as maybe possible unless you'd advise otherwise?” Healy's September 2017 response entertained PARK2 as the probable genetic explanation and suggested testing it first, moving to a panel if necessary. He recorded a dramatic improvement in motor symptoms on procyclidine and rasagiline. The movement disorder was being recognised not only in Galway but in another specialist environment. The genetic reasoning was already moving towards the explanation that would eventually be established in 2026. None of this required the patient to invent an account of his recovery years later.

Procyclidine transformed Moloney's experience of his body. His account of the time is not a description of minor improvement in the management of a chronic condition. It is the experience of recovering the use of a body that had been difficult to inhabit for years. In contemporaneous correspondence he described his walk as coming back together. He had been living with the effort of movement for so long that its disappearance was itself a remarkable sensation. The medication had presented visual difficulties, however, and the nurse suggested an interruption. Moloney contacted the consultant on 4 October. His eyesight was back to normal. The tension was building. His feet were restless. His walking was coming apart again. The therapeutic interruption was a clinical event in which the treatment response could be observed in the other direction.

Six months later, on 24 April 2018, the consultant recorded the feature of that interruption which ought to be among the most important pieces of clinical information in the entire case. When the procyclidine was stopped in September or October 2017, the first thing the patient noticed changing, and changing rapidly, was the return of the outside left corn. There are questions about precisely what the word “return” described—the experience of the lesion, the focal pain, the lesion itself—and they should be investigated rather than retrospectively settled. But the specificity of the anatomical reference is not in dispute in the document. The consultant had recorded a change at the original site of a longstanding foot problem in association with the interruption of neurological treatment.

It is difficult to think of a piece of information more directly relevant to the unanswered question of July. The doctor had observed dystonic-looking movement; he had begun treating the longstanding tension; the medication had changed the patient's experience of movement, and its interruption had produced a report about a particular place on the foot. He knew this because he wrote it. The patient did not need to construct a medical argument from it. He was supplying an observation to a neurologist who already possessed the history necessary to recognise its importance.

The rest of the April consultation makes the observation considerably more powerful. The record describes relief from tension as fantastic. The patient could walk twenty metres without it. Transitions from one floor surface to another were no longer a difficulty. He was sleeping well and moving normally in bed. He was back at work in shipping, performing physical tasks and undertaking some health and safety responsibilities. His summary of the change was recorded by his consultant: “I enjoy being alive.”

Monaghan also examined him. He made a second video, this time of hand movements and gait. The walk was very good, with a good arm swing occurring without conscious effort. There was residual bradykinesia, but the doctor described the gait in terms of remarkable improvement. This is the paired clinical observation the patient now needs the institution to preserve: the video made in July 2017, when the neurologist documented absent arm swing, severe rigidity and abnormal movement; and the video made in April 2018, when he documented the return of effective walking. The notes establish that the recordings were made. The recordings themselves, if retrievable, would allow an independent specialist to examine what medicine had accomplished rather than relying solely on the patient's memory of being well.

The neurological record is doing something of exceptional value here. It is establishing a reference point for the patient's future care. Not merely a set of symptoms, but a level of function achieved through treatment. There is also an explicit recognition that further improvement might be possible with levodopa or another dopamine agonist. The consultant's preference was to defer those treatments for good reasons of concern about adverse effects. But the expectation of further improvement was part of his assessment. Moloney was not necessarily approaching the limit of what neurological treatment could accomplish.

In the same consultation Monaghan explained that people with Parkinson's can experience an unusually magnified degree of pain. This is an important feature of the case, because it means that even the explanatory model the neurologist would later use in defence of his treatment establishes a relationship between the neurological condition and the experience of pain. The effect of neurological treatment on the patient's feet remained clinically important even if some of the underlying pathology had an independent origin. The doctor did not have to accept the neurological origin of every painful sensation in order to recognise that a neurological intervention could profoundly change the suffering associated with it. He had the patient's evidence of just such a change, including its focal anatomical component. The case does not require the consultant's own theory of pain to be discarded. It requires its implications to be followed.

There was also the matter of genetics. Monaghan apologised for having forgotten to discuss testing during the consultation. He described the suspected PRKN/PARK2 explanation, a possible targeted test or panel, and his preference for waiting for the benefits of a more developed genetic library. The rationale was that immediate knowledge was not likely to change management; testing was available if Moloney wished to proceed. Consider the strength of the knowledge accumulating in this document: suspected Parkin-associated disease, a remarkable therapeutic response, an observed improvement in movement, a focal foot phenomenon associated with interruption and an explicit understanding of the capacity of Parkinsonism to magnify pain. Yet the original informational division of the patient was still the historical basis of his care. The record does not identify a corresponding reconsideration of the original foot question, communicated as such to the other clinicians.

The events of 2018 also explain something about the patient that would be very easy to misrepresent retrospectively. Moloney did not go home from hospital and spend the next eight years developing a case against his doctor. When the treatment worked he forgot about the protest. He surrendered to the wonderful circumstance of being physically well. He went to work. He was grateful for the capacity to walk without the tension which had afflicted him for years. He had no reason to investigate the history of an admission which had apparently led to his recovery. The intellectual implications of the recovery were not something he was supposed to preserve for future use. The consultant possessed the knowledge. The patient possessed the benefit.

That distinction is the human counterpart of the information disparity created in July. The doctor and patient had both experienced the success of treatment, but they were in entirely different relationships to it. The patient could forget a dispute with the hospital. The doctor's responsibility was to recognise the clinical meaning of the observations that had followed it. The knowledge had been acquired in the course of doing his job. It belonged within the continuing account of the condition, available for use when the patient was no longer well.

The feet meanwhile continued to produce evidence. Podiatry treated recurrent lesions, including the outside of the left foot. Supports were used for toes; calluses and corns were managed. The records preserved the fact of physical abnormalities rather than merely an account of a subjective pain state. In March 2019 Brennan made another referral to podiatry that expressly related the clawing and foot difficulties to increased tone and abnormal gait associated with Parkinson's disease. He had not lost the capacity to recognise the problem he had first sent to the hospital. There is no demonstrated route by which that referral necessarily entered Monaghan's own reading. But that is itself part of the problem. The local evidence and the neurological knowledge needed to meet in a clinical inquiry; their presence in different records is not evidence that they met.

An instructive contrast appears in the neurologist's account of a different complaint in 2019. Moloney had developed severe pain around his right shoulder and arm. Monaghan examined the distribution of symptoms, graded triceps and wrist and finger extension, considered reflexes and the possibility of a nerve-root or plexus disorder, and sought imaging. On reassessment he found the weakness reproducible. He distinguished the possible underlying cause from the greater pain a person with Parkinson's might experience. When imaging remained outstanding he pursued the investigation and explained its limitations. “The actual cause for us is pretty important,” he wrote.

This is a particularly valuable document because it illustrates the difference between naming an uncertain cause and investigating one. The doctor did not require certainty at the outset. He was willing to consider a range of possibilities and explain how the investigation might distinguish among them. He did not allow the principle of pain amplification to relieve him of the need to examine its source. Here was the same neurologist, the same patient and an example of the kind of focused clinical reasoning that can be seen when a particular physical complaint receives dedicated attention. Nobody needs to claim the same tests were indicated for the feet. The comparison is one of clinical method. Where is the corresponding examination of the foot complaint whose history had occupied so much of Moloney's life?

V. A life that became smaller

There were real difficulties in maintaining the benefits of neurological treatment. In late 2018 the dopamine agonist rotigotine was introduced, and the ensuing adjustments generated a complicated pattern of benefit and adverse effects. The February 2019 consultation describes the balance as an extremely difficult tightrope. Higher doses could bring troubling involuntary movements and behavioural effects. Procyclidine had visual limitations, and the patient was not consistently certain of its benefit after a later interruption and resumption. These difficulties belong to the account because they establish the real nature of the work being done. The question is what information medicine used when deciding which benefits to preserve and which risks to incur. A tightrope is not a clinical destination; it is a description of how difficult it is to reach one.

There is a passage in the 2019 review which illustrates the extent to which the patient's experience could diverge from the appearance of his condition during a consultation. To a clinician looking at him on that particular day, the doctor wrote, one might have no idea that he had Parkinson's. The patient, however, did not feel sufficiently loose and described stiffness later in the day. These were temporal difficulties in the observation of a fluctuating condition, and Monaghan recognised them. A snapshot of a successful medicated state did not necessarily represent the full day. The patient could look well and nevertheless experience substantial difficulty. That was precisely the kind of clinical distinction which could become relevant to the feet as treatment evolved.

In September 2021 Sinemet entered the prescription history. The October consultation records that Moloney had felt somewhat undertreated and that a small dose was introduced following his contact with the movement-disorders nurse. Concerns about intraocular pressure led to the removal of procyclidine and its replacement by Sinemet. Monaghan recorded the patient's hope that the increased Sinemet would more than compensate for the removal of Kemadrin, the brand name of procyclidine. This was not simply a new medication being added to an existing list. There was a therapeutic contribution to be replaced, and the clinical history contained evidence of what that contribution had once been.

What did it mean to compensate adequately for the withdrawal of procyclidine? The question was not limited to whether the patient had sufficient fluidity of movement in an outpatient consultation. There had been a striking release from tension. There had been an improvement in walking. There had been a focal foot observation after withdrawal. These were things the neurologist had already identified or documented. A proper understanding of the substitution would require a way of examining what was preserved and what was lost. The doctor was concerned with the rapidly increasing levodopa dose and the possibility of dyskinesia. The concerns were real. But the benefit being exchanged for those risks had a clinical history too. It should have been possible to recognise its components and give them appropriate weight.

The same letter contains another passage whose significance extends beyond the medical advice itself. Moloney raised deep-brain stimulation. Monaghan wrote, “As I have reminded him,” and proceeded to describe DBS as applicable only to tremor. The case register raises two distinct issues: the clinical accuracy and suitability of that advice, and the basis for the implication that the patient had already been told it. They require separate examination. A doctor's record of what he has advised is not identical to his record of what the patient already knows. The reason this passage belongs in the same history as July 2017 is that it concerns the way a professional account can assign knowledge to the patient. Once the assignment is recorded, future readers can encounter it as part of a history of discussion and understanding. Whether the discussion actually occurred is a question about the communication history, not a question about whether the doctor believed the medical proposition. One cannot answer the former by defending the latter.

By 2022 the morning feet were again troublesome. The clinic record described a period of twenty to twenty-five minutes for them to warm up, pain associated with corns and calluses around the edges, and the patient's wondering whether it was neuralgic. The letter recorded a relatively satisfactory position overall. There was mild excessive movement on a modest dose of levodopa, and the consultant was attentive to it. Moloney was pursuing qualifications and getting on with his life. The accumulated clinical knowledge was available in his medical history. His everyday consciousness was occupied with everyday things.

On 26 March 2023, Brennan sent an urgent referral to the same consultant. The man who had told Monaghan in 2018 that he enjoyed being alive was describing severe burning pain in both feet and lower legs and, at its worst, a wish for death. Brennan had begun pregabalin at a low dose and reported that it had rapidly been escalated through several doctors. He recalled an earlier episode of arm pain in which a suspected plexus problem had ultimately subsided with better control of the Parkinsonian condition. He was worried about his patient, notwithstanding the appearance the patient presented. His request was explicit: urgent review before matters became worse.

Two days later a movement-disorders nurse emailed the neurologist. The pain was debilitating despite a reported 600 mg daily pregabalin dose; quality of life had deteriorated over months. The patient was seeking review. The nurse also reported that he had stopped his Parkinson's medication for ten days and believed at the time that it was making no difference from a Parkinson's perspective. He had come to entertain the idea that he had been spiritually healed. That material is not a reason to discard the earlier clinical observations. It makes them more valuable. A patient in distress may develop new understandings of his condition. He may alter his treatment. He may make distinctions that a clinician needs to examine. That is precisely why the record of the earlier patient, of his response to therapy and of the circumstances in which he had become well, was important to the clinicians now seeing him. His understanding was not fixed; medicine's responsibility to investigate the condition was not contingent on the accuracy of his latest explanation.

The request for access to the consultant encountered the pressure of outpatient capacity. One contact note records that a cancellation-list appointment was sought and that Moloney was to follow up with his GP in the interim. The precise sequence of the safety assessment, response, communications and follow-up must be established from the native contact and appointment records. The urgency is already established in the GP's words. His patient had been brought to the attention of the specialist service in a state of extraordinary distress, six years after that service had begun treating the movement disorder and five years after the consultant had documented exceptional improvement. A proper review of the care must be able to explain what happened between the warning and effective intervention, not simply point to the eventual existence of a long clinic letter.

The neurological consultation later in 2023 contains significant observations. The doctor considered cramping in the left lower limb as possible Parkinsonian dystonia and thought an OFF state more likely than an ON state. He described a sudden onset of severe pain as not characteristic of the usual development of peripheral neuropathy. He considered the possibility that the difficulty walking in the evenings was related to inadequate levodopa. There were further differential investigations, including testing for Fabry disease, subsequently reported as normal. There was genuine medical activity directed towards understanding the changes in the patient's condition. In a separate movement-disorders contact, the nurse recorded that the leg pain and shuffling appeared OFF-related and that the patient was being undertreated. Following discussion with the consultant, the medication was adjusted to Stalevo with an arrangement for feedback. The relationship between pain, movement and neurological treatment had again been recognised within the service.

The neurologist was again considering OFF dystonia. The nurse was connecting pain and shuffling to undertreatment. The patient was describing the difference neurological medication made to his ability to move. The clinical team was acquiring observations that reached back to the original question. The patient did not need to remember the second Monday for that question to matter. The specialist had the clinical history.

In 2024 the consultant recorded an unusually poignant description of the patient's circumstances. Moloney was generally well, he explained, and functioning within his day, but life was “a lot smaller”. Folding clothes took a long time. Pumping bicycle tyres for seven minutes could produce toe curling. He had gone from playing darts three hours a day to ninety minutes a week, substantially because of painful standing. Pregabalin had helped the feet, especially the right; some pain remained in the left. It had not relieved the toe curling during darts. He could still walk or cycle considerable distances. He had found ways of living within his boundaries.

Twenty-one hours of darts a week had become an hour and a half. Still, Moloney said the medication was “spot on”. He had learned to live within his boundaries. The words were an account of how he was managing his life, not a measurement of the life he had lost. The doctor had the longitudinal record against which that loss could be measured.

There is still an outside-left-foot symptom within this history. It appears in the 2018 observation, in the 2023 description of discomfort outside the left foot, and in the residual left-sided pain of 2024. The widespread burning pain of the 2023 crisis should not automatically be treated as identical to the original focal complaint. That distinction makes the focal thread more important rather than less. An independent examination ought to be able to follow the history of each painful phenomenon, establish which observations belong together and determine how neurological treatment modified them. The patient's description of different sensations in 2017 should have been the beginning of such an inquiry, not a substitute for its conclusion.

VI. The mornings, and the answer that changed the past

On the morning of 14 March 2025, Moloney’s relationship with his own past changed. He had gone to bed the previous night with a new controlled-release prescription. He woke to a different experience of his feet. Until that morning he had been living forward, trying to make his life work. Now he found himself looking back.

The preceding neurological consultation is an extraordinary document to read after the one from April 2018. The patient hobbled out of bed. The feet were so painful that he could barely tie his runners. The first hundred and fifty footsteps were torture. He described murder in the feet. Once the Parkinson's medication took effect, the experience changed. He could walk hundreds of steps over rocks in his wellingtons. The clinical account contained both states, separated within the course of a morning by the effect of neurological medication.

Monaghan considered the possibilities in familiar terms. A degree of peripheral neuropathy remained possible, and cessation of alcohol was welcomed. Pregabalin had been reduced by the patient following improvement and remained an option for the pain. The clinician considered pain amplification and the risks of increasing Sinemet. He expressly recognised painful dystonia as a phenomenon but did not consider this kind of pain directly Parkinsonian. More Sinemet might give further relief, he thought, though the pain was unlikely to go away. The patient's explanation was that there might be an element of how poorly controlled his Parkinsonism would be in the morning after the overnight medication gap. A controlled-release preparation at night was considered reasonable. The prescription was made on 10 March; Moloney records collecting and first taking it on 13 March, and the following morning as the turning point. His initial complaint would describe an independent trial; the clinic prescription and the patient's initiation of the treatment must be kept distinct. The important observation belongs to both accounts: substantial relief followed the change.

The prediction was limited relief. The experience was altogether different. Monaghan himself would acknowledge “profound relief currently” in his response to the complaint. The patient was astonished by what had happened to the pain. Here was something that could be experienced directly, without a medical argument, and it forced him to reconsider the years that preceded it.

The relief brought the past back. In 2018 the patient had surrendered into the experience of wellness. He had forgotten the protest. He had gone to work and enjoyed being alive. What was there to investigate when the medicine had worked? In March 2025 the same experience of suddenly regaining something he had lost provoked a different reaction. He could not make sense of the years between the two recoveries. The complaint followed on 19 March. The documentary investigation came afterwards.

The complaint began with a patient who wanted to know why the therapeutic possibility had not been investigated sooner, why an explanation involving neuropathy and alcohol had retained such importance, and why the understanding of his condition had not been reconsidered in the light of the response. It asked for independent review, disclosure and preservation of records. He did not at that stage possess the reconstructed sequence of the documents that would make the July 2017 issue so formidable. The records investigation followed the therapeutic revelation. To represent the patient as having been consciously pursuing this argument during the intervening years would be to misunderstand the experience that had brought him to it.

Monaghan responded on 25 March. He explained the difficulties of treating a young person with Parkinson's, the risk of dyskinesia and other adverse effects, the clinical acceptance that Sinemet relieved the experience of pain and the reasons for using pregabalin partly to avoid further Sinemet escalation. He described Parkinsonism as amplifying pain from other causes. He stated his belief that the patient had idiopathic Parkinson's disease. He referred to the extensive correspondence, the introduction of Sinemet in 2021, his willingness to consider different explanations and the investigations that had been undertaken. He reproduced the 2017 explanation of the strategy of delaying dopamine replacement, including the recognition that one might later regret not having started earlier. The old account had become an evidential resource in the defence of the intervening years.

The response was an explanation of the treatment of Parkinsonism. It set out the risks, the prescriptions and the reasons for caution. It emphasised that the majority of what had been done was concerned with Parkinson’s. Moloney had said something very similar on the second Monday of his admission. The hospital had investigated Parkinsonism. What he wanted to know was why it had not adequately investigated his feet.

The complaint could be understood as an argument about when a particular medication should have been prescribed. On that narrower question the clinician could point to prescriptions, risks, recorded discussions and the date in 2021 on which levodopa began. But the history of the feet was not answered by the commencement date of a drug. The issue was the specialist's recognition of a movement phenomenon and the subsequent handling of evidence that it bore a relationship to the feet, while the representation which had allowed the problem to be treated as separate remained uncorrected. The original investigation had been challenged before anybody had to decide whether Sinemet was appropriate. The patient had asked for the investigation, not for a particular drug. The medication response of 2025 brought the original question back into view; it did not create it.

Monaghan explained that his long letters were intended to bridge the gap between what was said and what was understood, to show how decisions had been made, when and by whom. He expected the reviewer to find the reasoning within them. The patient was now confronting precisely that claim. He had spoken to the neurologist on 10 July. The document designed to bridge the gap between their respective understandings did not record what he had said. Instead it attributed a position to him that supported the disposition he had challenged.

It is at this point that the original disparity becomes an evidential disparity in a dispute. The doctor invokes the record as the means by which his care may be understood. The patient must demonstrate that the record failed to convey something of fundamental significance about the care. The doctor can point to extensive explanations written in the ordinary course of his work. The patient must recover a conversation for which the doctor was responsible for providing a clinical account. If the reviewer reads the letters without examining their construction, the very omission that is the subject of the complaint can affect the apparent strength of the complaint. A review that proceeds on the authority of the completed document may never reach the clinical state of affairs to which the document should have answered.

The correspondence involving Counihan sharpens this problem. In 2017 the two neurologists' preference to delay levodopa had been communicated to Healy in collective terms. On 7 May 2025 Counihan responded that he had not been involved in Moloney's care, referring the matter to the treating clinician. Monaghan subsequently explained to the Medical Council that the advice was given in the initial complex-case discussion and that Counihan had no continuing involvement in management. In July the Council declined to proceed with the complaint about Counihan on the basis of his lack of direct involvement. The distinction between collegial advice and continuing care is legitimate. It is not an answer to the question of what the initial advice encompassed. The senior neurologist may have been asked to consider medication timing. If that was the scope of his involvement, his judgment cannot retrospectively establish the adequacy of an examination of the feet. The relevant information is what the meeting considered, what its members were supplied with and what responsibility followed from its discussion. It is an early version of the same issue that would shortly arise in a much more consequential institutional meeting.

VII. The hospital adopts the account

Initially the complaint followed the pathway for concerns about clinical judgment. In April 2025 the Quality and Patient Safety office explained the limits of the ordinary complaints investigation in that regard; the clinician supplied the clinical response. By 30 May the matter had entered the Incident Management Framework and a Preliminary Assessment Report was to be considered. In June the hospital indicated that an independent review would be undertaken following consultation with the clinical director of medicine, and asked the patient to suspend further correspondence temporarily because of the volume of material. These are important steps in the history, because they establish the institution's knowledge of the existence of a dispute and its assumption of responsibility for examining it.

On 27 August the Preliminary Assessment Report was presented to the Serious Incident Management Team. The outcome was conveyed on 3 September in correspondence from Quality and Patient Safety and the clinical director of medicine. The letter identified the complaint as concerning decisions anchored in an understanding of foot pain that was not reflective of the patient's case, and the allegation that Sinemet CR had not been prescribed until March 2025, with prolonged pain as a consequence. The multidisciplinary team was satisfied that the treatment decisions had been appropriate. It referred to consideration of risk factors in starting Sinemet at a young age, alternative potential diagnoses and the introduction of Sinemet in 2021. It concluded that there had been no deficit in the care provided.

The hospital described a complaint about decisions anchored in an account not reflective of the patient. It answered with the timing and risks of Sinemet and the consideration of alternative diagnoses. The conclusion was “no deficit”. The explanation disclosed no examination of the circumstances in which that account of the patient had been made authoritative. It had answered a question about treatment without showing that it had examined the informational foundation of the treatment.

By the time of that meeting the institution had received the patient's particularised request concerning the July protest and the students, and had supplied an August records response. That establishes institutional receipt; the undisclosed PAR and meeting material are needed to determine what the individual reviewers were actually given. The parallel is striking. In 2017 the complex-case meeting had access to neurological expertise, but it remains unclear what representation of the patient was placed before it. In 2025 a multidisciplinary meeting had access to the authority of an institutional review, but it remains unclear what representation of the original investigation was placed before it. In neither instance can the status of the people in the room establish the adequacy of the question they were asked to consider.

A reviewer reading the consultant’s letters does not thereby acquire the consultant’s knowledge of 10 July. Unless the review independently examines the disputed account against the original events, it inherits the very disparity at the heart of the complaint. The hospital must show what it examined: the original referral, the interview, the completed Impression, the protest, the clinical work on the feet and the subsequent therapeutic evidence. It must show that it investigated the construction of the account rather than merely the medicine practised within it.

The patient's efforts to secure that examination would increasingly move into the field of records and data governance. There is a particular irony in this development. The clinical record had been intended, among other things, as an aid to the patient in understanding his condition. It was now the instrument through which the hospital's treatment might be justified, and the patient was trying to establish what it had failed to say about him. He asked under Article 16 of the GDPR for correction or annotation. The September response treated the matter in terms of erasure and the need to preserve genuine historical medical opinion. That principle does not meet the particularity of his request. Preserving the historical fact that a neurologist considered peripheral neuropathy is not the same as establishing that the neurologist accurately represented the patient's understanding of his feet. One is a proposition about what the clinician believed; the other is a proposition about what another person communicated. A supplementary statement can preserve both positions without pretending that a dispute never occurred.

The records requests are not merely administrative irritants attached to the medical history. The patient cannot establish when the critical words entered the discharge account unless the hospital can locate its native version history or explain what records exist. He cannot identify what the serious-incident reviewers considered without the sources they were supplied with. The four medical students may represent a separate route to evidence about the event, involving ward and teaching records as well as the relevant university. Local podiatry and hospital neurology material may reside in different administrative systems. The difficulty of obtaining these things is itself closely related to the informational distribution of the original care. One organisation's absence of a record does not establish that a separate controller does not hold it. An access request must be answered by reference to an appropriate search, not by treating the fragmentation of record custody as evidence that the corresponding clinical relationship did not matter.

The later requests concerning restriction of processing under Article 18 raise questions about how contested information was handled while the dispute about accuracy was outstanding. The patient's dispute about whether some correspondence constituted personal data requires its own governance answer. Neither the hospital's clinical judgment nor an administrative response can stand in for a determination of those separate issues. In January 2026 the Quality and Patient Safety response took the position that a later dystonia diagnosis did not undermine the contemporaneous review. In August the Data Protection Commission's assessment was ongoing; this was not a regulatory finding. On 31 August Moloney sought information about two objects: the original July record, including its provenance and transmission, and the institutional review, including its source and decision history. The signed supplementary statement of 2 September supplies a documentary home for his account of the protest. Subsequent correspondence and complaints require confirmation against the controller's receipt and response records. The point of these requests is to reconstruct the circumstances in which the institution came to know what it believes it knows. They ask for the evidential foundation of the answers already given.

Meanwhile, on 4 February 2026, Seán O'Riordan, the movement-disorders neurologist at St Vincent's, wrote to Brennan confirming that Moloney had two pathogenic variants in the Parkin gene. His Parkinsonism and dystonia were attributable to compound heterozygous mutations. The letter belongs at the end of this history, but its resonance comes from the beginning. A dystonic-looking movement had been observed in July 2017. The possibility of PARK2 disease was entertained by Healy in September. It was explicitly suspected again in the consultation in which Monaghan documented the outside-left-foot observation. The genetic finding is new evidence of the cause of the movement disorder; it does not recreate the original feet or prove the origin of each painful lesion. It makes it all the more necessary to establish how the neurological evidence known at the time was investigated and carried forward. The later designation of idiopathic Parkinson's disease in the defence merits examination against the earlier suspicion. The hospital cannot answer a question about the adequacy of its earlier investigation merely by observing that later knowledge is later.

There is a natural difficulty for an institution confronted with a challenge of this kind. A correction in July 2017 might have required another examination, another conversation, a further referral and an additional paragraph in a clinical document. In 2018 it might have required a more explicit reconsideration in the light of an extraordinary therapeutic response. By 2025 it involved eight years of treatment decisions and the explanations of the people who had made them. By 2026 it involved the institution's own declaration of adequacy, the governance responses and the history of the patient's efforts to be heard. The practical cost of correcting the original account had increased as the account acquired authority. That is an institutional circumstance, not evidence of a particular person's private motive. It explains why the independence and remit of the examination matter so much. The patient had always had a much simpler interest: to obtain the medical understanding and treatment his physical condition required.

In July 2025 Monaghan told the Medical Council that he accepted he might be wrong, but thought and hoped that his management had been correct. Moloney remembers what it was like when that management worked. The patient’s case is not that his neurologist could never make him well. It is that he knew how well he could be, possessed evidence of what made him well, and had authorised an account that did not communicate the challenge to the understanding under which his treatment proceeded. That is what makes the history so difficult for the patient to reconcile.

The hospital has said there was no deficit. It can demonstrate that conclusion by producing the answer to Brennan’s original question, the examination and reasoning on the feet; by showing how the patient’s understanding was represented and when that wording entered the account; by establishing what happened on 10 July; by explaining how the treatment responses were brought to bear on the original disposition; and by disclosing what its reviewers actually examined. Those are the questions Moloney has spent the last twenty months trying to get answered. The date on which levodopa first appeared in a prescription cannot answer them.

The most difficult document for the hospital may eventually prove to be the consultation letter of 24 April 2018. It describes a man who had regained the capacity to walk with ease, had returned to work and enjoyed being alive. The clinician knew what the man had looked like before treatment. He examined him again. He filmed him again. He knew what had happened when the medication was interrupted, including the specific foot phenomenon the patient had described. There was, at that moment, an opportunity not merely to continue a successful treatment but to understand what the success had revealed. The patient did not have to ask the neurologist to remember it for him. That was the purpose of medicine's knowledge.

Five years later Brennan was asking for urgent help for a man whose pain had produced a wish for death. Two years after that, another change in medication brought profound relief. Moloney had not expected to become a historian of his own illness. He had expected to get on with his life. The difference between those expectations is what he is trying to make intelligible now.

In July 2017 the hospital had the patient in front of it. His GP had described the question. The neurologist's own examination had given the question greater clinical significance. The patient had drawn the neurologist's attention to the inadequate investigation while it was still possible to do something about it. The account that emerged from the admission made no mention of the challenge. It went out into medicine in his place.

Nine years later the patient is trying to get back into it.
