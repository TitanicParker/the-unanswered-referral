The Hospital Had Its Chance

In July 2017, a man entered a Galway teaching hospital with painfully abnormal feet and a disorder of movement. The hospital possessed the expertise to investigate him, the opportunity to examine him and, according to the patient, an explicit warning that something important had been missed. Eight years later, the institution would declare that there had been no deficit in his care. The medical record on which that history rests deserves a considerably closer reading.

I. The second Monday

On Monday, 10 July 2017, Galway University Hospitals still had an opportunity to do something about Liam Moloney's feet.

He was in the hospital. His feet were available for examination. His movement disorder had been recognised. His longstanding muscular tension had been identified and treated. His GP had sent a referral describing an extraordinary combination of physical difficulties in a man of thirty-nine whose capacity to work was rapidly deteriorating.

And now, according to Moloney, he was telling his neurologist that something had been missed.

He recalls four medical students attending the ward that morning. He asked them to summon his consultant, Dr Thomas Monaghan. He remembers reaching for an analogy familiar to anyone in Ireland: the National Car Test. What was the value of putting a car through an extensive inspection if it was going to emerge with bald tyres?

The hospital had investigated him extensively. But he was still standing on the problem he had brought into it.

There is something particularly disturbing about the timing of this alleged encounter. It did not occur after the deterioration of subsequent years. It did not occur in a solicitor's office, assisted by hindsight, genetic results or an acquired understanding of the medical literature. It occurred, on Moloney's account, inside the institution that was investigating him, before the investigation had formally concluded.

The patient was available. The problem was available. The specialist was available.

The opportunity was alive.

Nobody needed to reconstruct the condition of Moloney's feet from photographs taken years afterwards. Nobody needed to establish what medication he had been taking in some distant period of his life. Nobody needed to debate whether his recollection of a physical symptom had deteriorated with time.

He was there.

The four students have not yet independently corroborated his account. But there is something that can be independently examined: the completed clinical document that emerged from that admission.

It contains an account of the patient's understanding of the relationship between his feet and his muscular tension. It contains an interpretation of his earlier experiences. It contains a description of his clinical presentation, the neurologist's impressions and the intended management of his condition.

It contains no identifiable account of the objection Moloney says he made.

On 17 July, at half past seven in the evening, the discharge summary was confirmed.

The hospital had its account.

The patient had his illness.

For the next eight years, the two would travel together.

II. The question that arrived in Galway

There is nothing particularly obscure about the beginning of this case.

It begins in general practice, with a man whose feet hurt.

On 10 April 2017, Dr Aaron Brennan recorded bilateral foot pain, abnormal gait, mallet toes, calluses and pressure areas. Moloney was having difficulty working on his feet. The abnormalities were physically apparent. They were not merely sensations reported by a patient whom medicine could not quite understand.

By June, Brennan was considering something more extensive.

The patient was having difficulty initiating movement. His muscular tension was becoming increasingly conspicuous. His gait was abnormal. His ability to perform ordinary occupational activities was deteriorating.

On 8 June, the GP recorded the intention to seek a neurological opinion of the patient's:

“gait, feet and ‘muscle tension’”

Three things. One referral.

There was considerable intelligence in that formulation. Brennan was not attempting to solve the neurological problem himself. He was identifying the relationship that required specialist attention.

By 23 June, there was markedly increased tone in both legs and brisk reflexes in the upper limbs. By 29 June, the deterioration in dexterity and occupational function had become sufficiently concerning that urgent hospital assessment was arranged.

The referral described a man who could not properly relax his muscles, whose walking had changed, and whose toes clawed.

Brennan suspected Parkinsonism or some other underlying condition.

He had not simply referred a man with suspected Parkinson's disease. He had referred a deteriorating physical presentation in which the feet occupied a conspicuous place.

The significance of that distinction becomes apparent almost immediately.

In the hospital's abbreviated representation of the referral, the patient appears as follows:

“39Y/O WITH DIFFICULTY INITIATING MOVEMENT, INCREASED TONE AND STIFFNESS IN ALL 4 LIMBS, COGWHEELING AT THE WRIST.”

The feet have disappeared.

So has the clawing of the toes.

The uncertainty attached to the GP's assessment of possible cogwheeling is no longer apparent.

What remains is an efficient description of a possible Parkinsonian presentation. It is considerably less effective as a description of the problem Brennan had been investigating.

The hospital may have possessed both documents. The treating neurologist may have read the original referral. The surviving material does not establish the precise route by which the information entered the specialist's thinking.

But the compression itself is visible.

A GP had recognised a relationship worth investigating. The receiving institution produced a representation from which important elements of that relationship were absent.

This is where the first opportunity arose.

Had the compressed representation been corrected against the original referral, the hospital would have been obliged to confront the feet as part of the neurological question, rather than merely encounter them as another feature of a man with a movement disorder.

There is a considerable difference between those two things.

One asks what disease the patient has.

The other asks what that disease is doing to the patient.

Galway would make substantial progress with the first.

The second would become the subject of a dispute lasting nearly a decade.

III. The expertise was already in the building

It would be easier to understand what followed had Moloney entered an institution without the specialist resources to recognise his condition.

He did not.

University Hospital Galway was a major teaching hospital with an established neurology service, academic affiliations and access to specialist knowledge of Parkinson's disease and movement disorders.

Dr Thomas Monaghan was not conducting a speculative investigation outside his field. He was a consultant neurologist.

Professor Timothy Counihan, whose advice would subsequently feature in the management of Moloney's condition, had a professional and academic background that included Parkinsonism, movement abnormalities and the genetics of Parkinson's disease.

In 2015, Counihan had been among the authors of an article examining what medicine had learned about the genetics of Parkinson's disease.

The relevant intellectual environment existed.

The hospital did not need to be introduced to the concept of abnormal movement. It did not need to discover that neurological disease could produce abnormal posture. It did not need to learn, from a thirty-nine-year-old patient, that the position and movement of a foot might have something to do with the neurological machinery governing it.

Indeed, Monaghan's own examination generated an observation of extraordinary relevance.

He described a gait with a:

“footdrop appearance somewhat suggestive of a dystonic posture - NOT FOOT DROP”

Read that observation against Brennan's referral.

The GP had described painful abnormal feet, clawing toes and altered gait.

The neurologist had observed something resembling foot drop that he considered more suggestive of a dystonic posture.

The referral and the specialist examination had begun to converge.

This was not an obscure connection that could only become apparent after the genetic discoveries of 2026. It was present in July 2017, in the language of the treating neurologist.

The hospital had acquired a relevant positive finding.

It also acquired another important piece of knowledge: the muscular tension described by the patient was sufficiently significant for neurological treatment.

The discharge material records:

“Clonazepam was given at night to relieve tension in the legs.”

Consider what had happened within the admission.

A man had entered with a longstanding problem of muscular tension, abnormal movement and painful feet. The neurologist had recognised a movement abnormality suggestive of dystonia and had assumed therapeutic responsibility for the tension.

The hospital was no longer merely in possession of a GP's concern.

It had independently generated evidence relevant to that concern.

The consultant had the patient's history, the examination findings and access to colleagues with specialist expertise.

The question is not whether the hospital had sufficient information to establish an immediate and definitive explanation of every lesion on Moloney's feet.

The question is what it did with the information it unquestionably possessed.

Somewhere in the activity of the next several days, the investigation of a complex presentation became predominantly the investigation and management of Parkinsonism.

There was imaging. There were laboratory investigations. There was specialist discussion. There was consideration of the risks of introducing levodopa in a relatively young patient. There was a plan to obtain further diagnostic information.

This was substantial medicine.

And it is precisely the substantial nature of that medicine that makes the unresolved question so striking.

The patient had not been referred to an institution incapable of investigating him.

He had been referred to an institution that could do a great deal.

What remained at issue was whether it had done the particular thing his feet required.

IV. Where do I begin?

The first page of the neurologist's handwritten interview, dated 3 July 2017, begins with four words:

“Where do I begin?”

They are unusually revealing words to encounter in a medical document.

Moloney had more than a decade of experiences to communicate. His body had changed gradually. He had adapted to sensations that were difficult to describe. He had worked, changed employment, sought treatment and attempted to understand what was happening to him.

Now he was sitting opposite someone qualified to make sense of it.

His arms and legs no longer synchronised properly when he walked. His handwriting had deteriorated. His balance was poor. He believed a fall was imminent. His dexterity had diminished to the point where ordinary activities involving cardboard boxes had become difficult.

He recalled attending the hospital in 2014 with physical tension in his arms and legs.

“Wasn't seen by Neurology then.”

The second handwritten page records his belief that the condition he was experiencing in 2017 represented the maturation of the earlier condition.

The neurologist wrote:

“Liam feels this current state is the maturation of that.”

There is a patient attempting to connect the history of his own body.

Not a patient attempting to divide it.

Moloney traced the tension back to approximately 2004. He described an apparently contradictory experience: in order to feel relaxed in bed, he had to place his body into positions in which he could feel tension.

His account moved from bodily tension and posturing to the development of problems with his feet.

On the third handwritten page appeared the words:

“Corns”
“Calluses”
“Fallen arches”

He recalled treatment for his feet in 2009 and 2010.

Then he described an important development in 2012, when he left bar work.

Standing for long periods had aggravated his foot pain. Leaving that employment substantially reduced that particular experience. With the pain less prominent, he became more aware of the persistent tension.

The handwritten account records that foot pain and leg tension:

“weren't the same”

This was not a declaration by Moloney that he had two medically unrelated diseases.

It was the description of a man who could distinguish one unpleasant physical experience from another.

The distinction is hardly remarkable. A person can distinguish the pain of a cramped hand from the pain of a blister produced by the position of that hand. A person can distinguish the ache of a joint from the muscular forces acting around it.

Indeed, describing such differences is precisely what a patient is supposed to do when communicating with a doctor.

Moloney could tell Monaghan what he experienced.

He could not tell Monaghan what caused it.

That was why he was in the hospital.

Yet the distinction would acquire a substantially different character by the time it appeared in the neurologist's completed impression.

The document would say that Moloney had:

“in his own mind - markedly divorced”

the feelings of tension in his legs from the problems with his feet.

The movement between these two formulations is the documentary heart of the case.

“Weren't the same” describes a distinction between experiences.

“Markedly divorced” characterises the patient's understanding.

“In his own mind” places the separation inside the patient.

The words are no longer merely reporting what he felt. They are describing what the doctor says he believed.

And that description appears alongside a proposed explanation of the feet as possibly separate or attributable to secondary peripheral neuropathy.

The patient had supplied the history.

The doctor had supplied the medical hypothesis.

The completed account brought them into alignment.

Moloney's allegation is that this alignment profoundly misrepresented the clinical situation and his own understanding of it.

Whatever the eventual explanation for the wording, the institutional importance of that wording cannot be dismissed.

The document did not simply travel into an archive. It became part of the authoritative description of the patient.

It was a description available to future clinicians, to his GP and, eventually, to those investigating the adequacy of his treatment.

His own supposed understanding had become part of the clinical evidence.

The man who said his current condition was the maturation of a longstanding physical problem had emerged from the investigation represented as someone who had markedly divorced important components of that problem in his own mind.

There is an extraordinary amount of medical consequence contained in that small change of language.

V. The anatomy of an answer

Three phrases in the completed July 2017 account deserve to be read together.

The first concerns the possible relationship between the painful feet and the wider neurological presentation:

“Not clear.”

The second concerns the patient's supposed understanding:

“in his own mind - markedly divorced”

The third concerns management:

“As per above.”

Uncertainty. Attribution. Disposition.

The neurologist had not established that the painful feet were caused by peripheral neuropathy.

He had not established that the feet were medically unrelated to the abnormal movement.

He had observed a dystonic-looking posture, encountered structural abnormalities and treated the longstanding muscular tension.

He then produced an impression in which an uncertain medical explanation sat beside a much stronger characterisation of the patient's understanding.

The plan followed.

What happened to the unresolved clinical question in the movement from the impression to the plan?

Where was the specific commitment to establish the relationship between the neurological findings and the painful feet?

Where was the clear allocation of that investigation?

Who was going to establish whether the clawing toes were manifestations of the movement disorder?

Who would examine the relationship between altered muscular activity, foot posture, abnormal loading and the recurrent pressure lesions?

What information would return to neurology from podiatry?

What would constitute a reason to reopen the hypothesis?

The patient was not asking for a guarantee of certainty.

He was asking for medicine.

There is nothing inherently objectionable about a consultant recording a provisional diagnosis. Clinical investigation frequently proceeds through hypotheses. A conscientious doctor can identify several possible explanations, initiate treatment and arrange for further assessment.

But uncertainty generates work.

It is not an answer to the work.

The GP had already recognised that further specialist examination was necessary. He had referred the patient for precisely that reason.

For the hospital to return a document identifying a possible explanation without visibly establishing how that explanation would be investigated was to risk returning the uncertainty that had prompted the referral in the first place.

Only now the uncertainty possessed specialist authority.

A GP receiving the discharge account would encounter an established neurological diagnosis, an extensive hospital investigation and a consultant's impression.

He would also encounter the representation that the patient himself had markedly separated the tension from the feet.

The hierarchy of medicine matters here.

The GP had asked the specialist a question. The specialist's answer would naturally carry greater authority than the GP's original uncertainty.

The patient, meanwhile, had entered the hospital because he did not possess the expertise to understand the condition of his own body.

He was hardly equipped to audit the neurological reasoning contained in a discharge summary.

The account could therefore acquire an authority far beyond the confidence of the particular hypothesis it contained.

The words “Not clear” remained in the document.

But the patient had been discharged.

The uncertainty was still on paper.

The disposition was already in operation.

VI. The hospital's eight days

The admission lasted from Monday, 3 July, until Tuesday, 11 July.

It is worth considering just how much contingency existed within those eight days.

A different question asked on the first Monday might have changed the direction of the investigation.

A different emphasis in the presentation to the complex-case meeting might have changed what the specialists considered.

A careful examination of Brennan's original referral might have brought the painful feet into sharper focus.

A dedicated assessment of the observed dystonic-looking movement might have generated findings relevant to the toe clawing.

A documented comparison of the patient's physical presentation before and after neurological medication might have provided a clinical baseline of lasting value.

A clear instruction to investigate the feet further, with responsibility assigned to a named service, might have transformed the eventual discharge.

None of these possibilities required a medical revolution.

They required ordinary opportunities for correction within an investigation that was already taking place.

And then there was the second Monday.

Moloney's account is that by 10 July he had become concerned that the problem with his feet was not receiving the attention it required.

He had watched the hospital investigate him. He had experienced its considerable activity. He had been told about Parkinsonism.

But he understood that identifying a neurological condition had not necessarily answered the question of his feet.

There is something remarkable in the possibility that the patient recognised this distinction before his admission ended.

He did not have the vocabulary of a movement-disorders specialist. He did not know that genetic testing would eventually identify pathogenic variants in the Parkin gene. He did not know what the next eight years would contain.

He simply knew what he had come into the hospital with and what he believed had not been adequately examined.

He says he communicated it.

Medicine has an extraordinary resource in a conscious, articulate patient who recognises something about his condition that deserves attention.

That resource is particularly valuable during an admission, when the patient can still be examined and the clinical direction can still be changed.

On Moloney's account, the hospital possessed that resource on 10 July.

The neurologist did not need to accept the patient's medical judgment.

He needed to recognise the clinical significance of the concern.

The response might have been examination. It might have been an explanation of what had already been established. It might have been further specialist input. It might have been a definite arrangement for investigation after discharge.

It might have taken an afternoon.

The completed record does not identify the objection or its resolution.

This absence becomes especially difficult to overlook when one considers the amount of interpretative weight the same document placed on the patient's earlier account of his symptoms.

The earlier patient was useful to the explanation.

The later patient, if his testimony is correct, was challenging it.

The completed document preserved the first.

It did not visibly preserve the second.

By the time the discharge summary was confirmed on 17 July, the opportunity to examine Moloney in the circumstances of that admission had passed.

The document would survive.

The circumstances would not.

VII. The examination that cannot now be performed

In a dispute about medical treatment, it is natural to look for the eventual consequences.

How much pain did the patient experience? How much function did he lose? What medication could have been introduced sooner? What difference would it have made?

But Moloney's case contains an earlier loss, one that is easy to overlook because it does not present itself as a physical injury.

It is the loss of a particular opportunity to know.

In July 2017, the hospital had before it a man with longstanding muscular tension, abnormal gait, painful feet, clawing toes, pressure lesions and an observed posture suggestive of dystonia.

What would a detailed examination of those phenomena have established?

What would his feet have looked like when the tension was relieved?

What would have happened to the posture of his toes?

How did the location of the corns correspond to his abnormal movement?

What was fixed? What was dynamic?

What happened to his pain when the neurological presentation changed?

What findings supported a peripheral explanation?

What findings supported a neurological contribution to the structural and mechanical difficulties?

These questions belong to the living body of the patient in July 2017.

They cannot now be answered with the same certainty by looking at him in 2026.

His disease has progressed. He has taken numerous medications. He has undergone years of podiatric treatment. His physical condition and functional circumstances have changed.

Even a brilliant specialist examining him today cannot return him to the second Monday of that admission.

That is why the original opportunity mattered.

It is also why the dispute over the medical record matters.

If the hospital did not establish a sufficiently informative baseline, the patient subsequently faces a peculiar difficulty. He must demonstrate the consequences of an investigation whose missing findings would have helped him establish those consequences.

The absence becomes part of the problem of proving the absence.

Meanwhile, the original interpretation remains available in a clinical document.

The institution retains its historical account.

The patient cannot recover his historical body.

This is not merely an evidential inconvenience. It is a profound imbalance in the circumstances of retrospective investigation.

The hospital possessed the opportunity to document the state of affairs when the relevant state of affairs existed.

The patient depended upon it to do so.

Years later, he must challenge the resulting account without access to the very clinical opportunity that the hospital once possessed.

The passage of time does not affect both sides equally.

It preserves the authority of the written record while destroying the possibility of repeating the original examination.

VIII. The men who understood the medicine

After the admission, Monaghan sought the opinion of Professor Daniel Healy, a neurologist with expertise in the genetics of movement disorders.

The correspondence contains a strikingly candid description of the management strategy:

“Myself and Tim are keen to hold back on Levodopa insofar as maybe possible unless you'd advise otherwise?”

The “Tim” was Timothy Counihan.

The question of delaying levodopa in younger patients was a familiar clinical consideration. The possibility of dyskinesia and other complications mattered. These were legitimate medical concerns, discussed by men who understood the implications of the treatment.

But what, precisely, had their strategy answered?

It had answered a question about the management of Parkinsonism.

It had not necessarily answered the question Brennan had sent with the patient.

There are two distinct medical decisions at issue.

One concerns the timing of a particular medication.

The other concerns establishing what is happening to a man's painful and physically abnormal feet.

The second is not extinguished by the first.

Yet the timing of levodopa would eventually occupy a commanding position in the defence of Moloney's treatment.

It is worth seeing how extraordinary that development is when the original referral is returned to the centre of the story.

Brennan had not written to Galway asking its neurologists to prescribe levodopa.

Moloney says he had not summoned Monaghan on 10 July to demand levodopa.

He had challenged the investigation of his feet.

The hospital's eventual defence would emphasise the clinical wisdom of being cautious with a medication whose immediate introduction the patient had not originally demanded.

The institution had acquired a defensible answer to one question.

Years later, that answer would be placed before a substantially different question.

In September 2017, Healy expressed the opinion that Moloney probably had PARK2-associated disease and suggested genetic testing.

This was important.

A genetic explanation was being entertained within months of the admission, not discovered as an entirely unforeseen possibility nine years afterwards.

The hospital's specialist network was capable of recognising the unusual nature of the presentation.

Yet the history of Moloney's feet remained unresolved in the documents through which that expertise was expressed.

The intellectual resources were there.

The specialist knowledge was there.

The man whose feet required explanation was there.

This was not a case in which the relevant medicine lay beyond the horizon of contemporary knowledge.

It was a case in which relevant clinical knowledge was being actively acquired, while the question of how that knowledge related to the original painful-foot presentation remained inadequately answered in the surviving account.

IX. The year medicine worked

The most important event in Moloney's clinical history may not have been the admission.

It may have been what happened afterwards.

The initial neurological treatment worked.

Procyclidine, alongside rasagiline and the developing treatment regime, produced an extraordinary improvement in the patient's physical function.

His movement improved. His tension diminished. His walking became recognisable to him again.

In October 2017, in correspondence concerning the withdrawal of procyclidine because of visual difficulties, Moloney used a wonderfully uncomplicated expression:

“My walk is back together”

There is no medical theory in that sentence.

There is the experience of a man recovering something that had been coming apart.

By his account, the improvement represented the best physical condition he had experienced since 2004.

For approximately thirteen years, he had lived with an accumulating collection of physical difficulties. He had sought treatment for his feet. He had adapted his employment. He had attempted to make sense of the tension in his body. He had experienced deterioration in his movement.

And then medicine had made him considerably better.

There is no need to diminish this achievement in order to prosecute the case.

On the contrary, the achievement is essential to it.

Galway had demonstrated something extraordinarily valuable about Moloney's condition.

It was substantially modifiable.

The patient responded to this discovery in the way one would hope a patient might respond to successful treatment.

He got on with his life.

The confrontation he recalls from the second Monday of the admission ceased to occupy his attention. Why would he preserve a dispute about an investigation when the treatment emerging from that investigation had transformed his physical circumstances?

He was not preparing litigation.

He was not monitoring the consistency of the neurologist's differential diagnosis.

He was enjoying the benefit of medicine.

The improvement was so substantial that it changed his relationship with the events that had preceded it.

What mattered to him was no longer what the hospital had failed to investigate. What mattered was that the hospital appeared to have made him well.

This distinction is indispensable to understanding the years that followed.

The patient was entitled to experience recovery without assuming responsibility for the intellectual implications of that recovery.

That responsibility belonged elsewhere.

And the medical significance of the treatment became more conspicuous when procyclidine had to be withdrawn.

On 4 October 2017, Moloney described what was happening:

“I am feeling the tension building up, my feet are restless and my walk is falling apart....but my eyesight is back to normal.”

The treatment had presented a tolerability problem.

Its withdrawal had produced a clinical observation.

There was a loss of benefit.

The tension was returning. The walking was deteriorating. The feet were again becoming troublesome.

The patient had experienced both states.

The neurologist would subsequently document a particularly revealing aspect of the change.

In the April 2018 consultation, Monaghan recorded that:

“the first thing he noticed changing and changing rapidly was the return on the foot, of the outside left corn.”

The observation was remarkably specific.

The patient had not merely complained of feeling generally worse without his neurological medication.

He had identified the return of a phenomenon at a particular anatomical site, associated with his longstanding foot difficulties.

The significance of this observation lies not in claiming that a corn could conclusively establish a neurological diagnosis.

It lies in what the observation presented to the treating doctor.

The neurologist had documented a dystonic-looking movement in July.

He had heard the patient's account of longstanding tension and abnormal feet.

He had participated in a treatment regime that substantially improved the man.

He now had a report of the return of a specific foot problem associated with the withdrawal of a neurological medication.

The possible connection between the neurological presentation and the original painful feet had been supplied with further evidence.

This was a clinical opportunity of considerable importance.

The patient was better.

The doctor knew he was better.

The doctor knew something about the deterioration that accompanied the interruption of treatment.

Medicine had begun to answer, through treatment response, a question that had not been clearly resolved through the original investigation.

Where was that discovery taken?

X. What the doctor knew and the patient forgot

The April 2018 consultation deserves a position of exceptional importance in any examination of this case.

It belongs before the complaints.

Before the retrospective arguments about the wording of the record.

Before the institutional review.

Before the genetic confirmation.

Before the patient began to understand himself as someone with a case against the hospital.

The relevant knowledge was already in the neurologist's possession.

Moloney had achieved a level of physical wellness he had not experienced for years.

He could describe the relationship between taking a neurological medication and losing the benefit associated with it.

The neurologist had documented the return of the outside-left-foot problem.

The clinical significance of this belongs to 2018.

It is not an argument manufactured by the patient in 2025.

And it places a serious question over the subsequent course of care.

Monaghan wrote during the same period that Moloney could be much better if levodopa or another dopamine agonist were introduced, but that there were reasons for not doing so for some time.

The prospect of greater improvement was expressly recognised.

The patient's condition was not presenting itself as a fixed collection of physical defects upon which neurological medicine could make little impression.

He had already demonstrated considerable responsiveness.

It is difficult to overstate the importance of that fact without resorting to the sort of medical claim the evidence cannot establish.

The relevant standard is not that a progressive neurological condition must never deteriorate.

It is that the knowledge acquired through successful treatment should survive the deterioration that follows.

Once a clinician has documented a patient's capacity to improve, that improvement becomes part of the clinical history against which later difficulties can be assessed.

What had changed?

Why had the benefit been lost?

Could it be restored?

Was the original explanation of the symptoms adequate in light of the response?

Were the same phenomena returning in a different form?

Would an alternative treatment preserve the gains while avoiding the intolerable effects of the original medication?

These were not questions the patient was responsible for remembering.

He was living with the condition.

The neurologist was managing it.

A medical record is supposed to bridge that difference.

The patient could forget the argument of July 2017.

The significance of the clinical evidence acquired since then should not have been lost with it.

Moloney's trust in the treatment was entirely compatible with his failure to recognise what the treatment had demonstrated.

He was concerned with his wellness.

The clinician was responsible for understanding his illness.

In 2018, their interests appeared to be aligned.

The extraordinary recovery that followed the admission ought to occupy a central position in any subsequent investigation of how that alignment deteriorated.

Instead, the eventual defence would devote much of its attention to the difficulty of treating a young person with Parkinson's disease.

That difficulty was real.

But the patient's capacity to become considerably better was also real.

The historical record contained both.

The question is whether the second received the attention it deserved when the first became increasingly difficult to manage.

XI. The feet that refused to disappear

The years following the admission produced a physical record alongside the neurological one.

Podiatry continued to encounter Moloney's feet.

There were corns. There were pressure areas. There were toe supports, insoles and repeated treatment. There was a recurrent problem involving the outside of the left foot.

The toes continued to claw.

This was the patient whom Brennan had referred in 2017, still presenting with physical abnormalities of the kind that had prompted neurological concern.

The hospital's involvement in podiatric treatment is an important fact.

But the existence of podiatric treatment cannot be permitted to stand in for the missing neurological explanation.

A podiatrist can treat the consequences of pressure upon a foot.

That does not necessarily establish why the pressure is being generated.

Removing a corn does not explain the forces that repeatedly produce it.

Supporting a clawed toe does not establish what is making the toe claw.

The two kinds of work can be complementary.

They can also proceed indefinitely without the relationship between them ever being satisfactorily investigated.

In March 2019, Brennan again described the foot difficulties in connection with increased tone and abnormal gait relating to Parkinson's disease.

The GP was still making the connection.

This was nearly two years after the original referral.

The neurological service possessed an observation of dystonic-looking gait and a subsequent medication-linked foot phenomenon.

Podiatry possessed a continuing record of physical pathology.

The GP was again relating the feet to the neurological movement disorder.

The material had accumulated.

What had not become similarly conspicuous was a completed explanation bringing the observations together.

One can imagine how a patient might reasonably assume that the various services involved in his treatment possessed a common understanding of his condition.

He saw a neurologist because he had Parkinsonism.

He saw a podiatrist because his feet were painful.

He saw his GP when he required medical assistance.

Each professional was engaged in work relevant to his welfare.

Why would he assume that he needed to become the mechanism connecting them?

Yet the responsibility for that connection is precisely what requires examination.

Who was considering the neurological implications of the podiatric findings?

Who was considering the physical implications of the abnormal movement?

Who was bringing the medication response into the interpretation of the recurring foot lesions?

The existence of several professionals treating one patient is not, by itself, evidence that somebody is treating the relationship between the problems.

The feet remained visible.

The neurological disorder remained visible.

The patient's response to neurological treatment remained documented.

The relationship between those things continued to demand a more satisfactory account.

XII. The years the patient was not watching

In September 2021, Sinemet was introduced.

By then, Moloney had been receiving neurological treatment for approximately four years. The treatment regime had evolved. Some medications had been difficult to tolerate. Others had produced benefit.

There is an understandable temptation, when reading the history retrospectively, to imagine the patient observing each development with the knowledge that he now possesses.

That would be a serious distortion.

XVI. The disappearing adviser

XVIII. The authority to decide that nothing went wrong

One of the most formidable advantages possessed by a hospital in a dispute with a patient is that it has already written much of the history.

The patient experiences his condition.

The hospital describes it.

The patient communicates his symptoms.

The hospital decides which symptoms enter the record and in what terms.

The patient may express an understanding, uncertainty or objection.

The clinician decides how that communication is represented.

The record then travels through the healthcare system, acquiring authority from its professional authorship and its place in the history of treatment.

Years later, when the patient challenges the treatment, the record is available to explain why the treatment was appropriate.

The patient may discover that he is challenging not simply a series of clinical decisions, but an established description of himself.

Moloney's case brings this difficulty into unusually sharp focus.

There is a man in the handwritten interview of July 2017 who describes the maturation of a longstanding condition.

It did not visibly retain the representation of the patient who was challenging the adequacy of the investigation.

The difference is profound.

One version of the patient is compatible with the disposition.

The other calls the disposition into question.

What happens when subsequent medicine is practised upon the authority of the first version?

What happens when the institution later reviews the treatment and relies upon a history in which the second version is absent?

The patient finds himself attempting to establish the presence of a man who should have been represented in his own record.

That he asked.

That he objected.

Yet the patient is alleging precisely that his contemporaneous opposition did not enter the authoritative account.

To dismiss that opposition because it does not appear in the account would be to allow the contested document to settle the dispute about its own construction.

That is why independent examination matters.

An examination of how that medical history came into existence.

XXI. The cost of being wrong

By March 2025, the question had become retrospective.
