# The Positive Finding and the Peripheral Hypothesis

## Dystonic Lower-Limb Movement, PRKN Reasoning, the Procyclidine Dechallenge, and the GP's Unanswered Foot Referral (2017–2019)

**Documentary companion to:** [*The July 2017 Clinical Record — A Source-by-Source Reconstruction of the Patient's Account*](https://github.com/TitanicParker/the-unanswered-referral/blob/main/JULY_2017_CLINICAL_RECORD_SOURCE_RECONSTRUCTION.md)  
**Scope:** The 2017 GP referral, hospital neurological assessment and discharge; September 2017 Professor Healy opinion; September–October 2017 procyclidine interruption as reported in April 2018; 2017–2019 podiatry record; April 2018 neurological review; later detailed PRKN/PARK9 differential; March 2019 GP re-referral.  
**Method:** Keep positive examination findings, patient reports, treatment observations, diagnostic hypotheses and later clinical interpretations distinct. Test the recorded relationship between them, rather than treating their coexistence as an explanation.

---

## Significance for the controlling allegation — 25 November 2026

The patient's affirmative allegation is that the post-protest construction of the 2017 account subordinated clinical investigation to the preservation of an existing professional disposition, and that the resulting framework impaired subsequent reassessment, including recognition of possible treatment-related harm and safeguarding when suffering became intolerable. This is an allegation to test, not a finding of intention, causation or liability. [The investigative essay](journalistic-essay.html#gravity-of-case) and [the gravest allegation](index.html#gravity-statement) set out the full narrative.

The 2018 focal medication observation is a reopening threshold: its importance is not that it proves every lesion was dystonic, but that subsequent medication changes and pain required the original relationship to remain within the differential. The later 2021–2025 regimen and distress belong to the [treatment-related harm analysis](journalistic-essay.html#treatment-related-harm), outside this document's historical scope. The 2017–2019 evidence is preserved here without retrospectively importing later knowledge.

## 1. The proposition

The documentary question is not merely whether the treating neurologist considered peripheral neuropathy, or whether he recognised a movement disorder. He did both. The more revealing question is **how those two possibilities were weighed and represented when the same patient's presenting problem involved painful, physically abnormal feet, clawing toes, increased leg tone and disordered gait**.

The GP's original question was integrated. The neurologist's examination identified a dystonic-looking lower-limb abnormality. The admission treatment addressed muscle tension neurologically. Yet the completed Impression proposed that the corns, calluses and burning might have been completely separate or might represent a secondary peripheral neuropathy, while attributing to the patient a marked mental separation between leg tension and painful foot problems. In a later detailed discussion of genetic Parkinsonism, dystonia was not included in the explicit list of reasons for suspecting PRKN/PARK2, whereas PARK9 was entertained in connection with “the neuropathy question.”

This is **an asymmetry within one clinical account**, not two unrelated complaints. The positive motor finding is not brought to bear explicitly on the disputed foot relationship; the unestablished peripheral possibility is nevertheless preserved as a diagnostic consideration. The language attributed to the patient helps sustain the boundary between them.

Subsequent evidence makes that asymmetry more consequential. Professor Healy independently suspected PARK II in September 2017 and recorded a dramatic improvement in motor symptoms on procyclidine and rasagiline. When procyclidine was interrupted for two weeks in September–October 2017, the patient reported rapid recurrence of trouble at the original outside-left-foot corn site, accompanied by increased tension. The treating neurologist recorded this in April 2018, alongside continued PRKN suspicion. Podiatry documented recurrent physical pathology at the left fifth metatarsophalangeal region and clawed toes. In March 2019, the GP was still explicitly associating painful, clawing feet with abnormal tone and Parkinsonian gait.

The cumulative documentary challenge is therefore: **where was the initially referred foot–posture–tone relationship assessed, reconciled or expressly assigned for further neurological investigation after these convergent observations?**

## 2. The GP's referral: a combined question, not simply a request for a label

The contemporaneous GP record is the proper starting point because it precedes the hospital's interpretive framework.

- **10 April 2017:** Bilateral foot pain, abnormal gait, mallet-toe deformity, abnormal nails, calluses and pressure areas were observed and recorded.
- **8 June 2017:** The GP considered a neurological opinion concerning the patient's **“gait, feet and ‘muscle tension’.”** The conjunction is explicit: three aspects of one developing presentation.
- **12 June 2017:** An occupational-health physician also expressed concern about the gait.
- **23 June 2017:** The GP recorded substantially increased tone in both lower limbs and brisk reflexes.
- **29 June 2017:** The urgent hospital referral documented a stiff/festinant gait, movement-initiation difficulty, increased tone and stiffness, declining dexterity and work function, inability to relax in bed, and toe clawing with discomfort. Its differential was Parkinsonian **or other** neurological disease.

The GP was not asserting that the pressure lesions were already proved to be caused by dystonia. He had identified a combination of findings which called for neurological interpretation. The question was whether the abnormal feet and the abnormal movement could be understood together. That question does not cease to exist when Parkinsonism is diagnosed.

The GP also continued pursuing foot care during the admission: the record contains a **6 July 2017 podiatry-prioritisation referral**. This demonstrates that the clinically troublesome feet had not disappeared from primary care's view while the specialist investigation was under way.

## 3. Admission: positive motor evidence and an alternative explanation

### 3.1. What was compressed

The hospital's short **“Rx/reason for referral”** captured initiation difficulty, increased tone/stiffness and wrist cogwheeling, but omitted the original referral's bilateral painful-foot history, corns/calluses, toe clawing and difficulty relaxing in bed. It also rendered the GP's uncertain **“?Some cogwheeling”** more firmly as cogwheeling. The opening hospital formulation was narrower than the clinical question the GP had sent.

That textual compression did not mean the neurologist never learned about the feet. His handwritten interview recovered the longstanding foot history, painful lesions and tension. His examination independently supplied additional neurological evidence.

### 3.2. What the neurologist positively found

The completed hospital material describes a striking abnormality of the left lower-limb movement: a gait appearance resembling foot drop, while explicitly indicating that it was **not true foot drop**, and suggesting dystonic posture. Pes planus and hammertoes were also recorded. The neurologist recognised an abnormal motor system, diagnosed young-onset Parkinsonism, and treated troublesome nocturnal leg tension with clonazepam/Rivotril. He also considered procyclidine and rasagiline as part of neurological management.

These are not merely symptoms alleged by the patient. The dystonic-looking lower-limb movement was the clinician's own recorded examination finding. The treatment plan likewise treated tension as a neurological management problem.

The original GP's toe clawing and the neurologist's observed dystonic-looking movement are not identical descriptions. They do, however, concern lower-limb positioning and function within the same presenting syndrome. A completed clinical account could have examined their relationship or explained why they were independent. The question is where it did so.

### 3.3. What the Impression did instead

The discharge Impression proposed:

> “The leg symptoms including corns and callouses and burning may have been completely separate or perhaps a secondary peripheral neuropathy perhaps in somewhat respect related to the significant original alcohol intake? Not clear as this has improved.”

It continued:

> “However he has in his own mind - markedly divorced the feelings of tension in the legs which was/is the predominant problem - from the ‘corns and callouses’ issues and pains in the feet”

The peripheral-neuropathy proposal is expressly uncertain. The subsequent account of the patient's own understanding is rhetorically more definite. That juxtaposition matters because the handwritten interview had recorded that the patient **“Began to be aware that foot pain & leg tension weren't the same”**, with the separately placed parenthetical **“(as the leg pain settled)”**. The typed account had already strengthened the causal account of that awareness. The Impression then represented the patient as having **“markedly divorced”** the experiences.

A patient's ability to distinguish the felt quality or timing of foot pain from leg tension does not itself answer whether abnormal neurological movement contributed to toe posturing, loading and pressure lesions. The issue here is the record's passage from **experiential distinction** to an apparently supportive **separation of clinical problems**.

The same record's alcohol observations must also be read in their actual sequence: the interview recorded **“No improvement.”** following stopping alcohol, and the typed history said **“There was no improvement off alcohol.”** The Impression nevertheless used **“Not clear as this has improved”** while entertaining a possible alcohol-related peripheral component. These statements do not establish an improvement attributable to abstinence.

## 4. Professor Healy: an independent early specialist interpretation

On **12 September 2017**, Professor Daniel G. Healy wrote to the treating neurologist following specialist assessment. The legible substantive passages of his letter state:

> “Thank you for asking me to see this man who I think probably has Park II and I would suggest testing that initially. If it is negative I would go on to test a panel.”

He estimated onset in the late twenties or early thirties, and recorded:

> “He describes a dramatic improvement in his motor symptoms with Procyclidine low dose qds and Rasagiline 1mg od. He takes Clonazepam at night.”

His treatment discussion also stated:

> “patients with PARK II tend to manage quite well for long periods of time with relatively low doses of Dopamine replacement therapy. They can become quite dyskinetic if over-used.”

Professor Healy was not independently diagnosing the cause of each pressure lesion. His contribution is nonetheless material. A second specialist regarded PARK II as probable, recommended targeted genetic testing, and recorded a dramatic improvement on medication directed at the movement disorder. The contemporaneous opinion thereby strengthened the neurological framework in which the lower-limb posture and treatment response needed to be interpreted.

Nor should the advice be reduced to a generic injunction against levodopa: the passage includes **both** a discussion of low-dose dopamine replacement **and** a warning about overuse and dyskinesia. The distinction matters when assessing how this second opinion was later invoked.

## 5. Podiatry: the continuing physical and anatomical record

The foot history was not reducible to an abstract complaint of burning or an unlocalised neuropathic sensation. The community podiatry record repeatedly documented pressure pathology at the **left fifth metatarsophalangeal joint (L/5 MTPJ)**, together with toe abnormalities and measures intended to redistribute pressure.

| Date | Recorded finding or treatment |
| --- | --- |
| **10 April 2017** | Original podiatry referral date, distinct from later registration and first treatment. |
| **10 July 2017** | Podiatry registration date. |
| **14 September 2017** | First treatment: L/5 MTPJ callus; bilateral toe props for toes 2–4; calf tightness recorded and stretches advised. |
| **26 October 2017** | Corn/heloma durum with surrounding maceration at L/5 MTPJ; debridement and local offloading; template for insole. |
| **25 January 2018** | L/5 MTPJ lesion again; insole fitting, further treatment and bilateral toe props. |
| **22 March 2018** | Callus at L/5 MTPJ; **“Toes clawed”**. |
| **15 June 2018** | L/5 MTPJ callus; **“Toes clawed 1-3 on both feet.”** |
| **7 December 2018** | Continued left fifth-MTPJ callus and additional right-foot lesion; toe props and revised insoles discussed. |

This serial record matters for two reasons. First, it establishes recurring physical findings, in a reasonably consistent location, independently of the hospital Impression. Second, the repeated use of toe props, padding, debridement and insoles demonstrates ongoing management of abnormal loading and postural consequences.

Podiatric treatment can be valuable without settling the neurological source of those forces. A treated corn may recur. Toe supports can mitigate pressure while leaving the motor contribution unresolved. The relevant question is not whether podiatry was provided, but whether its findings were integrated with the movement-disorder assessment.

## 6. The anticholinergic dechallenge: a direct challenge to clinical separation

The most informative early post-discharge event was the **two-week procyclidine interruption in September–October 2017**, recommended because of blurring of vision. The treating neurologist later documented this history in his **24 April 2018 review**.

His own note says:

> “the first thing he noticed changing and changing rapidly was the return on the foot, of the outside left corn.”

He recorded that symptoms became somewhat worse and that the patient was more **“shifty” “unrelaxed”** off procyclidine. He had been off it for a full two weeks. Rasagiline/Azilect was maintained during the interruption.

The sequence has unusual documentary value:

1. **A neurological drug was deliberately stopped** because of an adverse effect.
2. **The patient reported deterioration** during the period off that treatment.
3. **The earliest reported change involved the original focal left-foot corn site.**
4. **General tension also worsened**, tying the foot-site complaint temporally to a change in neurological state.
5. **The treating neurologist recorded the association himself** in the same broader follow-up in which he continued to discuss PRKN/PARK2.

The wording does **not** demonstrate that a physical corn grew back within hours or days, or by itself prove the biomechanical mechanism. It is evidence of a reported **return of symptoms at a specific, previously documented pressure-lesion site**, during withdrawal of neurological medication. Podiatry's contemporaneous records independently establish that the site had persistent physical pathology.

That is precisely why the observation required integration. The 2017 Impression had entertained the foot problem as possibly separate or peripheral. By April 2018, the same clinician had recorded an event in which the original foot region and the neurological tension syndrome reportedly deteriorated together when motor-directed medication was withdrawn. That evidence does not automatically settle causation. It does undermine the adequacy of leaving the original division unexplored.

**Temporal precision:** The September–October 2017 interruption is reported in the 24 April 2018 note, not a newly created contemporaneous April 2018 dechallenge. The podiatry examination of 26 October 2017 overlaps the broad period but cannot be assigned a specific before/during/after relationship without the exact medication dates. Original October 2017 emails, mentioned in the repository's evidence register, offer a further route for establishing the sequence.

**Source:** [Neurology notes, 24 April 2018](https://github.com/TitanicParker/for-counsel/blob/main/docs/source-records/neurology-notes-2018.html).

## 7. The same 2018 review: treatment response, PRKN suspicion and neurological choice

The April 2018 record does not merely discuss a foot symptom in passing. It contains complaints of foot cramps and hand cramps, marked relief of tension under the existing regimen, the withdrawal-related left-foot episode, and continuing suspicion of PARK2/PRKN shared with Professor Healy.

The neurologist's Impression states:

> “Obviously he could be very much better if we would actually give him the levodopa or another dopamine agonist ---however for a very clearly set out reasons, we do not want to do that – at least for quite some time.”

It also notes visual tolerability constraints on further procyclidine increases. Later in the same material, the neurologist explains that a specific PARK2/PRKN test, panel or eventual broader sequencing might be considered, and refers to the PRKN suspicion shared with Professor Healy.

The relevant fact is not simply that levodopa was deferred. It is that the clinician knew motor-directed treatment had materially changed the patient's condition, recognised the consequences of withdrawal, and remained actively engaged with a specific movement-disorder hypothesis. Yet the left-foot pressure-site observation was not, in the examined passage, converted into an explicit explanation of the original foot–movement relationship or a plan to test it.

An assessment of the prudence of particular medications is a separate question from whether the foot-posture connection was adequately investigated.

## 8. The PRKN enumeration and PARK9: positive finding versus retained hypothesis

In the detailed subsequent genetic discussion identified in the record, the treating neurologist set out reasons for suspecting PRKN/PARK2: unusually young onset, akinetic rigidity, no dominant family history, marked symmetry and high cognitive functioning. This was detailed and deliberate diagnostic reasoning.

**The conspicuous omission is dystonia.** Although the neurologist had recorded an abnormal lower-limb gait suggestive of dystonic posture, the quoted enumeration of reasons for PRKN did not incorporate that positive sign. The original GP's toe clawing and the recurring physical foot deformities likewise did not feature in that enumeration.

By contrast, the clinician expressly entertained **PARK9**, linking it to **“the neuropathy question.”** Peripheral neuropathy was therefore retained as a live consideration within the genetic differential despite not having been established as the explanation for the original foot presentation.

This is the yin–yang structure of the case:

| Neurological motor evidence | Possible peripheral explanation |
| --- | --- |
| Toe clawing and increased tone in the GP's original referral. | Painful corns, calluses and burning capable of being described as a separate foot problem. |
| Dystonic-looking left-lower-limb movement personally observed by neurologist. | Secondary peripheral neuropathy proposed in the 2017 Impression, expressly uncertain. |
| Neurological treatment improves motor symptoms; withdrawal is followed by reported recurrence at original left-foot site and worsened tension. | The neuropathy possibility is carried forward into later genetic reasoning, including PARK9. |
| PRKN/PARK2 considered by both treating neurologist and Professor Healy. | No established peripheral-neuropathy mechanism shown to explain the recurring physical deformities and pressure lesions. |
| Dystonia absent from the later explicit PRKN enumeration. | Neuropathy expressly present as “the neuropathy question.” |

The argument is **not** that dystonia and neuropathy could not possibly coexist. It is that the record gives unequal explicit treatment to the evidential status of these possibilities, and does not show the necessary reconciliation with the presenting physical foot abnormalities. A directly observed movement abnormality is omitted from a detailed genetic rationale; a peripheral hypothesis is kept in play; and the patient's own language has been amplified in the direction of separating the problems.

The repository's evidence register assigns the detailed PARK9 discussion to the later genetic material (EV-13). Exact page numbering and punctuation for the complete enumeration should be verified against the native image before use as a formal quotation. The quoted phrase **“the neuropathy question”** is the portion specifically identified in the documentary register.

## 9. The GP receives the specialist answer—but the original question persists

The GP documented that formal neurological correspondence was awaited on **14 and 17 July 2017**. On **18 July** he recorded receipt of **“Extensive correspondence”**, noted early Parkinsonism, a positive DaTscan and further testing pending, and entered Parkinson's disease into his clinical record.

That provides an unusually clear record of the hospital diagnosis entering primary care. The GP obtained an important specialist answer. But that answer did not establish whether the original toe clawing, abnormal posture, loading and painful pressure lesions were manifestations of, consequences of, or independent from the neurological movement disorder.

The distinction matters because the GP's later conduct shows the original relationship remained alive. In a **March 2019** podiatry re-referral, he described foot issues **“in relating to increased tone and abnormal gait relating to Parkinson's disease”** and commented:

> “Parkinsons at very young age has issues with ‘clawing feet’ and pain”

The referral selected callus, rearfoot/arch pain and gait abnormalities as reasons for podiatry review. It also contained a **“No neuropathy”** tick within a low-risk podiatry classification. That checkbox is not an electrophysiological study or a definitive diagnosis. It should nevertheless be distinguished from the neurologist's retained neuropathy hypothesis: the GP's affirmative description continued to connect foot abnormalities with movement and tone.

The 2019 letter is therefore not evidence that primary care forgot the foot–motor question. Quite the opposite. The GP preserved it, while podiatry repeatedly treated its physical consequences. What is missing from the examined source chain is a specialist explanation that visibly integrates those continuing observations.

## 11. The documentary mechanism

The record presents a sequence of **selection, attribution and non-integration**.

1. **Selection at intake:** The hospital précis strips the GP's referral of the painful feet and toe clawing, leaving a predominantly parkinsonian motor description.
2. **Positive recovery:** The neurologist's own interview and examination recover foot pathology and add dystonic-looking movement; the treatment plan treats leg tension as neurologically significant.
3. **Attribution in the completed account:** The patient's reported distinction between sensations is strengthened to a marked mental divorce of problems, beside an unestablished peripheral-neuropathy possibility.
4. **Asymmetric diagnostic explanation:** Dystonia is absent from an express PRKN symptom enumeration, while possible neuropathy retains express consideration through PARK9.
5. **Contrary longitudinal signal:** Professor Healy's PARK II opinion, dramatic motor response, the procyclidine dechallenge and serial physical podiatry findings bring the neurological and focal foot strands back into contact.
6. **Unanswered return:** In 2019 the GP continues to describe the painful clawing feet in relation to Parkinsonian gait and increased tone.

The central problem is **not** that the neurologist failed to diagnose Parkinsonism; he did. Nor is it that foot lesions received no treatment; podiatry did provide treatment. The problem revealed by these documents is **the absence of a clearly traceable reconciliation between the positive neurological findings, the provisional peripheral explanation, the treatment-state evidence and the recurring anatomical foot pathology**.

The difference between these explanations was not resolved merely by attributing a distinction of sensations to the patient. It required clinical interpretation. The GP had referred precisely because the relationship was beyond the scope of an ordinary isolated foot complaint.

## 12. The question the hospital must answer

**Where, in the contemporaneous hospital or subsequent specialist record, did the neurologist explicitly evaluate whether the dystonic-looking lower-limb movement and Parkinsonian muscle tone contributed to toe clawing, abnormal loading, recurrent left-foot pressure lesions and pain—and how was that evaluation reconciled with the peripheral-neuropathy hypothesis, the reported procyclidine withdrawal response and the GP's continued neurological description of the feet?**

If the answer was that the relationship remained uncertain, the next documentary question is equally concrete: **who was made responsible for revisiting it, and what clinical information was to trigger that review?**

The request is not for retrospective certainty about every cause of pain. It is for the documented reasoning by which the specialist connected—or decided not to connect—the symptoms and signs that the GP had deliberately referred together.
